In 1943 the Secretary of the Interior created a reservation of about 1.8 million acres for the Neets'aii Gwich'in around Venetie, Alaska. In 1971 Congress passed the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act (ANCSA), which revoked that reservation and most others, extinguished aboriginal claims to Alaska land, and authorized $962.5 million and about 44 million acres for Native-owned corporations. The Venetie corporations took title to the old reservation land in fee and passed it to the tribal government.

When the tribe demanded roughly $161,000 in taxes from a contractor building a school, Alaska sued. The Supreme Court held the land was not "Indian country" as a "dependent Indian community," because it had not been set aside by the federal government for Indian use and was not under federal superintendence. ANCSA, the Court said, "far from designating Alaskan lands for Indian use," revoked existing reservations.

The ruling means most Alaska Native land owned by corporations falls outside the Indian-country framework that supports tribal taxing and regulatory power. Later cases, such as Yellen v. Chehalis (2021), continued to treat ANCSA's corporate structure as distinct from tribal governments.
