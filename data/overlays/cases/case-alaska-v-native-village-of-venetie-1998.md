In 1943 the Secretary of the Interior created a reservation of about 1.8 million acres for the Neets'aii Gwich'in around Venetie and Arctic Village, Alaska. In 1971 Congress passed the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act (ANCSA). It revoked the Venetie Reservation and all but one of the other Alaska reservations, extinguished all aboriginal claims to Alaska land, and authorized $962.5 million and about 44 million acres for Native-owned business corporations.

The Venetie and Arctic Village corporations chose a different path. In 1973 they elected to take title to the old reservation land, giving up the statute's money payments and transfers of other land. The United States conveyed fee simple title to them, and they transferred it to the Native Village of Venetie Tribal Government.

In 1986 Alaska entered a joint venture with a private contractor to build a public school in Venetie. When the contractor and the State refused the tribe's demand for about $161,000 in taxes, a dispute over the tribe's power to tax followed. The Supreme Court held the land is not "Indian country" because it is not a "dependent Indian community": it was not set aside by the federal government for Indian use, and ANCSA ended federal superintendence of it.

The ruling means that land held in fee under ANCSA, like Venetie's, is not Indian country unless it meets the Court's test for a dependent Indian community. The opinion notes that the Annette Island Reserve survived ANCSA and that allotments can still be Indian country.
