Arizona sued California over how much Colorado River water each state could use; Nevada, New Mexico, Utah and the United States joined. The United States asked the Court to recognize water rights for several Indian reservations along the river, created by statute and executive order rather than by treaty. A Special Master heard the evidence and recommended a decree.

The Court held that Congress, in the Boulder Canyon Project Act, had set up its own scheme for dividing the Lower Basin's water, with the Secretary of the Interior allocating it by contract. It also followed Winters v. United States (1908): when the government created the reservations it impliedly reserved enough water for them. The Court sustained the Master's conclusion that "enough water was reserved to irrigate all the practicably irrigable acreage on the reservations."

The "practicably irrigable acreage" measure became the standard way to quantify tribal reserved water rights in the arid West, and reserved rights carry the priority date of the reservation. Later disputes over the standard include Arizona v. Navajo Nation (2023), which concerned whether the United States had a duty to secure more water for the Navajo Nation.
