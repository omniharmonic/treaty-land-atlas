An 1868 peace treaty between the United States and the Navajo established the Navajo Reservation, which today spans some 17 million acres, almost all in the Colorado River Basin. A federal reservation implicitly reserves the water needed for it, under Winters v. United States (1908). The Navajo Nation said the federal efforts to help with its water needs did not satisfy the United States' trust duties under the 1868 treaty. It sued to compel the United States to assess its water needs, develop a plan, and possibly build pipelines, pumps or wells. Arizona, Nevada and Colorado intervened against the Tribe. The Ninth Circuit held the treaty imposed a duty to take affirmative steps.

The Supreme Court reversed, 5-4, in an opinion by Justice Kavanaugh. The treaty reserved necessary water but "did not require the United States to take affirmative steps to secure water for the Tribe." Duties must be found in a treaty, statute or regulation, and the government owes enforceable duties only to the extent it expressly accepts them, following Jicarilla (2011). Justice Gorsuch dissented.

The decision keeps the Winters right but limits claims that the trust responsibility requires the government to build water infrastructure.
