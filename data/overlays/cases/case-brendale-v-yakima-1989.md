The Treaty of 1855 with the Yakama promised the Nation its reservation for its "exclusive use and benefit." By 1989 about 80 percent of the reservation was trust land, but the rest was owned in fee by Indians and non-Indians, in a checkerboard pattern. Two non-member landowners wanted to develop land in ways the Nation's zoning ordinance barred but Yakima County's allowed. The reservation has a "closed area" and a more open area.

The justices could not agree on one opinion. Four, led by Justice White, held the Nation could not zone fee land owned by non-members: allotment and later sales meant the Nation no longer had "exclusive use" of it, and inherent sovereignty did not reach it. Justice Stevens, with Justice O'Connor, found the Nation could zone the closed area, which remained largely undeveloped, but not the open area.

The result: the county zoned in the open area, the Nation in the closed area. Brendale is a leading case on how allotment and the later sale of land inside reservations erode tribal civil authority over non-members, building on Montana v. United States (1981).
