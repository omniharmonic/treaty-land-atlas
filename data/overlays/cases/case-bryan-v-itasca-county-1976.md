Russell Bryan, an enrolled Chippewa, lived in a mobile home on trust land in Minnesota. Itasca County assessed a personal property tax on it. Minnesota's courts upheld the tax, reading Public Law 280, the 1953 law that gave certain states jurisdiction over Indian country, as granting civil authority that included taxation.

The Supreme Court unanimously reversed. Public Law 280's "central focus" was criminal jurisdiction, and the civil grant in section 4(a) was meant mainly to give a state forum for private legal disputes involving Indians. Nothing in the Act mentioned taxing Indians or Indian property on reservations, which would be a "significant omission" if Congress meant such a sweeping change. Ambiguous statutes, the Court said, are read in favor of Indians.

Bryan limited the reach of Public Law 280: states cannot use it to regulate or tax reservation Indians. It is the basis for the civil/regulatory versus criminal/prohibitory test applied in California v. Cabazon Band (1987), which kept state gaming law off reservations.
