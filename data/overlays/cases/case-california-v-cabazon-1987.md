The Cabazon and Morongo Bands of Mission Indians ran bingo games on their California reservations under tribal ordinances, and the Cabazon Band also ran a card club. The games were open to the public and drew mainly non-Indians. California and Riverside County tried to apply their bingo and card-game laws, so the Bands sued. No treaty was at issue.

The Supreme Court held for the Bands. California's bingo statute regulated rather than prohibited gambling, so Public Law 280 did not authorize its enforcement on reservations. Federal organized-crime legislation also did not let states enforce their gambling laws there. More broadly, state law is pre-empted when it interferes with "federal and tribal interests reflected in federal law," and the state's interest in preventing organized crime was not enough to override the federal goal of tribal self-sufficiency. The games were the Bands' main source of revenue.

Cabazon opened the way for tribal gaming. Congress responded the next year with the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act (1988), a statute that later cases such as Michigan v. Bay Mills (2014) and Ysleta del Sur Pueblo v. Texas (2022) interpreted.
