Under the Atoka Agreement, ratified in 1898 with the Choctaw and Chickasaw Tribes, allotted lands were to be "non-taxable while the title remains in the original allottees," but not longer than twenty-one years from the date of patent. Indian allottees later leased their allotments for oil and gas and reserved a royalty of one-eighth of the gross production. Oklahoma imposed a three percent tax on owners of royalty interests and applied it to these Indians.

The Supreme Court held the tax unlawful. It was not a tax on oil and gas after they were severed from the land, but a tax on the right the Indians kept as lessors and owners of the fee, which the Agreement's exemption forbade. The Court said tax exemptions secured to Indians by agreement with the national government are to be liberally construed in their favor, and read in the sense the Indians would naturally have understood.

The case is an early example of the canon that agreements with tribes are read as the tribe would have understood them. It protected the value of allotments by keeping state taxes off minerals produced from them.
