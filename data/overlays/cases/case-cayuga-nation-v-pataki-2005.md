The Cayuga Indian Nation and the Seneca-Cayuga Tribe of Oklahoma sued New York for the late-eighteenth-century loss of 64,015 acres of their "Original Reservation" in central New York. The land was reserved in an 1789 treaty with New York, but the Cayugas later conveyed it in 1795 and 1807 treaties that were not ratified by the federal government, so the tribes said those transfers violated the Nonintercourse Act. The Northern District of New York agreed and awarded about $248 million in damages and prejudgment interest. Defendants appealed, and the tribes cross-appealed on interest and the denial of ejectment.

After the Supreme Court decided City of Sherrill v. Oneida Indian Nation in 2005, the Second Circuit reversed. It held that "equitable doctrines, such as laches, acquiescence, and impossibility, can, in appropriate circumstances, be applied to Indian land claims," including possessory claims like this one, and that the claim was barred by laches. Judge Hall dissented in part.

The ruling ended one of the largest land claims in the eastern United States and shaped how courts treat similar claims. The Supreme Court denied certiorari in 2006.
