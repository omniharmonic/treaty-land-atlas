Georgia had passed laws extending its authority over the Cherokee Nation's territory and annulling Cherokee laws. The Cherokee Nation asked the Supreme Court for an injunction to stop Georgia, claiming to sue as a "foreign state" under the Constitution's grant of original jurisdiction over controversies between a state and a foreign state. The Cherokees' case relied on their many treaties with the United States, including the Treaty of Hopewell of 1785.

Chief Justice Marshall's opinion held that the Cherokee Nation is not a foreign state in the Constitution's sense, so the Court lacked original jurisdiction. Tribes within the United States "may more correctly perhaps be denominated domestic dependent nations." They occupy territory to which the United States asserts title "independent of their will," and "their relation to the United States resembles that of a ward to his guardian." The Court did not decide the merits of Georgia's laws.

The case created the "domestic dependent nation" idea and the guardian-ward image later used to justify federal power over tribes in Kagama and Lone Wolf. A year later Worcester v. Georgia held Georgia's laws had no force in Cherokee territory.
