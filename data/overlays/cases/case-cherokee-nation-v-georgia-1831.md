Georgia had passed a law to extend its laws over the territory occupied by the Cherokees and to annul all laws and ordinances made by the Cherokee Nation. The Cherokee Nation asked the Supreme Court for an injunction to stop Georgia from carrying out these acts. It claimed it could sue in the Court as a "foreign state," under the Constitution's grant of jurisdiction over controversies between a state and a foreign state. The reported arguments discuss the Cherokees' treaties with the United States, including the Treaty of Hopewell of 1785, which they say allowed the Cherokees "to send a deputy of their choice" to Congress.

Chief Justice Marshall's opinion held that the Cherokee Nation is not a foreign state in the sense the Constitution uses the term, so the Court could not hear the case. Tribes, he wrote, "may more correctly perhaps be denominated domestic dependent nations." They occupy a territory to which the United States asserts a title "independent of their will," and "their relation to the United States resembles that of a ward to his guardian." The opinion did not take up the merits of the controversy between Georgia and the Cherokees.

Later opinions kept the language. In United States v. Kagama (1886), the Court called Marshall's opinions in this case and in Worcester v. Georgia "perhaps the best statement" of the tribes' position.
