Article 10 of the 1866 treaty between the United States and the Cherokee Nation said every Cherokee Indian and freed person in the Cherokee nation could sell products of the farm or merchandise and ship them to market "without restraint," paying only any tax the United States levied on goods sold outside the Indian territory.

Two years later, Congress passed the Internal Revenue Act of July 20, 1868. Its 107th section said the internal revenue laws taxing distilled spirits, fermented liquors, tobacco, snuff and cigars "shall be construed to extend to such articles produced anywhere within the exterior boundaries of the United States." The United States moved to condemn and forfeit tobacco and other property in the Cherokee Nation for violating the revenue laws. Elias C. Boudinot and his partner, who claimed the property, said the treaty exempted it from taxation.

The Supreme Court, in an opinion by Justice Swayne, held that the 1868 act applied in the Indian Territory in the Western District of Arkansas, occupied by the Cherokee Nation, notwithstanding the earlier treaty. The reporter's summary of the case states the rule plainly: "An act of Congress may supersede a prior treaty."

The Court later relied on this rule. In Lone Wolf v. Hitchcock (1903), it cited The Cherokee Tobacco and wrote that "the power exists to abrogate the provisions of an Indian treaty."
