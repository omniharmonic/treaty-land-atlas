The Treaty of 1866 between the United States and the Cherokee Nation said Cherokee citizens could sell the products of their farms and ship them to market "without restraint," paying only taxes the United States might levy on goods sold outside Indian territory. In 1868 Congress passed an Internal Revenue Act extending federal taxes on liquor, tobacco and cigars to articles produced anywhere "within the exterior boundaries of the United States." The United States sought forfeiture of tobacco claimed by Elias C. Boudinot and others in the Cherokee Nation for violating that law.

The Supreme Court, in an opinion by Justice Swayne, held the 1868 act applied in the Indian Territory notwithstanding the earlier treaty. Its summary states the rule plainly: "An act of Congress may supersede a prior treaty."

The decision is the basis for the "later-in-time" rule used in Indian law: Congress may abrogate treaty terms unilaterally, with no consent from the tribe. Later cases on treaty rights, from Lone Wolf (1903) to Menominee Tribe (1968), start from this principle and ask whether Congress clearly intended the abrogation.
