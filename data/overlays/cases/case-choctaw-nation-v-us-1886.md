In the Treaty of Dancing Rabbit Creek, signed 27 September 1830, the Choctaw Nation ceded its lands in Mississippi. The Choctaws later said the United States had not carried out the treaty and had not paid them the proceeds of selling the ceded land. In the Treaty of 22 June 1855, the United States agreed to submit the questions to the Senate. In 1881 Congress authorized the Court of Claims to hear all questions of difference arising out of treaty stipulations with the Choctaw Nation, reviewing them de novo.

The Supreme Court reversed the Court of Claims and entered a larger award. It opened by stating that dealings with tribes, as those of a "superior towards an inferior who is under its care and control," must be interpreted as "justice and reason demand." The Senate's award was "the nearest approximation to the justice and right of the case." The final judgment was $2,981,247.30, less $250,000 already paid, plus unpaid annuities of $59,445.32 and $68,102 for land taken in fixing the Arkansas boundary. Chief Justice Waite dissented.

The case shows how treaty-cession claims moved through the Court of Claims and set a pattern for later compensation suits.
