The Oneida Nation's homeland once covered some six million acres of central New York. In 1788 the Oneidas ceded their lands to the State but kept a reservation of about 300,000 acres; the 1794 Treaty of Canandaigua acknowledged that reservation. New York kept buying reservation land, and by 1920 the Oneidas in the state retained only 32 acres. In 1997 and 1998 the Oneida Indian Nation bought parcels in Sherrill, New York, once inside the reservation, and refused to pay city property taxes.

The Supreme Court held the Nation could not unilaterally revive its ancient sovereignty over the parcels. Citing the area's long non-Indian character, two centuries of state and local governance, and the Oneidas' long delay in suing, it ruled that "The Oneidas long ago relinquished governmental reins and cannot regain them through open-market purchases from current titleholders."

Sherrill applied equitable doctrines such as laches and acquiescence to bar a sovereignty claim even though earlier cases (Oneida I and II) had recognized the underlying land claims. It shifted the route for regaining jurisdiction toward the Interior Department's land-into-trust process.
