In 1996, Elouise Cobell and other holders of Individual Indian Money (IIM) trust accounts sued the Secretary of the Interior and others, alleging breach of the government's fiduciary duties as trustee. The bulk of the trust assets, the court noted, were proceeds from transactions in land allotted to individual Indians under the General Allotment Act of 1887 (the Dawes Act). They relied on the American Indian Trust Fund Management Reform Act of 1994, which requires an accounting of the funds. After years of litigation, the district court held the Department in continuing breach and that a full accounting was impossible, and it ordered monetary relief to the class.

The D.C. Circuit held that "while the district court's analysis of duty and breach are generally correct, the court erred in freeing the Department of the Interior from its burden to make an accounting." It vacated the district court's orders and remanded.

The Cobell litigation, one of the largest class actions against the federal government, ended in a settlement approved by Congress in 2010. The case shows the long legacy of allotment on Indian trust assets.
