The Lake Traverse Reservation in South Dakota was created by an 1867 treaty with the Sisseton and Wahpeton bands of Sioux. In 1889 the tribe and the United States negotiated an agreement, ratified by an 1891 Act, which opened all unallotted lands to settlement. The tribe received a sum per acre in payment for ceding "all" its "claim, right, title, and interest" in the unallotted lands. Two consolidated cases asked whether South Dakota courts could exercise jurisdiction over tribal members there.

The Court, 6-3, held the 1891 Act terminated the reservation and returned the unallotted lands to the public domain. It found that the face of the Act, its circumstances and its legislative history "all point unmistakably" to this conclusion. State courts therefore had civil and criminal jurisdiction over tribal members on the non-Indian, unallotted lands. The Court distinguished Mattz v. Arnett and Seymour v. Superintendent.

Decoteau is a key case on reservation diminishment. Its focus on a clear cession for a sum certain was later refined in Solem v. Bartlett (1984).
