John Elk, an Indian born into a tribe, said he had left his tribe and lived in Omaha, Nebraska, subject to the jurisdiction of the United States. Charles Wilkins, a city registrar, refused to register him to vote in the April 1880 city election. Elk sued, claiming citizenship by birth under the Fourteenth Amendment. No treaty or land was at issue.

The Supreme Court ruled against Elk. It held that an Indian born a member of a tribe that still exists, who has voluntarily separated from it and lived among white citizens, "but who has not been naturalized, or taxed, or recognized as a citizen, either by the United States or by the State," is not a citizen under the Amendment's first section. In the Court's view, birth into a tribe was not birth "subject to the jurisdiction" of the United States in the amendment's sense.

The decision left citizenship for Native people to be conferred case by case through treaties and statutes until Congress acted. The Indian Citizenship Act of 1924 later extended citizenship by statute.
