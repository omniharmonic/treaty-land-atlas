John Elk, an Indian, said he had severed his tribal relations and fully surrendered himself to the jurisdiction of the United States. He lived in Omaha, Nebraska. On or about April 5, 1880, he went to Charles Wilkins, the registrar for one ward of the city, to be registered to vote in the city election held on April 6. Wilkins refused. Elk sued in federal court. He relied on the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments.

The Supreme Court ruled against him. It held that an Indian born a member of one of the Indian tribes within the United States, who had voluntarily separated himself from his tribe and taken up residence among the white citizens of a State, "but who has not been naturalized, or taxed, or recognized as a citizen, either by the United States or by the State," is not a citizen under the first section of the Fourteenth Amendment.

The Court read "subject to the jurisdiction" to mean "completely subject to their political jurisdiction, and owing them direct and immediate allegiance." Indians born as members of, and owing immediate allegiance to, one of the Indian tribes, it said, are "no more 'born in the United States and subject to the jurisdiction thereof'" than the children born in the United States of ambassadors or other public ministers.

Congress changed the rule by statute. An Act of June 2, 1924 declared all non-citizen Indians born within the territorial limits of the United States to be citizens, without impairing any Indian's right to tribal or other property.
