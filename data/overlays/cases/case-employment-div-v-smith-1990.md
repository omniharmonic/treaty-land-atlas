Alfred Smith and Galen Black were fired from a private drug-rehabilitation job for using peyote at a ceremony of their Native American Church. Oregon denied them unemployment benefits because they were discharged for work-related "misconduct," and its drug law made possessing peyote a felony. The case concerns religious liberty, not land or a treaty.

The Supreme Court held that the Free Exercise Clause permits a state to prohibit sacramental peyote use and thus to deny benefits. The Court reasoned that an individual need not be excused from a neutral law of general applicability just because it incidentally burdens religious practice. The Court declined to apply the balancing test from Sherbert v. Verner to an across-the-board criminal ban.

Congress and several states responded: Congress passed the Religious Freedom Restoration Act of 1993 and amended the American Indian Religious Freedom Act in 1994 to protect sacramental peyote use by Native American Church members. For Native religious claims involving government land use, the case is read together with Lyng v. Northwest Indian Cemetery (1988), which our data also covers.
