Crow Dog, a Brule Lakota, was convicted in the territorial court in Dakota and sentenced to death for killing Spotted Tail, also of the Brule Sioux band, in Indian country. He petitioned for habeas corpus. The question was whether the 1868 Fort Laramie Treaty with the Sioux and the agreement ratified by the 1877 Act gave United States courts power over such a crime.

The Supreme Court freed him. Revised Statutes section 2146 excepted "crimes committed by one Indian against the person or property of another Indian" from federal criminal jurisdiction in Indian country. Neither the treaty's "bad men" clause, nor the 1877 provision that the Indians "shall be subject to the laws of the United States," repealed that exception. The Court said repeals by implication are disfavored, especially in a case involving human life.

Congress answered with the Major Crimes Act of 1885, which put certain serious crimes under federal jurisdiction. The Supreme Court upheld that Act in United States v. Kagama (1886). Crow Dog is also a foundation for the principle that tribal governments keep authority over internal matters unless Congress clearly removes it.
