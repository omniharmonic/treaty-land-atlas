In 1795 the Georgia legislature sold western lands, between the Mississippi and Yazoo rivers, to land companies. A later legislature rescinded the sale after members of the first were charged with bribery. Peck had sold part of the land to Fletcher, and Fletcher sued Peck on the covenants in the deed, which said Georgia was "legally seised in fee of the soil thereof, subject only to the extinguishment of part of the Indian title thereon."

Chief Justice Marshall held the repeal could not undo the grant, because a grant is a contract a state cannot impair. The Court said even the grossest corruption would not authorize a court to disregard the law. On the Indian title question, "the majority of the court is of opinion that the nature of the Indian title, which is certainly to be respected by all courts, until it be legitimately extinguished, is not such as to be absolutely repugnant to seisin in fee on the part of the state."

This sentence is the first Supreme Court statement that states hold ultimate title subject to Native occupancy, which Johnson v. McIntosh (1823) later developed as the doctrine of discovery.
