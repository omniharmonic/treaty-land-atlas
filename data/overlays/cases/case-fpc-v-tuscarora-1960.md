The Tuscarora Indian Nation owned land in fee simple, bought with its own funds, near the Niagara River in New York. Congress directed the Federal Power Commission to license the Power Authority of the State of New York to build a hydroelectric project. The project's storage reservoir would flood part of those Tuscarora lands. The Nation objected that the land fell within a federal "reservation," that a treaty protected it, and that a law forbidding transfers of Indian land without a treaty barred the taking.

The Supreme Court disagreed on each point. Because the land was owned in fee and the United States held no interest in it, it was not a "reservation" under the Federal Power Act. The Act's broad terms let the Commission's licensees take lands "owned by Indians, as well as those of all other citizens," on payment of just compensation. The lands were not subject to any treaty with the United States, and 25 U.S.C. 177 does not bind the United States itself.

The case is a leading example of Indian-owned fee land being subject to general eminent-domain statutes. Later cases applied its reasoning on laws of general applicability.
