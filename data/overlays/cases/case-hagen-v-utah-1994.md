Hagen, an Indian, was charged in Utah state court with distributing a controlled substance in Myton, a town inside the original boundaries of the Uintah Indian Reservation. That land was opened to non-Indian settlement in 1905. Hagen argued that the crime occurred in "Indian country," so only federal courts had jurisdiction. The Tenth Circuit had earlier reached a similar conclusion in Ute Indian Tribe v. Utah, but the Utah Supreme Court ruled that Congress had diminished the reservation.

The Supreme Court agreed with Utah. Applying Solem v. Bartlett, it looked at the statutory language, the contemporaneous understanding of the Act, and who moved onto the opened lands. It found that the Act of May 27, 1902, which provided that "all the unallotted lands within said reservation shall be restored to the public domain," showed an intent to terminate the reservation status of those lands. Myton was therefore not in Indian country.

Hagen is one of the cases treating surplus-land acts as diminishing reservations. It reinforced Solem's three-part inquiry that McGirt v. Oklahoma (2020) later discussed.
