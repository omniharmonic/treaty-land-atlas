An 1868 treaty with the Crow Tribe promised its members "the right to hunt on the unoccupied lands of the United States so long as game may be found thereon" in exchange for most of the Tribe's territory. In 2014 Wyoming charged Clayvin Herrera with off-season elk hunting in Bighorn National Forest. Wyoming's courts relied on a Tenth Circuit decision, Crow Tribe v. Repsis, which in turn relied on Ward v. Race Horse (1896), and held the treaty right expired at statehood. They also held that the national forest was categorically occupied.

The Supreme Court, 5-4, in an opinion by Justice Sotomayor, ruled for Herrera on both points. The case was "controlled by Mille Lacs, not Race Horse." The Crow treaty right "did not expire upon Wyoming's statehood." The Court also held Bighorn National Forest did not become categorically "occupied" when it was created, since the Tribe would have understood "unoccupied" to mean not settled by non-Indians. The case was remanded on whether hunting in the forest was otherwise lawful.

Herrera repudiated Race Horse's reasoning and confirmed that treaty rights survive statehood absent clear congressional abrogation.
