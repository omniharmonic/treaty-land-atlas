Allotment in the late 19th century split reservation land into individual parcels, and through generations of inheritance many parcels became divided into tiny undivided shares. To reduce this fractionation, Congress enacted section 207 of the Indian Land Consolidation Act of 1983. It said small interests, representing 2 percent or less of a tract and earning under $100 the previous year, could not pass by inheritance or will but would escheat to the tribe. It provided no compensation. Members of the Oglala Sioux Tribe, who were heirs or devisees of owners, sued.

The Supreme Court held the heirs had standing and that the original section 207 "effected a 'taking'" of their decedents' property without just compensation, in violation of the Fifth Amendment. It treated the right to pass property to heirs as a property right.

The decision forced Congress to rewrite the rule. A revised version was later also held to be an unconstitutional taking in Babbitt v. Youpee (1997). The problem of fractionated allotments continues to complicate land use and trust administration.
