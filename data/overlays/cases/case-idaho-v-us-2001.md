The Coeur d'Alene Tribe lived in and about what is now Idaho and traditionally used Lake Coeur d'Alene and the St. Joe River for food, fiber, transportation, recreation and cultural activities. In 1873 the Tribe agreed to give up, for compensation, all claims to its aboriginal lands outside a specified reservation that included part of the river and virtually all of the lake. The agreement required congressional approval. President Grant set the land aside in an 1873 Executive Order anyway, with the reservation's northern boundary running across the lake.

Congress neither ratified the agreement nor compensated the Tribe. The Tribe petitioned for a proper treaty, and Congress authorized negotiations. In 1887 the Tribe agreed to cede its rights to all land except that within the Executive Order reservation, and the Government promised to compensate the Tribe and hold the land forever as Indian land. A new agreement in 1889 had the Tribe cede the reservation's northern portion, including two-thirds of the lake, for compensation. While the ratifying bill was pending in the House, Congress admitted Idaho to the Union in 1890. In 1891 Congress ratified the 1887 and 1889 agreements.

The United States sued Idaho to quiet title to the submerged lands, in trust for the Tribe. The Supreme Court, 5-4, held that the National Government holds title in trust for the Tribe to the lands under the portions of the lake and river within the reservation. Idaho conceded that the Executive Branch had interpreted the 1873 reservation to include submerged lands, and the Court found that Congress recognized the full extent of that reservation and intended to bar passage of title to Idaho.
