Idaho and the United States disputed who owned the submerged lands under part of Lake Coeur d'Alene and the St. Joe River. In 1873 the Coeur d'Alene Tribe agreed to give up its aboriginal claims outside a specified reservation. Congress did not ratify that agreement, but President Grant set aside a reservation by Executive Order in 1873. Further agreements followed in 1887 and 1889, in which the Tribe ceded its northern portion, including two-thirds of the lake, for compensation. Congress admitted Idaho as a state in 1890 and ratified the agreements in 1891.

The Supreme Court held that the National Government holds title to the submerged lands, in trust for the Tribe. It applied a two-step test: did Congress intend to include submerged lands in the reservation, and did it intend to defeat the future state's title? Idaho conceded the Executive Order reservation included the submerged lands, and Congress had recognized it.

The case shows that tribal land rights can include lake and river beds despite the usual presumption favoring state title to navigable waters, when Congress clearly recognizes a reservation.
