Johnson and Graham's lessee claimed land in Illinois under purchases made in 1773 and 1775 from the Illinois and Piankeshaw Indians. William M'Intosh held the same land under a grant from the United States. The dispute was framed as a question of whose title a United States court would recognize, not as a treaty case. The case reached the Supreme Court on a case stated by the parties.

Chief Justice Marshall held that private purchases from Indian nations "northwest of the river Ohio" could not be recognized in the courts of the United States. His reasoning was that the United States, like European powers before it, maintains that "discovery gave an exclusive right to extinguish the Indian title of occupancy, either by purchase or by conquest." Tribes kept a right of occupancy, but only the government could acquire the underlying title.

The decision is the starting point for the "doctrine of discovery" in American property law. It underlies later cases such as Tee-Hit-Ton v. United States (1955) on the limited protection of aboriginal title. Many Native nations and scholars criticize its reasoning.
