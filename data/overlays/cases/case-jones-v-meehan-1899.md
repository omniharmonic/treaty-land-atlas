Under Article 9 of the Treaty of October 2, 1863 (the Old Crossing treaty) with the Red Lake and Pembina bands of Chippewa, the bands ceded a large tract to the United States. The treaty set apart "a reservation of six hundred and forty acres near the mouth of the Thief River for the chief Moose Dung." After his death in 1872, rival claimants traced title through him, and the case turned on whether he held a fee title and who inherited it.

The Supreme Court held that the treaty itself converted the reserved sections into individual property, "equivalent to a present grant of a complete title in fee simple," without any act of Congress or patent. A treaty with Indians must be read "in the sense in which they would naturally be understood by the Indians." Inheritance was governed by the tribe's laws and customs, not state law or the Secretary of the Interior.

Jones v. Meehan is an important statement of the canon that ambiguous treaty terms are read as the tribe would have understood them. It protected the title of individual treaty reservees against later executive interference.
