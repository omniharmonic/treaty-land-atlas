After Ex parte Crow Dog (1883), Congress passed the Major Crimes Act, part of the Indian Appropriation Act of March 3, 1885. It gave United States courts jurisdiction over seven named crimes, including murder, committed by Indians on reservations. Kagama and another Indian were indicted in California for a murder committed on the Hoopa Valley reservation, the victim also being an Indian of the reservation. The question reached the Supreme Court on a certificate of division.

The Court, in an opinion by Justice Miller, upheld the Act as constitutional. It said the government "has recognized in the Indian tribes heretofore a state of semi-independence and pupilage" and has "the right and authority, instead of controlling them by treaties, to govern them by acts of Congress." States, however, had no such power over tribes as long as they maintained tribal relations.

Kagama is a foundation of Congress's claimed "plenary power" over tribes, which the Court grounded in the tribes' location within the United States rather than in the Constitution's text. Later cases, including Lone Wolf v. Hitchcock (1903), relied on it to uphold unilateral congressional acts, and it remains cited in modern debates about the scope of that power.
