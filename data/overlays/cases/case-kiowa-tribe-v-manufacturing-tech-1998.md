The Kiowa Tribe of Oklahoma owns land in Oklahoma, and the United States holds land in trust for it there. After the Tribe's then-chairman signed a promissory note in the Tribe's name, the Tribe agreed to pay Manufacturing Technologies $285,000 plus interest for stock issued by a third party. The note recites that it was signed at Carnegie, Oklahoma, where the Tribe has a complex on trust land. The company says it was executed and delivered in Oklahoma City, beyond tribal lands. The note says nothing in it "subjects or limits the sovereign rights of the Kiowa Tribe of Oklahoma."

When the Tribe defaulted, the company sued in state court. The trial court rejected the Tribe's claim of sovereign immunity and entered judgment for the company, and the Oklahoma Court of Civil Appeals affirmed, holding that tribes can be sued in state court for breach of contract involving off-reservation commercial conduct.

The Supreme Court reversed, 6-3, in an opinion by Justice Kennedy. It held that tribes enjoy sovereign immunity from civil suits on contracts, whether governmental or commercial, on or off a reservation. Congress had not authorized suit, and the Tribe had not waived immunity. The Court wrote that there are "reasons to doubt the wisdom of perpetuating the doctrine," but said it would "defer to the role Congress may wish to exercise in this important judgment." Justice Stevens dissented, joined by Justices Thomas and Ginsburg.

In Michigan v. Bay Mills (2014), the Court said it would "decline to revisit our prior decisions holding that, absent such an abrogation (or a waiver), Indian tribes have immunity even when a suit arises from off-reservation commercial activity."
