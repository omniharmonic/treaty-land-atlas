The Medicine Lodge Treaty of 1867 with the Kiowa and Comanche set apart a reservation. Article 12 said that no treaty ceding any part of it would be valid against the Indians unless signed by at least three fourths of all adult male Indians occupying it. Congress later passed the Act of June 6, 1900 to allot the land and dispose of the surplus. Lone Wolf, a Kiowa leader, sued to stop it, saying the required consent had not been obtained and that amendments Congress added had not been put to the Indians.

The Supreme Court held that Congress had "plenary authority" over tribal relations, a power "not subject to be controlled by the courts." Article 12 could not strip Congress of all power to act if three fourths of the men did not agree. The Court said it would presume Congress acted in good faith and would not inquire into motives.

Lone Wolf is among the best-known cases for unilateral congressional abrogation of treaty terms, though later courts have required clearer statements. Menominee Tribe v. United States (1968) shows limits on implied abrogation.
