As part of a project to create a paved 75-mile road linking two California towns, the Forest Service upgraded part of the road on federal land and planned to finish a stretch through a portion of a national forest in northwestern California. That land had traditionally been used for religious purposes by Yurok, Karok and Tolowa Indians.

A study commissioned by the Forest Service found the area had historically been used for religious rituals that depend upon privacy, silence and an undisturbed natural setting. The study recommended against completing the road, saying it would irreparably damage sacred areas. In 1982 the Service decided not to adopt that recommendation and prepared a final environmental impact statement for the road. An Indian organization, individual Indians, nature organizations and the State of California sued. The district court enjoined the road and a timber-harvesting plan, and the Court of Appeals affirmed in pertinent part.

The Supreme Court reversed, 5-3, in an opinion by Justice O'Connor; Justice Kennedy took no part. It held that "The Free Exercise Clause does not prohibit the Government from permitting timber harvesting" or building the road. The affected individuals would not be coerced by the government into violating their beliefs, nor penalized for religious activity. "Even assuming that the Government's actions here will virtually destroy the Indians' ability to practice their religion," the Court said, the Constitution gave no principle to uphold the claim. Justice Brennan dissented, joined by Justices Marshall and Blackmun.

In 2024 the Ninth Circuit's en banc opinion in Apache Stronghold called Lyng "the Supreme Court's controlling decision" for a free exercise claim over a federal land transfer.
