Plaintiff, calling itself the Mashpee Tribe, claimed to be a tribe of Indians living continuously in and around the town of Mashpee, Massachusetts, since time immemorial. It sued under the Indian Nonintercourse Act, 25 U.S.C. 177, saying its tribal land was taken between 1834 and 1870 without the required federal consent. The 1976 suit was filed against a class of landowners in the town. The defendants denied that plaintiff was or ever had been a tribe, which was a threshold question for both standing and the Act's protection.

After a 40-day trial, the jury answered special questions and found no tribe existed on several key dates, including July 22, 1790, June 23, 1869, May 28, 1870 and August 26, 1976, though it found one in 1834 and 1842. The district court entered judgment for the defendants. The First Circuit affirmed, holding the trial court acted correctly in denying a continuance for the Interior Department's recognition decision, and that the jury instructions, taken as a whole, were largely consistent with the plaintiff's own position.

The Mashpee obtained federal acknowledgement from the BIA in 2007, and Carcieri v. Salazar (2009) later affected their trust-land petition.
