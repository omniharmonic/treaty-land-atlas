The plaintiff, calling itself the Mashpee Tribe, claimed to be a tribe of Indians that had lived in and around the town of Mashpee, Massachusetts, continuously since time immemorial. Its suit rested on the Indian Nonintercourse Act, which was first passed in 1790 and bars land conveyances from Indian tribes unless made by treaty or convention. The tribe said its tribal land was taken from it between 1834 and 1870 without the required federal consent. The suit, filed August 26, 1976, was against a defendant class representing landowners in the town.

The district court severed the question of whether the plaintiff was a tribe for a separate trial. The Mashpee asked for a delay until the Department of the Interior decided whether to recognize it as a tribe; the court denied that, and the trial began October 17, 1977. It lasted 40 days.

The jury answered special questions. It found that the proprietors of Mashpee and their families constituted an Indian tribe on March 31, 1834 and March 3, 1842, but not on July 22, 1790, June 23, 1869 or May 28, 1870. It also found no tribe on August 26, 1976, and no continuous tribal existence. The court dismissed the case, and the First Circuit rejected each of the plaintiff's claims of error and affirmed.

The Mashpee Wampanoag Tribe later obtained official acknowledgement from the Bureau of Indian Affairs in 2007, according to a 2016 federal court opinion.
