Mattz, a Yurok, was fishing with gill nets that a California game warden seized. He argued the nets were taken in Indian country, where state fishing laws did not apply. The Klamath River Reservation was created by Executive Order in 1855, and in 1891 it was joined to the Hoopa Valley Reservation. The Act of June 17, 1892 declared the lands "subject to settlement, entry, and purchase" under the homestead laws, with allotments for Indians living there. California courts held the reservation had ended.

The Supreme Court disagreed. It held the 1892 Act did not terminate the reservation, so the land was still Indian country under 18 U.S.C. 1151. Allotment provisions, it said, were "completely consistent" with continued reservation status, and referring to the "Klamath River Reservation" in the past tense merely identified the land that had just been added to Hoopa Valley.

Mattz is the early case holding that opening a reservation to settlement does not by itself disestablish it. It was distinguished in DeCoteau (1975) and shaped the later Solem v. Bartlett test.
