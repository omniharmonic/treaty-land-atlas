McClanahan, an enrolled member of the Navajo Nation, lived on the Arizona part of the Navajo Reservation, and all her income came from reservation sources. Arizona assessed its personal income tax on her. The state courts upheld the tax.

The Supreme Court unanimously reversed in an opinion by Justice Marshall. It held that "by imposing the tax in question on this appellant, the State has interfered with matters which the relevant treaty and statutes leave to the exclusive province of the Federal Government and the Indians themselves." The relevant treaty was the Navajo Treaty of 1868. The Court read it, with federal statutes, in light of the tribe's semi-autonomous status, rather than asking whether a state tax might be reasonable in the abstract.

McClanahan helped fix a framework in which state authority over reservation Indians is tested against federal pre-emption and tribal sovereignty. It was followed by cases such as White Mountain Apache Tribe v. Bracker (1980) and Bryan v. Itasca County (1976), which limited state taxation and regulation in Indian country.
