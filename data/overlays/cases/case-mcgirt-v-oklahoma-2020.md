Jimcy McGirt, an enrolled member of the Seminole Nation, was convicted by an Oklahoma court of serious sex offenses committed on land in the Creek Nation's historic territory. He argued that Oklahoma lacked jurisdiction because the land is Indian country, where the federal Major Crimes Act applies. The question was whether Congress ever disestablished the Creek Reservation. An 1833 treaty promised the Creek Nation a "permanent home" and a patent in fee simple; an 1856 treaty promised that no portion of its lands would be embraced in any Territory or State.

The Supreme Court, 5-4, in an opinion by Justice Gorsuch, held that "land reserved for the Creek Nation since the 19th century remains 'Indian country.'" Only Congress can disestablish a reservation, and it must clearly say so. Allotment-era laws lacked language of "present and total surrender of all tribal interests," and Congress's other intrusions on Creek self-government did not end the reservation. The opinion says "Congress has since broken more than a few promises to the Tribe."

McGirt applies Solem's framework and affected criminal jurisdiction across eastern Oklahoma. The Court limited it two years later in Oklahoma v. Castro-Huerta (2022).
