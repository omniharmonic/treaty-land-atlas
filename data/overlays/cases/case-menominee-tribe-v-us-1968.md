The Menominee Tribe was granted a reservation in Wisconsin by the Treaty of Wolf River in 1854. The treaty set aside land for the Menominees "for a home, to be held as Indian lands are held."

In 1954 Congress passed the Menominee Termination Act. It provided for the end of federal supervision over the property and members of the tribe, after which state laws would apply to them in the same way as to others. The Act did not become fully effective until 1961. The Wisconsin Supreme Court, in another proceeding, held that the Termination Act had abrogated the Menominees' hunting and fishing rights. The Tribe then sued in the Court of Claims for compensation for the loss of those rights.

The Court of Claims held that the Termination Act had not extinguished the rights, which were retained under the 1854 treaty, and the Supreme Court affirmed in an opinion by Justice Douglas. The same Congress that passed the Termination Act also enacted Public Law 280, which two months later was amended to apply to the Menominee Reservation. That law gave Wisconsin general jurisdiction over Indian country, with the proviso that nothing in it "shall deprive any Indian or Indian tribe . . . of any right, privilege, or immunity afforded under Federal treaty . . . with respect to hunting, trapping, or fishing."

Read together, the Court held that the hunting and fishing rights "survived the Termination Act of 1954," and that, as the syllabus puts it, a purpose to abrogate treaty rights is not to be lightly imputed to Congress.
