The United States set aside land for the Menominee in the Treaty of Wolf River of 1854, "for a home, to be held as Indian lands are held." Congress passed the Menominee Termination Act in 1954, ending federal supervision of the tribe and its property. The Wisconsin Supreme Court held the Act had ended the Menominees' hunting and fishing rights. The Menominee Tribe then sued in the Court of Claims for compensation for their loss.

The Supreme Court, in an opinion by Justice Douglas, held that the treaty language includes the right to hunt and fish, and that those rights "survived the Termination Act of 1954." The same Congress had also extended Public Law 280 to the Menominee Reservation with a proviso protecting treaty hunting and fishing rights. Read together, the Court held, the two laws left the treaty rights in place, and an intent to abrogate treaty rights is not lightly imputed to Congress.

The case is a leading statement of the rule that abrogation requires clear congressional intent. It limits the effect of the Cherokee Tobacco and Lone Wolf line of cases.
