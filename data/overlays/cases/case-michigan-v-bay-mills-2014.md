Michigan and the Bay Mills Indian Community had a gaming compact under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act. It allowed casino gaming on Indian lands in Michigan but not elsewhere. Bay Mills opened a second casino on land it bought with money from a congressionally created land trust, and said the parcel qualified as Indian land. Michigan sued to enjoin it. The 1836 treaty is linked in our data because the trust funds came from a treaty land purchase.

The Supreme Court held that tribal sovereign immunity barred the suit. Justice Kagan's opinion said tribes, as "domestic dependent nations," keep immunity from suit, including for commercial activity off Indian lands, unless Congress clearly removes it. The Act's text lifted immunity only for gaming on Indian lands, and Michigan's suit alleged the casino was off them. The Court also noted Michigan had other remedies and left open whether the result would differ if none existed.

The decision reaffirmed Kiowa (1998) and left any change to Congress, while confirming that tribal immunity does not depend on where the conduct occurs.
