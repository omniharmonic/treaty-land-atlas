Michigan and the Bay Mills Indian Community had a gaming compact under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act. It allowed Bay Mills to operate a casino on Indian lands within Michigan, but not outside that territory. Bay Mills ran a casino on its reservation in the Upper Peninsula. In 2010 it opened another in Vanderbilt, a village in the Lower Peninsula about 125 miles away.

Bay Mills had bought the Vanderbilt property with accrued interest from a federal appropriation. Congress had made that appropriation to compensate the Tribe for 19th-century takings of its ancestral lands, under the Michigan Indian Land Claims Settlement Act. The Tribe said it could operate a casino there because the property qualified as Indian land. Michigan disagreed and sued to enjoin the casino. The district court granted the injunction, but the Sixth Circuit vacated it.

The Supreme Court, 5-4, in an opinion by Justice Kagan, held that "immunity protects Bay Mills from this legal action." Congress had not abrogated tribal immunity from a State's suit to enjoin gaming off a reservation or other Indian lands. The Court declined to revisit its prior decisions that tribes have immunity even when a suit arises from off-reservation commercial activity. It said Michigan must resort to other mechanisms, including legal actions against responsible individuals.

The Court noted it did not decide whether the result would differ if no alternative remedy were available.
