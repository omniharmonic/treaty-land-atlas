In an 1837 Treaty several Chippewa Bands ceded land in present-day Minnesota and Wisconsin to the United States. The United States guaranteed hunting, fishing and gathering rights on the ceded land "during the pleasure of the President." In 1850 President Taylor issued an Executive Order ordering the Chippewa removed from the ceded land and revoking those rights. The removal policy was abandoned. An 1855 Treaty set aside reservations for the Mille Lacs Band but did not mention the earlier rights. Minnesota became a state in 1858. In 1990 the Mille Lacs Band sued the State.

The Supreme Court, 5-4, held that the Chippewa retained the rights. The Executive Order was ineffective because the President lacked authority for it and it was not severable. The 1855 Treaty, which transferred land, contained no express language ending usufructuary rights. Statehood did not extinguish them, since Congress must clearly express an intent to abrogate treaty rights. The Court said Ward v. Race Horse (1896) was misplaced.

The case confirms that off-reservation treaty rights can outlast statehood and that abrogation must be clear. It is central to modern co-management of ceded-territory resources.
