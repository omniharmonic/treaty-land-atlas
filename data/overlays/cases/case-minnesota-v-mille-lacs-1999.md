In 1837, several Chippewa Bands ceded land in present-day Minnesota and Wisconsin to the United States. The United States, in turn, guaranteed the Indians certain hunting, fishing and gathering rights on the ceded land "during the pleasure of the President of the United States."

In an 1850 Executive Order, President Taylor ordered the Chippewa's removal from the ceded territory and revoked those usufructuary rights, meaning the rights to use and take resources from the land. The United States ultimately abandoned its removal policy, but its attempts to acquire Chippewa lands continued. An 1855 Treaty set aside lands as reservations for the Mille Lacs Band, but made no mention of whether it abolished rights guaranteed by earlier treaties. Minnesota was admitted to the Union in 1858. In 1990, the Mille Lacs Band and several of its members sued Minnesota and its Department of Natural Resources, asking a court to declare that they retained their rights.

The Supreme Court, 5-4, in an opinion by Justice O'Connor, held that the Chippewa retain those rights. The 1850 Executive Order was ineffective, because the President's power to issue it must stem from an Act of Congress or the Constitution. The Mille Lacs Band did not give up its 1837 rights in the 1855 Treaty. And Minnesota's admission to the Union did not extinguish them: treaty rights are not impliedly terminated at statehood.

The Court said the State's reliance on Ward v. Race Horse (1896) was "misplaced." Chief Justice Rehnquist dissented, joined by Justices Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.
