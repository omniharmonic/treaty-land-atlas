The 1891 Act first authorized mineral leasing of Indian lands. A 1924 amendment said production of oil, gas and other minerals "may be taxed by the State in which said lands are located." The Indian Mineral Leasing Act of 1938 later set uniform leasing rules and had no provision for state taxation, but a general clause repealed all inconsistent laws. The Blackfeet Tribe challenged Montana taxes on its royalty income from leases issued to non-Indian lessees under the 1938 Act.

The Supreme Court held that Montana could not tax the royalties. It applied two canons: states may tax Indians only if Congress has clearly consented, and statutes are read liberally in favor of Indians. Nothing in the 1938 Act's text or history showed Congress meant to let states tax tribal royalties. The general repealer clause could not supply the clear consent required, and the 1924 taxing language referred to lands leased under the earlier laws.

The case reinforced the clear-statement rule protecting tribal income from state taxation. Cotton Petroleum v. New Mexico (1989) later allowed state taxes on non-Indian lessees' production, a limit on the principle.
