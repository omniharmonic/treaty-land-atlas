The Crow Tribe tried to bar non-members from hunting and fishing anywhere on its reservation, including fee land owned by non-Indians. It relied on its claimed ownership of the Big Horn riverbed, on the Fort Laramie treaties of 1851 and 1868, and on inherent sovereignty. The 1868 treaty set the reservation apart "for the absolute and undisturbed use and occupation" of the Tribe. The United States sued for the Tribe against Montana.

The Supreme Court held that title to the riverbed passed to Montana at statehood, because the treaties were not strong enough to overcome the presumption that the government holds beds under navigable waters for future states. It held that the Tribe can regulate hunting and fishing by non-members on tribal or trust land but has no power over non-Indians on land owned in fee by non-members. Tribal power beyond what is needed for self-government and internal relations cannot survive without express congressional delegation.

Montana became the starting point for tribal civil jurisdiction over non-members. Its two exceptions, for consensual relationships and for threats to tribal welfare, are applied in Strate, Hicks, Plains Commerce Bank and Brendale.
