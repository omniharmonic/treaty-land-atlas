Non-Indian employees of the Bureau of Indian Affairs sued over the agency's employment preference for qualified Indians, which dated to the Indian Reorganization Act of 1934. They said the 1972 Equal Employment Opportunity Act implicitly repealed the preference and that it violated the Fifth Amendment's due process guarantee. A three-judge federal court agreed and enjoined the preference. No treaty was involved.

The Supreme Court unanimously reversed. Congress had not repealed the preference: it left in place similar Indian preferences in the 1964 Civil Rights Act and enacted new ones shortly after the 1972 Act. The Court called this "a prototypical case where an adjudication of repeal by implication is not appropriate." It also held the preference was not racial discrimination but a classification tied to the government's unique relationship with tribes.

Mancari is the foundation for the "political, not racial" rationale used to defend laws specific to federally recognized tribes. It was invoked in Haaland v. Brackeen (2023) and in other challenges to tribal-specific laws.
