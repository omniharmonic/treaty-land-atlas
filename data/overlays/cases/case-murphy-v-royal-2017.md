Patrick Dwayne Murphy, a Creek citizen, was convicted of murder in an Oklahoma state court and sentenced to death. He argued that he should have been tried in federal court because the crime happened in Indian country, within the Creek Reservation. The state accepted that the offense occurred inside the Creek Reservation if Congress had not disestablished it. The Tenth Circuit's panel opinion issued in August 2017 (866 F.3d 1164); on November 9, 2017, it denied rehearing and issued the opinion reported at 875 F.3d 896.

The Tenth Circuit applied the Supreme Court's test and concluded: "We conclude the Reservation remains intact and therefore the crime was committed in Indian country." Only Congress may disestablish or diminish a reservation, and allotment and the other statutes the State cited did not do so. The court remanded for the district court to issue a writ of habeas corpus. Chief Judge Tymkovich, concurring in the denial of rehearing, said the panel faithfully applied Supreme Court precedent.

The Supreme Court took the case, then affirmed in Sharp v. Murphy (2020) for the reasons in McGirt. The ruling began the shift that placed much of eastern Oklahoma within Indian country for major-crimes purposes.
