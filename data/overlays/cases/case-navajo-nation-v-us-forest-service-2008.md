Several Indian tribes and their members consider a mountain peak in northern Arizona sacred in their religion. A ski area covers approximately one percent of that mountain. In 2002, the ski area submitted a proposal to the Forest Service to upgrade its operations, including a request to make artificial snow from recycled wastewater. The ski area had suffered highly variable snowfall for several years, with operating losses that threatened its operation.

The plaintiffs sued under the Religious Freedom Restoration Act (RFRA) and other laws. They said that using snow made from recycled wastewater, which contains 0.0001% human waste, would desecrate the entire mountain and devalue their religious exercises. The district court ruled for the government. A three-judge panel of the Ninth Circuit reversed, and the court then took the case en banc to clarify its interpretation of "substantial burden" under RFRA.

The en banc court ruled for the government. It held that where "there is no showing the government has coerced the Plaintiffs to act contrary to their religious beliefs under the threat of sanctions," or conditioned a benefit on conduct that would violate their beliefs, there is no "substantial burden." Otherwise, the court wrote, "each citizen would hold an individual veto" over government action on its own land. Judge William Fletcher dissented, joined by Judges Pregerson and Fisher.

In Apache Stronghold v. United States (2024), a majority of the en banc court overruled Navajo Nation's narrow definition of "substantial burden" under RFRA, while a different majority held the Lyng-based claim failed.
