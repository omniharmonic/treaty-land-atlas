Several Native nations and others challenged a federal decision to allow a ski area on national forest land in Arizona to make artificial snow from recycled wastewater, containing a tiny fraction of human waste. The plaintiffs said use of the snow on a mountain sacred in their religion would desecrate it and harm their ceremonies. They sued under the Religious Freedom Restoration Act (RFRA) and other laws. No treaty was at issue.

The Ninth Circuit, sitting en banc, ruled for the government. It said the project may offend the plaintiffs' feelings and reduce their spiritual satisfaction, but that is not a "substantial burden" under RFRA. A burden is substantial only when the government coerces people to act contrary to their beliefs under threat of sanctions or conditions a benefit on such conduct. Otherwise, the court reasoned, each citizen would hold "an individual veto" over federal action on federal land.

The court relied on Lyng v. Northwest Indian Cemetery Protective Association (1988). The Supreme Court denied certiorari in 2009. In Apache Stronghold (9th Cir. 2024), the en banc court overruled this decision's narrow definition of "substantial burden" while keeping the Lyng-based result.
