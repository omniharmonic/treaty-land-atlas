In 1854 the Omaha Tribe ceded most of its Nebraska land by treaty for a fixed sum, keeping a 300,000-acre reservation. In 1865 it ceded more land by treaty. Later Congress used a different method: the 1882 Act authorized the Secretary of the Interior to survey, appraise and sell about 50,000 acres of reservation land west of a railroad right-of-way, with proceeds held in the Treasury for the Tribe. A buyer founded the village of Pender there. In 2006 the Tribe applied its beverage ordinance to Pender retailers, who sued, joined by Nebraska.

The Supreme Court unanimously held the 1882 Act did not diminish the reservation. Only Congress can diminish a reservation, and its intent must be clear. The Act lacked the usual textual signs, such as explicit cession language or an unconditional promise of compensation. It simply opened land "for settlement" under the Secretary's rules.

Parker applied Solem's framework and confirmed that statutory text controls. The Court said later demographic change and jurisdictional practice cannot alone diminish a reservation, a point McGirt v. Oklahoma later echoed.
