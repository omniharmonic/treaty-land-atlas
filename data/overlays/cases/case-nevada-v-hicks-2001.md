Hicks is a member of the Fallon Paiute-Shoshone Tribes of western Nevada and lives on the Tribes' reservation. State game wardens suspected him of having killed a California bighorn sheep off the reservation, a gross misdemeanor under Nevada law. A state judge issued a search warrant for his home that was "subject to obtaining approval from the Fallon Tribal Court," and the wardens executed state-court and tribal-court warrants.

Hicks then sued the wardens in their individual capacities, and Nevada, in Tribal Court. He alleged trespass, abuse of process, and violation of constitutional rights remediable under 42 U.S.C. section 1983. The Tribal Court held that it had jurisdiction, and the Tribal Appeals Court affirmed. The wardens and the State went to federal court, which held for Hicks on that issue. The Ninth Circuit affirmed, concluding that the fact that Hicks's home is on tribe-owned reservation land is sufficient to support tribal jurisdiction over civil claims against nonmembers.

The Supreme Court reversed, in an opinion by Justice Scalia. It held the Tribal Court did not have jurisdiction to adjudicate the wardens' alleged conduct in executing a search warrant for an off-reservation crime. A tribal court's adjudicatory authority over nonmembers is "at most as broad as the tribe's regulatory authority." The Montana rule applies to both Indian and non-Indian land, and "tribal ownership is not alone enough to support regulatory jurisdiction over nonmembers."

The Court also said that tribal authority to regulate state officers executing process for off-reservation violations of state law is not essential to tribal self-government. Justice Souter, joined by two others, concurred in the opinion; Justices O'Connor, Stevens and Breyer concurred in the judgment.
