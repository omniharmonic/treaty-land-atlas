Hicks, a member of the Fallon Paiute-Shoshone Tribes, lived on the Tribes' reservation in Nevada. State game wardens searched his home for evidence of an off-reservation crime, using state-court and tribal-court warrants. He then sued the wardens and Nevada in tribal court for trespass, abuse of process and civil-rights violations. The tribal courts held they had jurisdiction, and the Ninth Circuit agreed because the home was on tribe-owned land.

The Supreme Court reversed. It held the tribal court did not have jurisdiction over the wardens' alleged conduct. A tribal court's authority over non-members is at most as broad as the tribe's regulatory authority, and the Montana rule that tribal power over non-members cannot survive without express congressional delegation, applies to Indian and non-Indian land alike. Tribal ownership of the land is one factor, not enough alone. Regulating state officers executing process for off-reservation violations is not necessary to tribal self-government.

Hicks extended the Montana framework to tribe-owned land and left states with authority on reservations for off-reservation violations of state law.
