With extensive federal help, the Mescalero Apache Tribe built a comprehensive scheme for managing fish and wildlife on its New Mexico reservation. Federally approved tribal ordinances regulate in detail how both members and non-members may hunt and fish there. New Mexico's rules conflicted with and sometimes were stricter than the tribal rules, and the State applied them to non-members on the reservation. The Tribe sued.

The Supreme Court unanimously held, in an opinion by Justice Marshall, that the application of New Mexico's laws to on-reservation hunting and fishing by non-members was pre-empted by federal law. Concurrent state jurisdiction would effectively nullify the Tribe's authority, interfere with its regulatory scheme, and threaten Congress's goal of tribal self-government and economic development. The State had not identified interests to justify concurrent authority; the license revenue it cited was insufficient.

Mescalero shows how tribal authority over non-members on tribal lands is supported by federal pre-emption, in contrast to Montana v. United States (1981) on non-Indian fee land. It is often cited with White Mountain Apache v. Bracker (1980).
