The Northwestern Bands of Shoshone Indians signed a treaty with the United States on July 30, 1863 (Box Elder). A special act of Congress in 1929 let them sue in the Court of Claims for claims "arising under or growing out of" that treaty. They asked for compensation for lands they claimed, arguing the United States had taken land they held by Indian title.

The Supreme Court affirmed the Court of Claims' dismissal. It held the 1863 treaty was not a recognition or acknowledgment by the United States of Indian title to the lands it mentioned, so a claim for the taking of those lands did not arise under the treaty. Permission for travel or mining, and use of the word "claim" or the phrase "country claimed," did not acknowledge title. Treaties, the Court said, are construed "according to their tenor."

The decision illustrates the limit set in cases like Tee-Hit-Ton (1955): without recognized title, a taking of aboriginal land generally brought no constitutional compensation. Claims such as these were later heard by the Indian Claims Commission.
