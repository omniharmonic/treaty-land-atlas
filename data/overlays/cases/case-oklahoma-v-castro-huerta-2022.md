Victor Castro-Huerta, a non-Indian, was convicted in Oklahoma state court of child neglect of his stepdaughter, a Cherokee Indian, in Tulsa and was sentenced to 35 years. While his appeal was pending, McGirt v. Oklahoma (2020) recognized that much of eastern Oklahoma, including Tulsa, is Indian country. He then argued only the federal government could prosecute him. The Oklahoma Court of Criminal Appeals agreed.

The Supreme Court reversed, 5-4, in an opinion by Justice Kavanaugh. It held that "The Federal Government and the State have concurrent jurisdiction to prosecute crimes committed by non-Indians against Indians in Indian country." States have jurisdiction over Indian country unless it is preempted. Neither the General Crimes Act nor Public Law 280 preempts, and state jurisdiction here does not infringe tribal self-government. Justice Gorsuch dissented, joined by three others.

The ruling reduced the jurisdictional effect of McGirt for crimes by non-Indians and has been criticized as eroding Worcester v. Georgia's principle of state exclusion from Indian country. The Court noted Worcester's view had long been abandoned.
