In 2015, Victor Manuel Castro-Huerta, a non-Indian, lived in Tulsa with his stepdaughter, who is a Cherokee Indian. He was charged by Oklahoma with child neglect, convicted in state court and sentenced to 35 years in prison.

While his appeal was pending, the Supreme Court decided McGirt v. Oklahoma, and the eastern part of Oklahoma, including Tulsa, came to be recognized as Indian country. Castro-Huerta then argued that only the federal government could prosecute him. The Oklahoma Court of Criminal Appeals agreed and vacated his conviction.

On June 29, 2022, the Supreme Court reversed. Justice Kavanaugh wrote for a five-justice majority that "The Federal Government and the State have concurrent jurisdiction to prosecute crimes committed by non-Indians against Indians in Indian country." Concurrent means both governments can bring charges. The Court said states have jurisdiction in Indian country unless federal law or tribal self-government blocks it, and found neither did here. It also said the old idea that Indian country is separate from state territory, associated with Worcester v. Georgia (1832), "has long since been abandoned."

Justice Gorsuch dissented, joined by Justices Breyer, Sotomayor and Kagan. The ruling matters because the decision means a state, not only the United States, can now prosecute these cases on reservations.
