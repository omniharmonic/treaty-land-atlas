Mark Oliphant, a non-Indian, was arrested by Suquamish tribal police on the Port Madison Reservation in Washington State, and Daniel Belgarde, a Colville tribal member, was arrested in a separate incident. Each was charged in tribal court and sought release in federal court. The Suquamish signed the 1855 Treaty of Point Elliott, which our data lists as the treaty at issue.

The Supreme Court, 6-2, reversed the Ninth Circuit. Justice Rehnquist wrote that "Indian tribal courts do not have inherent criminal jurisdiction to try and to punish non-Indians" without congressional authorization. The Court reasoned from the earliest treaties, from 19th-century congressional practice, and from the premise that tribes, "by submitting to the overriding sovereignty of the United States," necessarily yield power to try non-Indians except as Congress permits. Justice Marshall dissented, joined by Chief Justice Burger.

Oliphant created a gap in criminal enforcement on reservations, since non-Indian offenders fell outside tribal courts. Congress responded in part with later statutes, and United States v. Lara (2004) and Oklahoma v. Castro-Huerta (2022) are among the later cases on related questions.
