The Oneida Indian Nation of New York and the Oneida Indian Nation of Wisconsin sued the counties of Oneida and Madison in New York. They sought the fair rental value of land the Oneidas had ceded to the State of New York in 1795, for the years 1968 and 1969.

Their complaint said the Oneidas had owned and occupied the land from time immemorial. It said treaties with the United States in the 1780s and 1790s had confirmed their right to possess it until the United States bought it. It said the Nonintercourse Act, a federal law forbidding the conveyance of Indian lands without the consent of the United States, implemented those treaties in 1790, and that the 1795 cession had no such consent.

The federal district court ruled that the claim arose under state law and dismissed it for lack of jurisdiction. The Second Circuit affirmed. On January 21, 1974, the Supreme Court reversed and sent the case back. Justice White wrote for a unanimous Court, and the opinion states that "the rudimentary propositions that Indian title is a matter of federal law and can be extinguished only with federal consent apply in all of the States, including the original 13."

The result was that the Oneidas' suit could be heard in federal court. The opinion decided the question of federal jurisdiction, not whether the 1795 cession was valid.
