The Oneida Indian Nation and related plaintiffs sued two New York counties over land the Oneidas ceded to New York State in 1795. They asked for the fair rental value for a set period. They alleged that treaties of the 1780s and 1790s with the United States had confirmed their right to possess the land until the United States purchased it, that the 1790 Nonintercourse Act barred conveying Indian land without federal consent, and that the 1795 cession lacked that consent.

The district court dismissed for lack of federal jurisdiction, and the Second Circuit affirmed. The Supreme Court reversed, holding the complaint states a controversy arising under federal law. The Oneidas asserted a current right to possession conferred by federal law, independent of state law. And "Indian title is a matter of federal law and can be extinguished only with federal consent."

Oneida I opened the federal courthouse door to eastern land claims based on the Nonintercourse Act. It led to Oneida II (1985), which recognized a federal common-law right to sue, and to City of Sherrill v. Oneida (2005), which limited the remedies available.
