Oneida Indian tribes sued Oneida and Madison Counties in New York, alleging their ancestors conveyed tribal land to New York State in 1795 in violation of the Nonintercourse Act of 1793, which barred buying Indian land without federal approval. They sought damages for the fair rental value for a two-year period of land the counties now occupy. The district court found the counties liable and made New York indemnify them; the Second Circuit affirmed liability.

The Supreme Court, in an opinion by Justice Powell, held that the Oneidas have a federal common-law right of action to enforce their possessory rights. Their right of occupancy need not depend on a treaty or statute, and the Nonintercourse Act did not pre-empt that remedy. The Court also rejected the counties' defenses, including that a borrowed state statute of limitations barred the claim.

Oneida II left open whether equitable considerations should limit relief against present-day parties, a question the Court answered in City of Sherrill v. Oneida (2005). The case encouraged other eastern land claims, including in Maine and Massachusetts.
