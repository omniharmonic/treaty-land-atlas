By an 1864 Treaty, the Klamath and related tribes ceded aboriginal title to lands in Oregon and gained a reservation, with "the exclusive right of taking fish in the streams and lakes, included in said reservation." Later a boundary dispute arose over land erroneously excluded from the reservation. After lengthy negotiations the Tribe and the United States signed a 1901 Cession Agreement, ratified by Congress, in which the Tribe received payment and ceded some reservation land, conveying "all their claim, right, title and interest."

The Tribe sued Oregon to stop interference with hunting and fishing on those ceded lands. The Supreme Court, in an opinion by Justice Stevens, held that the right to hunt and fish free of state regulation did not survive there. The 1864 Treaty language limited the right to the reservation, and the 1901 Agreement's broad language diminished the reservation boundaries. Nothing in the Agreement evidenced intent to preserve special off-reservation rights.

Klamath is a key case on how land cession agreements can end hunting and fishing rights. Minnesota v. Mille Lacs (1999) distinguished it.
