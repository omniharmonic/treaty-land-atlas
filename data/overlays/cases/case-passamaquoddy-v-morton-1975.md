The Passamaquoddy Tribe told the Bureau of Indian Affairs it had lost most of its aboriginal territory in a treaty negotiated in 1794 with Massachusetts, whose claims Maine inherited. It also said Maine had wrongfully diverted 6,000 of the 23,000 acres reserved to the Tribe in that treaty. They asked the Secretary of the Interior to sue Maine, because a claim might be barred after July 18, 1972. The Secretary refused on the ground that the United States had no trust relationship with the Tribe.

The First Circuit affirmed the district court. It held that the Indian Nonintercourse Act, which protects the lands of "any tribe of Indians," applies to the Passamaquoddy and establishes a trust relationship for land transactions covered by the Act. The court stressed that the trust "has as its source the Nonintercourse Act" and does not extend to every subject. It did not decide whether the United States must sue Maine.

The ruling led to settlement of the Maine land claims. Congress enacted the Maine Indian Claims Settlement Act in 1980, and the case encouraged the eastern land claims litigation discussed in Oneida I and II.
