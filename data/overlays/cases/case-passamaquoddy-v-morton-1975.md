The Passamaquoddy Tribe lives on two reservations in Washington County, Maine. Its representatives wrote to the Bureau of Indian Affairs with grievances against Maine and its predecessor, Massachusetts. They said Maine had taken most of the Tribe's aboriginal territory in a treaty negotiated in 1794, and had wrongfully diverted 6,000 of the 23,000 acres reserved to the Tribe in that treaty.

The Tribe asked the Secretary of the Interior to sue Maine on its behalf before July 18, 1972, the date an action would allegedly be barred. The Secretary did not act. The Interior Department's Acting Solicitor advised that there was no trust relationship between the United States and the Tribe. On June 2, 1972, the Tribe's representatives sued for a declaration that the Tribe is protected by the Indian Nonintercourse Act.

The district court ruled for the Tribe, and the First Circuit affirmed, with qualifications. The Nonintercourse Act, passed by the First Congress in 1790, protects the lands of "any tribe of Indians." The Tribe argued, and the courts agreed, that this includes a tribe not otherwise recognized by the federal government.

The court stressed that "the 'trust relationship' we affirm has as its source the Nonintercourse Act," so it covers land transactions the Act reaches. It did not decide whether the United States must actually sue Maine; the court called that a separate question not raised below.
