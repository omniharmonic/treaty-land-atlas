Plains Commerce Bank, a non-Indian bank, owned land in fee simple on a tribal reservation. It sold that land to non-Indians. The Longs, an Indian couple who had leased the land with an option to buy, sued in tribal court, claiming the Bank offered them worse terms than the buyers received. A tribal jury ruled against the Bank on three claims including discrimination, and the tribal court gave the Longs an option to buy the land they occupied, nullifying part of the sale.

The Supreme Court, in an opinion by Chief Justice Roberts, held the tribal court lacked jurisdiction over the discrimination claim. Under Montana, tribes generally lack authority over nonmembers' activity on fee land, and neither of Montana's exceptions authorized regulating the Bank's sale of its own fee land. Because "a tribe's adjudicative jurisdiction does not exceed its legislative jurisdiction," the tribal court could not hear the claim.

The decision narrowed the "consensual relationship" exception and reinforced the loss of tribal authority over land once it passes into non-Indian fee ownership, building on Brendale (1989).
