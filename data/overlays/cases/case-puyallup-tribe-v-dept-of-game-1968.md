Article III of the Treaty of Medicine Creek, made with the Puyallup, Nisqually and other Indians, secured "the right of taking fish at all usual and accustomed grounds and stations ... in common with all citizens of the Territory." The cases concerned salmon and steelhead that hatch in the Puyallup and Nisqually Rivers. The Indians fished with set nets for their own use and for commercial sale. Washington tried to regulate that fishing, and its Supreme Court held the treaty rights could be regulated if the rules were reasonable and necessary.

The United States Supreme Court, in an opinion by Justice Douglas, held that the State "may in the interest of conservation regulate fishing by the Indians 'in common with' the fishing by others." It did not decide whether set nets at the places the Indians used them were allowed, because the record did not reach that question.

Puyallup I recognized state power to regulate treaty fishing for conservation. It was a step toward the later litigation in United States v. Washington (Boldt decision, 1974) and Washington v. Fishing Vessel Association (1979), which defined the tribes' share of the catch.
