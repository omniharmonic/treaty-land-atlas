The Rosebud Sioux Reservation in South Dakota was defined by an 1889 Act, and the 1868 Treaty had required consent of three fourths of adult male tribal members for land cessions. In 1901 an agreement for a fixed-sum payment, which would have diminished the reservation, was approved by three fourths of the adult men but never ratified by Congress. Instead Congress passed the Acts of 1904, 1907 and 1910. They required the tribe to cede land in certain counties for sale to homesteaders and towns, with the proceeds credited to the tribe only as the land sold, or for transfer to South Dakota for school use.

The Supreme Court, 6-3, held the language and legislative history of those Acts "clearly" showed a congressional intent to diminish the reservation. That the Acts were unilateral and lacked the treaty's three-fourths consent did not directly bear on intent, and nor did the change from a fixed-sum payment.

Rosebud, with DeCoteau (1975), is an example of a finding that surplus-land acts ended reservation status. Solem v. Bartlett (1984) later set a more detailed framework.
