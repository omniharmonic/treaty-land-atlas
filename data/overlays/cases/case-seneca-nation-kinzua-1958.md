The Seneca Nation of Indians asked a federal court to stop the construction of the Allegheny Reservoir Project, which would flood a substantial amount of Seneca land. The district court dismissed the complaint.

Both sides agreed on two points. First, the flooding would infringe rights the Seneca acquired by treaty in 1794 (7 Stat. 44). Second, Congress can authorize a taking of land by eminent domain even though a treaty exists. The only question was whether Congress had shown, in a sufficiently clear and specific way, that it meant to do so.

The D.C. Circuit looked at an Act of August 18, 1941 that appropriated $45,000,000 for the Ohio River Basin plan modified to include the Allegheny Reservoir, and at an appropriation in August 1957. The hearings, debate and Senate report on the 1957 bill showed Congress knew that the Seneca lands would be flooded, that the Nation was unwilling to give up any of its rights in them, and that the lands could be taken by eminent domain.

One of New York's senators, Senator Ives, said the action would be "virtually wiping out the Allegheny Reservation of the Seneca Nation." He added that the Government "has the legal right to do what is proposed," but that he was distressed by "the moral responsibility we have." On November 25, 1958, the court, in a short per curiam opinion, held: "We think Congress has authorized the taking of these lands." It affirmed the dismissal.
