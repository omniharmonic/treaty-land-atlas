Paul Seymour, a member of the Colville Tribe, was convicted in Washington state court of attempted burglary in Okanogan County, at a place he said was within the Colville Indian Reservation. He sought habeas corpus, saying the offense occurred in "Indian country," so only the United States had jurisdiction under 18 U.S.C. 1153. The state court denied relief on the ground that the place was no longer part of a reservation.

The Supreme Court, in an opinion by Justice Black, reversed. It held that the Act of March 22, 1906, which provided for disposition of surplus lands in the South Half of the diminished Colville Reservation, did not dissolve the reservation. The land was within its limits. Even if a non-Indian held a patent in fee, 18 U.S.C. 1151 defines Indian country to include "all land within the limits of any Indian reservation ... notwithstanding the issuance of any patent." A federally laid-out townsite did not change the result.

Seymour set the presumption that opening reservation land to settlement does not end the reservation unless Congress clearly says so. Mattz, DeCoteau, Rosebud and Solem later built on it.
