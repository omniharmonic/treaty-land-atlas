A treaty of July 3, 1868 set apart a reservation for the Shoshone Indians exclusively. On March 18, 1878, a band of Arapahoes, under military escort, settled on the land, and others followed. The Commissioner of Indian Affairs directed or sanctioned this, intending the settlements to be permanent, and later acts of Congress on cessions and allotment treated the two tribes as equal beneficiaries. The Shoshones protested throughout. In 1927 Congress allowed them to sue in the Court of Claims for the taking of an undivided half interest in their tribal lands.

The Supreme Court reversed the Court of Claims' award on how to measure damages. It held that the guardianship of the United States did not permit forcing a tribe with an exclusive treaty right of occupancy "to share it with another tribe without just compensation." A taking can be partial, and the original wrongful act, ratified by the government's later actions, relates back to March 18, 1878, the date for valuation. Interest or a fair equivalent is added.

The case shows that treaty-guaranteed tribal land is compensable property under the Fifth Amendment, in contrast to unrecognized aboriginal title in Tee-Hit-Ton (1955).
