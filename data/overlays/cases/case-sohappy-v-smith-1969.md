Fourteen members of the Yakima Indian Nation sued Oregon fish and game officials for a decree defining their treaty right "of taking fish at all usual and accustomed places" on the Columbia River and its tributaries, and how far Oregon could regulate it. The United States filed a parallel case on behalf of the Yakima, Umatilla, Nez Perce and others, and the Warm Springs Tribe intervened. The tribes' rights came from the 1855 treaties of the Columbia Plateau.

Judge Belloni, in a decision in July 1969, applied the Supreme Court's Puyallup decision. State regulation of treaty fishing must be necessary for conservation of the fish, must not discriminate against the Indians, and must meet appropriate standards. The court found Oregon's regulations had not met those limits, and held that the treaty Indians "having an absolute right to that fishery, are entitled to a fair share of the fish produced by the Columbia River system." The 1918 Columbia Interstate Compact did not impair the treaty right.

Sohappy v. Smith, with the later Boldt decision (1974), set the model for treaty-fishing litigation in the Northwest.
