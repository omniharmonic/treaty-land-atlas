In 1908 Congress authorized the Secretary of the Interior to open 1.6 million acres of the Cheyenne River Sioux Reservation in South Dakota for homesteading, with sale proceeds credited to the Indians. A tribal member was convicted in state court of attempted rape on opened land. He argued that the crime occurred in Indian country, so South Dakota lacked jurisdiction.

The Supreme Court unanimously agreed with him, in an opinion by Justice Marshall. It held "only Congress can divest an Indian reservation of its land and diminish its boundaries," and must clearly evince that intent. The Act's words "sell and dispose" and the credit of proceeds to Indian accounts suggested the Secretary acted as sales agent. Isolated phrases, the circumstances and later events did not show a clear intent to diminish. The opened lands also kept their Indian character.

Solem's framework, looking first to statutory text, then to historical context, then to later events, is used in Hagen v. Utah (1994), Nebraska v. Parker (2016) and McGirt v. Oklahoma (2020).
