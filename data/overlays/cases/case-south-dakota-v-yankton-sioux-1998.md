The Yankton Sioux Reservation in South Dakota was established by an 1858 Treaty. After the 1887 Dawes Act, tribal members took allotments and the government negotiated for the rest. An 1892 agreement said the Tribe would "cede, sell, relinquish, and convey to the United States" its unallotted lands for $600,000. A saving clause said nothing in it would abrogate the 1858 treaty. Congress ratified the agreement in 1894 and non-Indians rapidly acquired the land. The dispute arose over environmental regulation of a solid waste facility on unallotted fee land within the original boundaries.

The Supreme Court held that the 1894 Act's operative language and the circumstances of its passage showed Congress intended to diminish the reservation. The land was therefore not Indian country under 18 U.S.C. 1151, and the State had primary jurisdiction.

The decision shows that language of cession for a sum certain strongly signals diminishment, and that a treaty saving clause need not preserve boundaries. It sits beside Hagen and contrasts with Solem.
