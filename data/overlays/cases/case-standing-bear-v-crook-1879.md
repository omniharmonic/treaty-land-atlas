Standing Bear and other Ponca Indians were held by General George Crook, commanding the Department of the Platte, so they could be removed to the Indian Territory. Treaties of 1858 and 1865 had reserved a tract for the Poncas. The court recounts that a Sioux treaty of 1868 then absolved the reservations set apart for the Poncas "without consultation with, or knowledge or consent on the part of, the Ponca tribe." Standing Bear testified that of 581 Ponca who went to the Indian Territory, 158 died within a year or so, and that he left to return home.

Judge Elmer Dundy held that "an Indian is a 'person' within the meaning of the habeas corpus act" and may seek the writ in federal court when held under color of United States authority in violation of law. He wrote that in time of peace no authority exists for transporting Indians from one section of the country to another without their consent, or confining them to a reservation against their will. The relators were ordered released.

The decision is a landmark of Native civil rights. It did not settle citizenship (see Elk v. Wilkins, 1884).
