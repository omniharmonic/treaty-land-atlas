The Tee-Hit-Ton Indians, an identifiable group of the Tlingit Tribe of Alaska, claimed that the United States took timber from Alaskan lands in and near the Tongass National Forest that belonged to them. They sued in the Court of Claims for compensation under the Fifth Amendment. No treaty was at issue. Congress had passed the Organic Act for Alaska of 1884 and a 1900 act without recognizing any permanent Indian rights in the lands, and a 1947 resolution authorized timber sales without deciding the validity of Native claims.

The Supreme Court held the Tee-Hit-Ton were not entitled to compensation. It said "Permissive Indian occupancy may be extinguished by Congress in its own discretion without compensation," relying on Johnson v. McIntosh. Indian occupancy that Congress has not specifically recognized as ownership may be taken without payment. The Court rejected the argument that the Tlingit's way of life or Russian treatment of Natives took them out of that rule.

Tee-Hit-Ton drew a sharp line between recognized title, which brings Fifth Amendment protection (Shoshone Tribe, Creek Nation), and aboriginal title. It has been widely criticized but not overruled. Congress addressed Alaska claims through the 1971 settlement act discussed in Alaska v. Venetie.
