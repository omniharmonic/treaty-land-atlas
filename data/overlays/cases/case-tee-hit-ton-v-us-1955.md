The Tee-Hit-Ton Indians, an identifiable group of between 60 and 70 individuals residing in Alaska, belonged to the Tlingit Tribe. They claimed that the United States took timber from Alaskan lands in and near the Tongass National Forest that allegedly belonged to them. The area claimed was said to contain over 350,000 acres of land and 150 square miles of water. They sued in the Court of Claims for compensation under the Fifth Amendment.

No treaty was involved. The Court found that neither the Organic Act for Alaska of 1884 nor the Act of June 6, 1900 recognized any permanent rights of Indians in the lands they occupied. A Joint Resolution of August 8, 1947 authorized the sale of the timber without recognizing or denying the validity of claims to possession.

On February 7, 1955, Justice Reed wrote for the Court that "Permissive Indian occupancy may be extinguished by Congress in its own discretion without compensation," relying on Johnson v. McIntosh and other cases. The Court held that "Indian occupancy, not specifically recognized as ownership by action authorized by Congress, may be extinguished by the Government without compensation."

It rejected the argument that the group's stage of civilization, its concept of property ownership or Russia's treatment of Natives took it out of the rule that applies to Indians in the States. The Court of Claims judgment was affirmed. Justice Douglas dissented.
