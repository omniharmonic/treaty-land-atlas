In 1855 the Yakimas and other Indians owned and occupied lands in Washington Territory that the United States wished to open to settlers. Representatives of the Government met in council with representatives of the Indians on May 29, 1855. After discussions lasting until June 11, the Indians agreed to a treaty. They were to cede 16,920 square miles of their territory and keep 1,233 square miles for the confederated tribes. In return the Government agreed to pay $200,000, build schools, shops and mills and a hospital, and provide teachers and helpers for twenty years.

The treaty reserved to tribal members the right to take fish "at all usual and accustomed places, in common with the citizens" of Washington Territory.

Sampson Tulee, a member of the Yakima tribe, was convicted in Klickitat County, Washington, of catching salmon with a net without first obtaining a state license. The Washington Supreme Court affirmed. Tulee argued the state license law conflicted with the treaty.

On March 30, 1942, Justice Black wrote for the Supreme Court that the State may impose on the Indians, equally with others, restrictions "of a purely regulatory nature" about the time and manner of fishing outside the reservation that are needed to conserve fish. But the State "can not require them to pay license fees that are both regulatory and revenue-producing." The Court reversed the state judgment.
