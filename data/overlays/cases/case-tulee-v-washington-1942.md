Sampson Tulee, a member of the Yakima Tribe, was convicted in Klickitat County, Washington, of catching salmon with a net without a state license. He argued that the 1855 treaty with the Yakima, which reserved the right to take fish "at all usual and accustomed places, in common with the citizens" of the Territory, barred the state license requirement. The treaty is the Treaty of May 29, 1855.

The Supreme Court, in an opinion by Justice Black, held that the State may impose on the Indians, equally with others, regulations of a "purely regulatory nature" about the time and manner of fishing outside the reservation that are needed for conservation. But it cannot require them to pay license fees that are both regulatory and revenue-producing. The state conviction was reversed.

Tulee is an early case reading the Stevens treaties' fishing clause in favor of tribes and distinguishing conservation regulation from taxation-like fees. It anticipated Puyallup (1968), United States v. Washington (1974) and later Yakama cases such as Cougar Den (2019).
