A Crow tribal police officer on a public highway through the Crow Reservation in Montana approached a parked truck late at night. The driver, Cooley, appeared to be non-native, and the officer saw two semiautomatic rifles on the front seat. The officer ordered him out, searched him, and saw drug paraphernalia. A federal grand jury then indicted Cooley on drug and gun offenses. The District Court suppressed the drug evidence and the Ninth Circuit affirmed, reasoning that the tribal officer first had to determine that the suspect was non-Indian before acting.

The Supreme Court unanimously reversed, in an opinion by Justice Breyer. It held that "a tribal police officer has authority to detain temporarily and to search non-Indian persons traveling on public rights-of-way running through a reservation for potential violations of state or federal law." Under Montana v. United States, the second exception, tribal authority over conduct that threatens the tribe's health or welfare, "fits almost like a glove."

Cooley narrowly preserved tribal law-enforcement authority in the wake of Oliphant's ban on tribal criminal jurisdiction over non-Indians. The Court stressed it is about detaining and searching, not prosecuting, non-Indians.
