In an 1833 treaty and patent, the United States conveyed a large tract to the Creek Tribe in fee simple. In an 1866 treaty the Creeks ceded half and the United States guaranteed them quiet possession of the rest. By an error of the Land Department, part of the Creek land was included in the survey for Sac and Fox Indians under an 1867 treaty. When the Sac and Fox lands were receded to the United States under an 1891 act, the Creek land was wrongly included, allotted to Sacs and Foxes, and sold to settlers. The United States kept the proceeds.

The Supreme Court held the Creeks had a claim for compensation. The United States had appropriated the land in circumstances involving an implied undertaking to pay, and the taking occurred by the 1891 disposals, which the government in effect confirmed. Compensation was measured at the time of disposal, plus interest at 5 percent.

Creek Nation states that "Property of an Indian Tribe under guardianship of the United States cannot constitutionally be appropriated by the United States without just compensation." That rule for recognized tribal property was reaffirmed in Shoshone Tribe (1937).
