Lara, an Indian who was not a member of the Spirit Lake Tribe, ignored the Tribe's order excluding him from its reservation and struck a federal officer arresting him. He pleaded guilty in Tribal Court to violence to a policeman. The federal government then charged him with assaulting a federal officer, and he argued that the Double Jeopardy Clause barred the second prosecution.

After Duro v. Reina (1990) held tribes lacked sovereign authority to prosecute nonmember Indians, Congress amended the Indian Civil Rights Act to "recognize and affirm" the tribes' "inherent power" to exercise criminal jurisdiction over all Indians. The Supreme Court, in an opinion by Justice Breyer, held that Congress has the constitutional power to lift the restrictions on tribal criminal jurisdiction over nonmember Indians. Because the Tribe acted in its capacity as a sovereign authority, the Double Jeopardy Clause did not bar the federal prosecution.

Lara confirmed Congress's authority to adjust the scope of inherent tribal sovereignty. It is the main precedent behind later restorations of tribal criminal jurisdiction, including over some non-Indians, and was referred to in Oklahoma v. Castro-Huerta (2022).
