In 1964 the Navajo Nation leased coal-mining rights to the predecessor of Peabody Coal Company, under the Indian Mineral Leasing Act of 1938. The lease capped the royalty at 37.5 cents per ton, subject to adjustment by the Secretary of the Interior at 20 years. By then the rate yielded about 2 percent of gross proceeds, well below the rate Congress set for federal coal. In 1984 a Bureau of Indian Affairs official raised it to 20 percent. While Peabody's appeal was pending, Secretary Hodel urged the parties to negotiate, and in 1985 they agreed to 12.5 percent.

The Navajo Nation sued for damages for breach of trust. The Supreme Court, in an opinion by Justice Ginsburg, reversed the Federal Circuit. It held that earlier cases (United States v. Mitchell I and II) controlled, and that a tribe must invoke a source of law that "can fairly be interpreted as mandating compensation by the Federal Government." The Act and its regulations did not impose that liability.

Navajo Nation (2003) narrowed the trust responsibility enforceable through damages suits to duties spelled out in statutes and regulations, a rule later applied in Jicarilla (2011).
