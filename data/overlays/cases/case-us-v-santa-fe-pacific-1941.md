The Act of July 27, 1866 granted lands in Arizona to the Atlantic & Pacific Railroad Company. The United States, in its own right and as guardian of the Walapai (Hualpai) Indians of Arizona, sued the railroad's successor, the Santa Fe Pacific Railroad. It asked the court to establish the Walapai's right to lands the railroad claimed, inside and outside the Indians' reservation, and for an accounting.

On December 8, 1941, Justice Douglas wrote for the Supreme Court. The railroad's grant lands, the Court held, "were subject to any existing Indian right of occupancy" until the United States extinguished it through a voluntary cession by the Indians, as section 2 of the Act provided. "Indian title" exists, the Court said, where land was part of a tribe's ancestral home, "definable territory occupied exclusively by that tribe." A tribal claim "need not be based upon treaty, statute, or other formal governmental action." Extinguishing such title is a power of Congress that courts cannot inquire into.

The Court found that a forcible removal of the Walapais in 1874 was not sanctioned by Congress and could not affect their right to lands outside the reservation then set aside. But the Executive Order of January 4, 1883 created the Walapai Indian Reservation at their request. Its creation and acceptance by them amounted to a relinquishment of tribal claims to lands outside it, which the Court treated as a voluntary cession. The Court affirmed with a modification: the United States was entitled to an accounting from the railroad for rents and profits from reservation lands the Walapais had occupied from time immemorial.
