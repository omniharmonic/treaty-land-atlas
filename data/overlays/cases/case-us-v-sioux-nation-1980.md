The Fort Laramie Treaty of 1868 set apart the Great Sioux Reservation, including the Black Hills, for the "absolute and undisturbed use and occupation" of the Sioux, and required the signatures of three fourths of adult Sioux men for any cession. In 1876 an "agreement" signed by only 10 percent of adult men gave up the Hills and hunting rights for rations. Congress enacted it in 1877. The Sioux regarded that as a breach. A 1920 act let them sue; the Court of Claims dismissed in 1942, and a 1978 act later allowed new review of the merits.

The Supreme Court, in an opinion by Justice Blackmun, affirmed. The 1877 Act effected a taking of tribal property set aside by treaty, not a mere change in the form of investment, so just compensation with interest was owed. Principal was $17.1 million, the 1877 market value, plus interest. The 1978 act waiving res judicata was constitutional. The Court of Claims called it "a more ripe and rank case of dishonorable dealings" than any in our history.

The case is a leading example of a court finding that a unilateral act ending treaty rights was a compensable taking.
