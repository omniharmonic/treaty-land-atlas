In September 1970 the United States, as trustee for several western Washington tribes, sued the State of Washington over state restrictions on treaty fishing. The tribes had signed treaties in the 1850s, including Medicine Creek and Point Elliott, with identical wording that secured "the right of taking fish, at all usual and accustomed grounds and stations ... in common with all citizens of the territory." Many more tribes later joined. The Washington Departments of Fisheries and Game and a reef net owners association were defendants.

After a lengthy trial, Judge George Boldt held that "in common with" means "sharing equally the opportunity to take fish." Non-treaty fishermen may take up to 50 percent of the harvestable fish at the tribes' usual and accustomed grounds, and treaty fishermen may take up to the same percentage. The decision also treated off-reservation fishing by other citizens as a privilege, not a right, and held the tribes cannot rescind the agreement or limit non-Indian fishing under it.

The Ninth Circuit affirmed (520 F.2d 676) and the Supreme Court largely upheld the allocation in Washington v. Fishing Vessel Association (1979). The decision led to tribal co-management of Northwest salmon fisheries.
