Wheeler, a member of the Navajo Tribe, pleaded guilty in Tribal Court to contributing to the delinquency of a minor. He was then indicted by a federal grand jury for statutory rape arising from the same incident. He argued that the tribal offense was a lesser included offense of the federal one, so the Double Jeopardy Clause barred the federal trial. The Ninth Circuit agreed, reasoning that tribal and federal courts were not "arms of separate sovereigns."

The Supreme Court reversed. The controlling question was the source of a tribe's power to punish its own members: inherent sovereignty, or authority delegated by Congress. Tribes still possess sovereignty "not withdrawn by treaty or statute, or by implication as a necessary result of their dependent status." From the Navajo treaties and federal criminal statutes, the Court found the Navajo Tribe had never given up this power. A tribe punishing a member acts as an independent sovereign, so the two prosecutions are not "for the same offence."

Wheeler is the foundation for inherent tribal criminal jurisdiction over members and for the doctrine that tribal sovereignty is not a delegation. Lara (2004) and later cases relied on it.
