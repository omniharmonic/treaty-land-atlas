Under an 1859 treaty (listed in our data as the Treaty of 1855 with the Yakama), the Yakima Indians relinquished land to the United States but kept the right to take fish at "all usual and accustomed places, in common with the citizens of the Territory" and to build temporary buildings to cure them. Winans and others held land along the Columbia River under federal patents, and used state-licensed fishing wheels that excluded Indians from a well-known fishing place. The United States sued to protect the Yakima's access.

The Supreme Court, in an opinion that put the rule in a famous phrase, said it would "construe a treaty with Indians as they understood it and as justice and reason demand." The treaty was "not a grant of right to the Indians but a reservation by the Indians of rights already possessed and not granted away by them." The reserved rights imposed a servitude on the land, continuing against later buyers and the State. Statehood did not allow Washington to disregard them.

Winans is the foundation of the "reserved rights" doctrine for treaty fishing, cited in Tulee, Puyallup and Fishing Vessel.
