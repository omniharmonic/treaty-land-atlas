Washington taxes importers who bring fuel into the state by "ground transportation." Cougar Den, a wholesale fuel importer owned by a Yakama Nation member, hauled fuel from Oregon over public highways to Yakama-owned gas stations on the reservation. The State assessed $3.6 million in taxes, penalties and fees. Cougar Den argued that the 1855 treaty between the United States and the Yakama Nation, which reserves the Yakamas' "right, in common with citizens of the United States, to travel upon all public highways," pre-empted the tax. The Washington Supreme Court agreed.

The Supreme Court affirmed, but without a majority opinion. Justice Breyer, joined by two justices, found the tax taxed the importation by highway and was pre-empted, because the Court had read the treaty language before, as the Yakamas would have understood it. Justice Gorsuch, joined by Justice Ginsburg, concurred in the judgment on different grounds. Chief Justice Roberts and Justice Kavanaugh each dissented.

The ruling reaffirmed that treaty-reserved rights can override state taxes in the specific setting of highway travel. It follows Winans and Tulee on the Stevens treaties.
