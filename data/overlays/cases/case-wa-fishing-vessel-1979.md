In 1854 and 1855 the United States made a series of treaties with Indian tribes in what is now Washington. The tribes gave up their interest in much land in exchange for payments, small reserved parcels, and the "right of taking fish at usual and accustomed grounds and stations ... in common with all citizens of the Territory." In 1970 the United States sued the State on its own behalf and as trustee for seven tribes. The District Court held the tribes were entitled to 45 to 50 percent of the harvestable fish.

The Supreme Court, in an opinion by Justice Stevens, held that the treaty language secured more than access and an equal chance to fish: it secures to the tribes "a right to harvest a share of each run of anadromous fish that passes through tribal fishing areas." This followed Winans, Puyallup and other earlier decisions. The Court largely affirmed the District Court's allocation.

Fishing Vessel confirmed Judge Boldt's 1974 decision and led to co-management of Pacific Northwest fisheries. The Court later split evenly in the culverts case (2018).
