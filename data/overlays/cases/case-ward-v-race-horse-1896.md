Race Horse, a Bannock, was held for violating Wyoming's game laws. The Bannock Treaty of February 24, 1869 (Fort Bridger) said they "shall have the right to hunt upon the unoccupied lands of the United States so long as game may be found thereon." Race Horse sought release through habeas corpus, relying on the treaty. The lower court discharged him.

The Supreme Court reversed. The treaty right, it held, did not give the Bannocks the right to hunt in Wyoming in violation of the state's laws. The Wyoming statehood act admitted the State "on an equal footing with the original States in all respects whatever" and contained no reservation for Indians. The Court reasoned that the hunting privilege lasted only while the land remained unoccupied federal land, a temporary right the admission of the State ended. It also cited the rule that exemptions must be strictly construed.

Race Horse was widely seen as a low point for treaty rights. Minnesota v. Mille Lacs (1999) distinguished it, and Herrera v. Wyoming (2019) held it was no longer good law on the effect of statehood.
