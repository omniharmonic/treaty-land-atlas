Pinetop Logging Company, a non-Indian business authorized to do business in Arizona, had a contract with an organization of the White Mountain Apache Tribe. It felled tribal timber on the Fort Apache Reservation and transported it to the tribal organization's sawmill. All of its work was done on the reservation, and the hauling was on roads of the Bureau of Indian Affairs and the Tribe.

Arizona agencies sought to impose a motor carrier license tax, based on gross receipts, and a use fuel tax on diesel burned on highways. Pinetop paid under protest and sued in state court, arguing that federal law barred the taxes. The trial court and the Arizona Court of Appeals ruled for the State.

On June 27, 1980, the Supreme Court reversed. Justice Marshall wrote that "the tradition of Indian sovereignty over the reservation and tribal members must inform the determination whether the exercise of state authority has been pre-empted by operation of federal law." When a State asserts authority over non-Indians on a reservation, the Court said, it must make a particularized inquiry into the state, federal and tribal interests at stake.

The Court found federal regulation of the harvesting, sale and management of tribal timber, and of the roads, so pervasive that it left no room for the taxes. It said they would undermine the federal policy that timber profits benefit the Tribe. Justice Stevens dissented, joined by Justices Stewart and Rehnquist.
