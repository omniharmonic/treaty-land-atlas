Pinetop Logging Company, a non-Indian business, cut timber on the Fort Apache Reservation under a contract with a tribal organization, and hauled it to the tribe's sawmill. All of its work took place on the reservation, on roads owned by the Bureau of Indian Affairs and the Tribe. Arizona sought to impose a motor carrier license tax based on gross receipts and a use fuel tax. Pinetop paid under protest and sued, arguing federal law barred the taxes.

The Supreme Court held the taxes pre-empted. The tradition of Indian sovereignty "must inform the determination" of whether state authority is pre-empted, and where a State regulates non-Indians on a reservation, a "particularized inquiry" into state, federal and tribal interests is required. Federal regulation of tribal timber harvesting and sale, and of reservation roads, was so pervasive that it left no room for the additional burden of the taxes.

Bracker established the interest-balancing test for state regulation of non-Indians on reservations. It was applied in Mescalero (1983) and distinguished in later cases that allowed state taxes on non-Indian activities.
