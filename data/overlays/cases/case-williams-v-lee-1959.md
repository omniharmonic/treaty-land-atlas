A non-Indian trader ran the Ganado Trading Post on the Navajo Reservation in Arizona under a federal trading license. He sued a Navajo man and his wife, who lived on the reservation, in an Arizona state court to collect for goods sold on credit there. They moved to dismiss, saying jurisdiction lay in the tribal court. The Arizona Supreme Court held that because no Act of Congress expressly forbade state courts from hearing the case, they could.

The United States Supreme Court, in an opinion by Justice Black, reversed. Allowing state jurisdiction here "would undermine the authority of the tribal courts over Reservation affairs and hence would infringe on the right of the Indians to govern themselves." That right was recognized by Congress in the Treaty of 1868 with the Navajos and had never been taken away.

Williams v. Lee is the origin of the "infringement test": a state may not act where it infringes on the right of reservation Indians to make their own laws and be ruled by them. It protected tribal courts as the forum for reservation disputes, and later cases like Strate and Hicks built on it.
