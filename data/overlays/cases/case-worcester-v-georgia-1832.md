Samuel Worcester, a missionary, was indicted in a Georgia court for residing in the Cherokee Nation without a state license, and sentenced to four years of hard labor. Georgia had passed laws extending its authority over Cherokee lands. Worcester brought a writ of error to the Supreme Court, arguing that the Georgia statute was unconstitutional and void because it conflicted with treaties with the Cherokees, including the Treaties of Hopewell (1785) and Holston (1791).

Chief Justice Marshall's opinion reversed the conviction. It held that the doctrine of discovery regulated claims among European nations, but "could not affect the rights of those already in possession." The Court said: "The Cherokee nation, then, is a distinct community, occupying its own territory, with boundaries accurately described, in which the laws of Georgia can have no force." Relations with the Nation belonged to the national government by the Constitution and laws.

Worcester is the foundation of the principle that states have no power to govern Indian country. Georgia did not comply, and the Cherokee Nation was removed from its homeland under the 1835 Treaty of New Echota, which some Cherokees disputed. The case's reasoning remains central to tribal sovereignty doctrine.
