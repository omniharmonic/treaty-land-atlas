In 1968 Congress recognized the Ysleta del Sur Pueblo as an Indian tribe and assigned its trust responsibilities to Texas. In 1983 Texas renounced those responsibilities as inconsistent with the State's Constitution, and said it opposed any new federal trust law that did not let it apply its own gaming laws on tribal lands.

Congress restored the Tribe's federal trust status in 1987 with the Ysleta del Sur and Alabama and Coushatta Indian Tribes of Texas Restoration Act. Section 107 says that "gaming activities which are prohibited by [Texas law] are hereby prohibited on the reservation and on lands of the tribe," but denies Texas "civil or criminal regulatory jurisdiction." Soon after, Congress passed the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act (IGRA), which lets tribes offer bingo, a class II game, in states that permit such gaming for any purpose by anyone.

In 2016 the Tribe began offering bingo, including electronic bingo machines. Texas sued to shut down all of its bingo operations. The district court and the Fifth Circuit sided with Texas, holding that the bingo operations were impermissible because they did not follow Texas's bingo regulations.

On June 15, 2022, the Supreme Court vacated that ruling, 5-4. Justice Gorsuch wrote that "The Restoration Act bans as a matter of federal law on tribal lands only those gaming activities also banned in Texas." Texas admits it allows bingo "according to rule[s]," which makes its laws regulatory rather than prohibitory. Chief Justice Roberts dissented, joined by Justices Thomas, Alito and Kavanaugh.
