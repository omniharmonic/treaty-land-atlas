The Act of March 3, 1871 changed how the United States dealt with tribes. It ended new treaty-making with tribes while protecting existing treaties, as the treaty-making record explains.

The Supreme Court, in Antoine v. Washington (1975), explained the background. It said the 1871 Act "resulted from the opposition of the House of Representatives to its practical exclusion from any policy role in Indian affairs." For nearly a century the Executive Branch had made treaties with advice and consent of the Senate, while the House only paid for them.

After 1871 the government kept making deals, now called agreements, and Congress ratified them by statute. Antoine involved an 1891 agreement with the Colville Tribe that Congress ratified. The Court held that such ratifying statutes are "Laws of the United States" made in pursuance of the Constitution, and that the 1871 Act did not affect "Congress' power to legislate on the problems of Indians, including legislation ratifying contracts" with tribes.

The Court also applied a canon of construction: the wording of treaties and statutes ratifying agreements with Indians is not to be construed to their prejudice.
