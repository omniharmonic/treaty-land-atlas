Assimilation was a goal written into federal law. The Civilization Fund Act of 1819, as quoted in a 2022 Interior Department report, aimed at "introducing among them the habits and arts of civilization." The Dawes Act of 1887 tied allotment to citizenship: an Indian who took up residence apart from a tribe and "adopted the habits of civilized life" was declared a citizen of the United States.

The Supreme Court has described the allotment program in these terms. In Hodel v. Irving (1987), it said the legislation was animated in part by a desire to force Indians to abandon their nomadic ways "to speed the Indians' assimilation into American society."

Boarding schools were another tool. In 2022 the Department of the Interior's Federal Indian Boarding School Initiative released an investigative report. Its introductory letter says that, for the first time, the U.S. Government is accounting for its role in operating Indian boarding schools "to forcibly assimilate Indian children." The report identifies 417 institutions across 37 states or then-territories, and confirms that at least 973 American Indian, Alaska Native and Native Hawaiian children died while attending them.

These are the Department's findings about its own predecessors' policy, as published. Native nations' own accounts of boarding schools are theirs to tell and are not summarized here.
