Federal courts use special rules to read treaties and statutes involving Native nations. They are called canons of construction.

In Winters v. United States (1908), the Supreme Court wrote: "By a rule of interpretation of agreements and treaties with the Indians, ambiguities occurring will be resolved from the standpoint of the Indians." In United States v. Winans (1905), the Court reasoned that the treaty "was not a grant of rights to the Indians, but a grant of right from them,—a reservation of those not granted."

Minnesota v. Mille Lacs Band (1999) restated the rules: "Indian treaties are to be interpreted liberally in favor of the Indians," and the Court interprets them "to give effect to the terms as the Indians themselves would have understood them." The Court there held that the Chippewa kept the hunting, fishing and gathering rights of an 1837 treaty, despite an 1850 executive order, an 1855 treaty and Minnesota statehood.

The canons also apply to laws ratifying agreements. In Antoine v. Washington (1975), the Court said that the wording of treaties and statutes ratifying agreements with the Indians is not to be construed to their prejudice. In Bryan v. Itasca County (1976), it described the canons as applicable to statutes claimed to terminate Indian immunities.
