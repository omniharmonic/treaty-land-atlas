Disestablishment is the complete end of a reservation, where diminishment only shrinks its boundaries. The Supreme Court has said that only Congress can do either one, and that doing so requires a clear expression of congressional intent. The Court has also said it will not lightly infer such a breach once Congress has established a reservation.

Some cases do find disestablishment. In DeCoteau v. District County Court (1975), the Court held the Lake Traverse Reservation in South Dakota, created by an 1867 treaty, "terminated and returned to the public domain by an 1891 Act" that ratified a previously negotiated 1889 agreement with the tribe. The syllabus says the Act opened all unallotted lands to settlement and also appropriated money.

More recently the Court went the other way for the Creek Nation. In McGirt v. Oklahoma (2020), the Court wrote that Oklahoma had pointed to no language in the relevant statutes that could plausibly be read as an act of cession, and that the Creek Reservation "persists today." It also said Congress "has since broken more than a few promises to the Tribe" yet that did not end the reservation.

The Court's reasoning in McGirt includes this passage: "wishes don't make for laws." Legislators may wish an inconvenient reservation would disappear, but Congress must actually disestablish it.
