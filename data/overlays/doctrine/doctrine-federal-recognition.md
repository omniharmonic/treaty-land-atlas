Federal recognition is the United States' acknowledgment that a tribe exists as a government with which it has a relationship. In the Federally Recognized Indian Tribe List Act of 1994, Congress found that the United States "has a trust responsibility to recognized Indian tribes, maintains a government-to-government relationship with those tribes, and recognizes the sovereignty of those tribes."

The same findings list the ways a tribe may be recognized: "by Act of Congress; by the administrative procedures set forth in part 83 of the Code of Federal Regulations ... or by a decision of a United States court." They add that a recognized tribe "may not be terminated except by an Act of Congress," and that Congress "has expressly repudiated the policy of terminating recognized Indian tribes."

The Secretary of the Interior must publish in the Federal Register a list of all Indian tribes the Secretary recognizes as eligible for the special programs and services provided by the United States to Indians because of their status as Indians. The Act defines an Indian tribe as one the Secretary of the Interior acknowledges to exist as an Indian tribe.

Congress has used its power directly. The Menominee Restoration Act of 1973 extended recognition to the Menominee, and a 2025 law extended it to the Lumbee Tribe of North Carolina.
