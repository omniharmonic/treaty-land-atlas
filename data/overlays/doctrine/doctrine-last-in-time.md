The Constitution makes treaties "the supreme law of the land" but does not say what happens when a treaty and a statute conflict. The Supreme Court answered in The Cherokee Tobacco (December Term 1870), a case about a federal tax on tobacco in the face of a prior treaty that exempted the Cherokee Nation's tobacco. The Court wrote: "A treaty may supersede a prior act of Congress, and an act of Congress may supersede a prior treaty."

The Court then applied that rule to Native nations. The Court wrote of treaties with Indian nations that "They have no higher sanctity." In that case "the act of Congress must prevail as if the treaty were not an element to be considered." The Court said that if a wrong had been done, "the power of redress is with Congress, not with the judiciary."

The rule was restated for foreign treaties in Whitney v. Robertson (1888): if the two are inconsistent, "the one last in date will control the other," provided the treaty stipulation is self-executing. In Lone Wolf v. Hitchcock (1903), the Court allowed Congress to act on tribal land despite a treaty's requirement of consent by three fourths of adult male Indians.

Modern courts add a clear-statement requirement: United States v. Dion (1986) requires clear evidence that Congress considered the conflict and chose to abrogate the treaty.
