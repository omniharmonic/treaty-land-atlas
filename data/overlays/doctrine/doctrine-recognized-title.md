Recognized title is Indian title that Congress has acknowledged in a treaty, agreement or statute. It is contrasted with aboriginal title, which has no such recognition. The difference matters when the United States takes the land.

The Supreme Court drew the line in Tee-Hit-Ton Indians v. United States (1955): "Where the Congress by treaty or other agreement has declared that thereafter Indians were to hold the lands permanently, compensation must be paid for subsequent taking." For land held only by occupancy, the Court held there was no such right.

The Sioux Nation case shows the rule at work. In the Fort Laramie Treaty of 1868, the United States pledged that the Great Sioux Reservation, including the Black Hills, would be "set apart for the absolute and undisturbed use and occupation" of the Sioux. In 1980 the Court held that an 1877 Act implementing a later agreement "effected a taking of tribal property which had been set aside by the Fort Laramie Treaty," which "implied an obligation on the Government's part to make just compensation to the Sioux."

Compensation in these cases is money. The Court noted a principal sum of $17.1 million, with interest, as the fair market value of the Black Hills as of 1877.
