The Library of Congress says that "By the mid-nineteenth century the policy of removal to a single large Indian territory was replaced by a policy of isolating Indians on reservations." The Bureau of Indian Affairs dates the "reservation system" from 1850 to 1891. In its words: "Between 1850 and 1891, numerous treaties and other written agreements were made that required tribes to relocate to distant territories or confined them to small areas that were 'reserved' portions of the tribes' aboriginal territories. These reservations were created by treaties, statutes, and executive orders."

Reservations varied. The 1851 Fort Laramie Treaty, as the National Park Service describes it, recognized tribal territory on the Plains and offered annuities and protection in exchange for safe passage of American citizens. By contrast, in Winters v. United States (1908), the Court described an 1888 reservation, a tract set apart "as an Indian reservation as and for a permanent home and abiding place" of the Gros Ventre and Assiniboine bands in Montana.

Reservations carry legal weight. In Solem v. Bartlett (1984), the Court said "Only Congress can divest an Indian reservation of its land and diminish its boundaries." The BIA dates the allotment era from 1887, which overlaps the end of the reservation-system period it describes.
