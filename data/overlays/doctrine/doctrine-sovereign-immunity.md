Sovereign immunity means a government cannot be sued without its consent. The Supreme Court has applied that idea to Native nations for decades, and has said Congress, not the courts, should decide whether to narrow it.

In Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez (1978), a woman and her daughter sued the Pueblo over a membership rule, citing the Indian Civil Rights Act. The Court held that suits against the tribe under that law were barred by the tribe's sovereign immunity, because nothing in the Act subjected tribes to such lawsuits in federal court.

Kiowa Tribe v. Manufacturing Technologies (1998) extended the rule to contracts. The Court wrote that tribes are immune from suits on contracts, whether governmental or commercial, and whether made on or off a reservation. It left any change to Congress.

In Michigan v. Bay Mills Indian Community (decided May 27, 2014), Michigan tried to stop a casino the Community opened on land it had bought, which Michigan said was outside Indian lands. The Court held the suit was barred by tribal sovereign immunity. It declined to overrule Kiowa. It also pointed to other tools a state has, such as suing the responsible tribal officials rather than the tribe, or bargaining for a waiver in a gaming compact. The rule stands today as stated in those decisions: immunity applies unless Congress has clearly authorized the suit or the tribe has waived it.
