The trust doctrine is the idea that the United States owes special duties to Native nations. Its legal force has changed a great deal over time.

In Cherokee Nation v. Georgia (January Term, 1831), the Cherokee Nation asked the Supreme Court for an injunction to restrain the State of Georgia. Chief Justice Marshall described tribes within the United States as domestic dependent nations and wrote that their relation to the United States resembles that of a ward to his guardian. That passage is the source of much later talk of a federal guardianship.

In United States v. Mitchell (Mitchell II, decided June 27, 1983), allottees on the Quinault Reservation sued over mismanagement of timber on their allotted lands. The Court held the United States accountable in money damages. It reasoned that federal timber statutes and regulations gave the government such elaborate control over Indian forests that a fiduciary relationship necessarily arose.

United States v. Jicarilla Apache Nation (decided June 13, 2011) set limits. The Court held that a common-law exception to attorney-client privilege for trustees does not apply to the general trust relationship. It said the Government's trust obligations are established and governed by statute, not the common law, and that the applicable statutes and regulations control. Together these cases show a trust relationship that shapes how statutes are read, but a tribe seeking damages must point to a specific trust-creating statute or regulation.
