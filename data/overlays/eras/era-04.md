Allotment was the federal policy of breaking up land that tribes held in common into individual parcels. The General Allotment Act, passed February 8, 1887, authorized the President to survey reservations and allot the land in severalty. A head of a family was to receive one-quarter of a section, and single adults one-eighth. Patents would say the United States would hold the land in trust for twenty-five years.

The Encyclopedia of the Great Plains, from the University of Nebraska, explains the larger design. Land left over after allotment was deemed "surplus" and could be sold to non-Indians. It says the act was designed to detribalize Indians and assimilate them, and that by the time the process was stopped in 1934, Indian-held land had dropped from 138 million acres to 48 million acres. The encyclopedia gives this as a general figure, not a table.

The Supreme Court made it harder to resist. In Lone Wolf v. Hitchcock (decided January 5, 1903), the 1867 Medicine Lodge treaty with the Kiowa and Comanche required the signatures of three fourths of adult men for any cession. The Court held that Congress has always exercised plenary authority over tribal relations and that the three-fourths clause did not limit it.

The encyclopedia names one example: the Devils Lake Sioux Reservation in North Dakota was allotted in 1904, over the objections of many residents, leaving 92,000 surplus acres to be sold to non-Indians.
