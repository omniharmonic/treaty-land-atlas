The 1868 treaty with the Ute defined a large reservation. By 1872 the United States wanted part of it back, and Congress authorized negotiations to extinguish the Ute right to a portion. The Colorado Encyclopedia says miners had entered the San Juan Mountains despite the treaty. The first negotiation failed, as the agreement's preamble puts it, "owing to the refusal of said Indians to relinquish their right to any portion of said reservation."

A new commission was named in June 1873. On September 13, 1873, at the Los Pinos agency, commissioner Felix R. Brunot and the chiefs, headmen and men of seven Ute bands signed an agreement. In Article I the Ute relinquished a described strip of the reservation, but kept Uncompahgre Park. In Article II the United States agreed that the Ute could "hunt upon said lands so long as the game lasts and the Indians are at peace with the white people."

Article III promised a perpetual trust fund producing $25,000 a year for the Ute "annually forever." Article VI gave Ouray, head chief, a $1,000 salary for ten years. Congress ratified and confirmed the agreement by an Act approved April 29, 1874 (18 Stat. 36). The Colorado Encyclopedia puts the land taken at 3.7 million acres and says it led to mining in the San Juan Mountains. It also says the surveyor failed to exclude Uncompahgre Park, which was quickly settled, and that the Utes were later force-marched to Utah in 1881.
