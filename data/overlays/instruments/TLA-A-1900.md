On October 21, 1892, at Fort Sill, the Jerome Commission (David H. Jerome, Alfred M. Wilson and Warren G. Sayre) concluded an agreement with the Comanche, Kiowa and Apache. Subject to allotments and 480,000 acres of common grazing land, the tribes ceded their reservation, the one set by the 1867 Medicine Lodge treaty. Each person over eighteen could select 160 acres, and parents could select the same for their children.

The Medicine Lodge treaty required signatures of three-fourths of adult men for any cession. The Supreme Court Historical Society says tribal leaders were determined "not to sell the country," that many said the commissioners lied, and that Jerome told them "Congress has full control of you." It states the commissioners later changed the agreement without input from tribal councils, and the final document contained mostly counterfeit signatures. Kansapedia says the opposition leader A'piatan accused the commission of fraud.

Congress ratified a modified version on June 6, 1900. In Lone Wolf v. Hitchcock (1903), the Court held Congress could abrogate treaty provisions and that courts could not inquire into its motives.
