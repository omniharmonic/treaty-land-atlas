This is a colonial record. It dates to 1722, decades before the United States existed. The National Archives set of ratified Indian treaties begins here because, in its own words, United States treaty-making followed a custom established by Great Britain and the colonial governments.

The record is a bundle of speeches given at Albany, New York, from late August to mid-September 1722. William Burnet, governor of New York, met the Five Nations (the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga and Seneca) to renew what both sides called the Covenant Chain, their long-standing alliance. Burnet asked them to stay away from the French and keep trade paths open. The governors of Virginia and Pennsylvania came to Albany as well, at the Nations' wish, to settle matters of their own.

The Virginia governor, Alexander Spotswood, proposed that the Potomac River and the high ridge of mountains along Virginia's frontier be the permanent boundary between Indians under Virginia and Indians of the Five Nations. People crossing it would need a passport from the governor of Virginia or New York. A note added to the record says the Five Nations, with the Tuscarora, fully agreed to the propositions on peace and boundaries on 6 September 1722, and signed them.

The text is a set of minutes, not a modern treaty. It does not record land sales. Its pages do not say what happened to the boundary afterward.
