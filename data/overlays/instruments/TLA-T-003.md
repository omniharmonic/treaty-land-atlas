This is a colonial record from 1744, long before the United States existed. The National Archives series includes it because United States treaty-making, in the Archives' words, followed a custom set by Great Britain and the colonial governments.

The council opened on 22 June 1744 in the court house at Lancaster, Pennsylvania. Pennsylvania's lieutenant governor, George Thomas, hosted commissioners from Virginia and Maryland and deputies of the Six Nations (Onondaga, Seneca, Cayuga, Oneida and Tuscarora, as listed). The Six Nations' speaker, Canassatego, said they had conquered the earlier people of the Susquehanna country, so its land now belonged to them. He also said that the lands now in question had never been sold.

Maryland's commissioners said they could not admit the Six Nations' right, but offered 300 pounds in currency, partly in goods, if the Six Nations gave a written release of all claim to Maryland lands. A deed for Maryland's proprietor, Lord Baltimore, was signed by Canassatego and thirteen other leaders, and the rest signed it on 2 July.

Virginia's commissioners offered goods worth 200 pounds and 200 pounds in gold in return for a deed recognizing the King's right to Virginia lands, plus an agreed road. The Six Nations agreed, and asked that their case be put to the King as settlement spread. At Logstown in 1752, Ohio Indian leaders said they had understood the deed's reach differently.
