In autumn 1768 Sir William Johnson, the Crown's superintendent of Indian affairs, met the Six Nations, along with Shawnee, Delaware and Ohio Seneca representatives, at Fort Stanwix in New York. Commissioners from Virginia, Pennsylvania and New Jersey attended. The record of the proceedings describes a treaty "for the settlement of a Boundary Line between the Colonies and Indians" under the King's orders.

The Proclamation of 1763 had set a line meant to be temporary until treaties could fix a boundary the nations agreed to, according to Encyclopedia Virginia. The National Park Service notes that land speculators and settlers already had interests beyond that line. The proceedings show Johnson arriving with boats of goods "intended for the Present to be made by the Cession of Lands," and Pennsylvania traders also pressed for land to cover their 1763 losses.

Encyclopedia Virginia reports that the Haudenosaunee ceded more than 27 million acres in the Ohio Valley, land it says they claimed but did not occupy. Other nations that hunted there were not compensated and refused to accept the line. Conflict followed, including Dunmore's War in 1774, and the line stayed contested until the Treaty of Greenville in 1795.
