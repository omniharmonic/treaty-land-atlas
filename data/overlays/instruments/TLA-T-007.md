In autumn 1768 Sir William Johnson, the Crown's superintendent of Indian affairs, held a treaty at Fort Stanwix, New York. It was with the Six Nations, the Shawnee, Delawares and Senecas of Ohio, and other "dependant Tribes," "for the settlement of a Boundary Line between the Colonies and Indians, pursuant to His Majesty's orders." Johnson arrived with twenty boats of goods "intended for the Present to be made by the Cession of Lands to the King." Commissioners from Virginia, Pennsylvania and New Jersey attended. Negotiations began October 24, and Encyclopedia Virginia says 3,102 Indigenous people were present, mostly Haudenosaunee.

The Proclamation Line of 1763 was meant, Encyclopedia Virginia says, to be "a temporary boundary until a series of formal treaties" could set a consensual one. At Fort Stanwix the Haudenosaunee outlined a line from Owego, New York, through Pennsylvania to the Ohio River and on to the headwaters of the Tennessee River. The principals signed or marked the treaty on November 5, 1768. In the deed the Indians granted the King the tract bounded by the line, which began at the mouth of the Cherokee (Hogohege) River on the Ohio.

The Haudenosaunee received cash and goods valued at £10,460 from Great Britain, plus 10,000 Spanish dollars from Pennsylvania. The Ohio Valley Lenape and Shawnee, who stood to lose the most, received about $5,500 worth of goods and objected. Encyclopedia Virginia says that periodic attacks, settler retaliation and Dunmore's 1774 campaign followed, and that the dispute over the line ended only after a military defeat in 1794 and the 1795 Treaty of Greenville.
