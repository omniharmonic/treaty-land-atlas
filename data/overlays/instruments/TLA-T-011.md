The Treaty of Hopewell was concluded on November 28, 1785, at Hopewell, on the Keowee River, between Benjamin Hawkins, Andrew Pickens, Joseph Martin and Lachlan McIntosh for the United States and "the Head-Men and Warriors of all the Cherokees." The commissioners "give peace to all the Cherokees, and receive them into the favor and protection of the United States."

The Mississippi Encyclopedia says the commissioners sought to impose a federal Indian policy on the postrevolutionary South and to nullify Spanish influence among southern nations. It says a Cherokee delegation of 918 men and women attended, that discussion centered on boundary disputes, and that "despite the protestations of some Cherokee leaders," the commissioners and 37 Cherokee chiefs and warriors signed.

Article III has the Cherokees acknowledge they are "under the protection of the United States of America, and of no other sovereign whosoever." Article IV set "the boundary allotted to the Cherokees for their hunting grounds." Under Article V a citizen who settled on land beyond it, or stayed after six months, forfeited U.S. protection, and "the Indians may punish him or not as they please." Article XII gave the Cherokee the right to send a deputy of their choice to Congress.

The Tennessee Encyclopedia says the treaty officially ended fighting between the United States and the Cherokees. It adds, "Settlers moved into that land, however," and the more definitive Treaty of Holston followed on July 2, 1791.
