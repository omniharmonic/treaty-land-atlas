The Treaty of Hopewell was signed on November 28, 1785, on the Keowee River in what is now South Carolina. Four U.S. commissioners made it with Cherokee leaders. The Cherokee acknowledged they were "under the protection of the United States of America, and of no other sovereign whosoever." Article IV drew a boundary for the Cherokee "hunting grounds," and Article V said U.S. citizens who settled beyond it forfeited U.S. protection. Article XII allowed the Cherokee to send a deputy to Congress.

The Mississippi Encyclopedia says the commissioners aimed to impose a federal Indian policy on the postwar South and to nullify Spanish influence among southern nations. It reports that discussion centered on boundary disputes and that the commissioners and 37 Cherokee chiefs and warriors signed "despite the protestations of some Cherokee leaders."

The Tennessee Encyclopedia says settlers moved into the land anyway, which led to the more definitive Treaty of Holston in 1791. The Hopewell boundary and the later disputes with Georgia and the Cherokee Nation cases in this atlas are linked from the violation and case records.
