On January 3, 1786, U.S. commissioners made a treaty at Hopewell, on the Keowee River, with Choctaw chiefs who acted for the nation. The Choctaw acknowledged themselves to be under U.S. protection "and of no other sovereign whosoever." Article III set a boundary for the lands "allotted to the Choctaw nation to live and hunt on," starting at the 31st parallel, and reserved three tracts of six miles square for trading posts "to the use and under the government of the United States."

The Mississippi Encyclopedia says the commissioners wanted to impose a federal Indian policy and nullify Spanish influence in the South. It describes a Choctaw party that arrived worn down by an exhausting two-and-a-half-month journey and that asked for food and new clothing before talks began.

Article IV said settlers on those lands forfeited U.S. protection. In 1801 the Treaty of Fort Adams, also in this atlas, opened by recalling that the United States had given the Choctaw peace and protection at Hopewell, and later treaties took Choctaw land.
