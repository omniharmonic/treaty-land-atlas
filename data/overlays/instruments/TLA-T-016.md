On 9 January 1789, at Fort Harmar on the Muskingum River, Arthur St. Clair, governor of the Northwest Territory, met the sachems and warriors of the Six Nations. He acted as commissioner plenipotentiary for the United States. The treaty says its purpose was to remove causes of controversy, regulate trade and settle boundaries.

The Six Nations, except the Mohawks, who did not attend, agreed to renew and confirm the engagements made at Fort Stanwix in October 1784. That included a boundary line running from a creek near Niagara, past Buffalo Creek, to the Pennsylvania border and down to the Ohio River. The treaty says the line would be the western boundary of Six Nations lands and that the Six Nations yield to the United States all claim to the country west of it.

For this, they received peace, the presents they had received before, and goods worth three thousand dollars delivered by St. Clair. The United States confirmed to the Six Nations the lands they lived on east and north of the line, keeping only six miles square around the fort at Oswego. The Oneida and Tuscarora were again secured in their lands.

The treaty also renewed peace and friendship, and gave the Mohawks six months to declare their assent. The text lists sachems' names and marks. It does not say what became of the Mohawk offer.
