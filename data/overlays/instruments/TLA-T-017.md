On August 7, 1790, Secretary of War Henry Knox signed a "Treaty of Peace and Friendship" in New York for the United States, "by and with the advice and consent of the Senate," with the "Kings, Chiefs and Warriors of the Creek Nation." President Washington's government proclaimed it on August 13, 1790 (7 Stat. 35).

Article I promised "perpetual peace and friendship." In Article II the Creek signers acknowledged the Creek Nation to be "under the protection of the United States of America, and of no other sovereign whosoever," and agreed not to treat with any individual state. Article IV set a boundary between U.S. citizens and the Creek Nation along the Savannah, Keowee, Oconee and Altamaha rivers. Article V had the United States "solemnly guarantee" the Creek lands to the west and south of that line. Settlers on Creek lands would forfeit U.S. protection.

The Encyclopedia of Alabama says the treaty "asserted U.S. authority to treat with the Creek Nation, ending efforts by the state of Georgia to act unilaterally." Knox drafted it for the United States, and Alexander McGillivray represented a select group of chiefs. The Creeks also ceded the land of the allied Oconee, which had been contentious, and received an annual payment.

Six secret articles, kept from most Creek leaders, included a $100 yearly stipend for select chiefs and a brigadier general's commission for McGillivray with $1,200 annual pay. They were not made public until 1848.
