On August 7, 1790, Secretary of War Henry Knox signed a treaty in New York with Alexander McGillivray and other Creek leaders. It promised "perpetual peace and friendship," put the Creek under U.S. protection "and of no other sovereign whosoever," and set a boundary between Georgia and the Creek Nation. Article IV described the line from the Savannah and Keowee rivers to the Oconee and Altamaha.

The Encyclopedia of Alabama says the treaty asserted U.S. authority to deal with the Creek and ended Georgia's effort to treat separately with tribes. Knox drafted it for the United States and McGillivray spoke for a select group of chiefs. The Creek also ceded land of the allied Oconee, which had been a contentious issue, and received an annual payment. Six secret articles, kept from most Creek leaders, included a $100 yearly stipend for select chiefs and a commission for McGillivray.

The encyclopedia says the secret articles drew acrimonious debate and were not made public until 1848. Later treaties in this atlas, including Colerain in 1796 and Fort Jackson in 1814, refer back to the New York treaty.
