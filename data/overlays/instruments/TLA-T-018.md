The Tennessee Encyclopedia says the 1785 Treaty of Hopewell did not stop settlers moving onto the Cherokee land. On July 2, 1791, William Blount, governor of the territory south of the Ohio and superintendent of Indian affairs for the southern district, signed a "Treaty of Peace and Friendship" at the Holston River with "the undersigned Chiefs and Warriors of the Cherokee Nation." The treaty was proclaimed February 7, 1792 (7 Stat. 39).

In Article II the Cherokee acknowledged they were under U.S. protection "and of no other sovereign whosoever," and agreed not to treat with any foreign power or state. Article IV set a new boundary and, "in order to extinguish forever all claims" to the land to its right, the United States agreed to deliver valuable goods and to pay $1,000 a year. The Cherokee signers "release, quit-claim, relinquish and cede, all the land to the right of the line." The Tennessee Encyclopedia says the annuity was later raised to $1,500, and that the cession covered claims east of the Clinch River and north of a line through Kingston.

Article V gave U.S. citizens free use of a road from Washington district to Mero district and of the Tennessee River. Article VI gave the United States "the sole and exclusive right of regulating their trade." In Article VII the United States "solemnly guarantee to the Cherokee nation, all their lands not hereby ceded."

The Tennessee Encyclopedia says further cessions followed in the First Treaty of Tellico, signed October 2, 1798, which granted land that settlers had entered.
