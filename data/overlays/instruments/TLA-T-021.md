On November 11, 1794, at Canandaigua, New York, Timothy Pickering signed a treaty with the chiefs of the Six Nations. He acted as President Washington's agent. The Haudenosaunee side was large: Ganondagan, a New York State historic site, says the nations sent 1,600 representatives, including 800 Seneca, and that Quaker observers came at the Seneca's invitation.

The treaty named the lands the United States recognized as belonging to the Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga and Seneca. The United States promised it would "never claim the same, nor disturb them." Seneca land would stay theirs "until they choose to sell the same to the people of the United States." In return, the Six Nations were bound by the treaty never to claim other lands within the United States, and the Seneca ceded the right to build a wagon road from Fort Schlosser to Lake Erie. The United States delivered $10,000 in goods and set yearly payments at $4,500, to be spent on clothing, animals, tools and craftsmen.

Ganondagan says the treaty restored to the Six Nations lands in western New York that had been ceded under the Fort Stanwix treaty. It also says there have been violations, yet the treaty "has never been broken" and is still recognized by the Six Nations and the United States.
