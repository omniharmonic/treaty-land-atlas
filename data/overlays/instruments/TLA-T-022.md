On December 2, 1794, Timothy Pickering signed a treaty at Oneida, New York, for the United States. The other parties were chiefs of the Oneida, Tuscarora and Stockbridge nations. The treaty's opening says these nations "adhered faithfully to the United States, and assisted them with their warriors" in the Revolutionary War. It says the Oneidas and Tuscaroras were driven from their homes, and that their houses were burnt and their property destroyed. The United States had promised to reward them, and it said it was now able to keep that promise.

The treaty set out what the reward would be. The United States agreed to pay $5,000. It would build a grist mill and a saw mill, provide millers for three years and train young men of the three nations in milling and sawing. It would also pay $1,000 toward a new church at Oneida to replace the one burned in the war.

In return the three nations "relinquish all other claims of compensation and rewards for their losses and services in the late war." This treaty is about compensation, not land. The Treaty of Canandaigua, signed with the Six Nations three weeks earlier, dealt with land. Later disputes over Oneida land are covered in the atlas's linked records, not in this treaty.
