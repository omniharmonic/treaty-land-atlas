On May 31, 1796, deputies of the Seven Nations of Canada met in New York City with agents of New York State. Abraham Ogden attended as a commissioner "appointed under the authority of the United States, to hold the treaty." This was a sale to a state, not to the federal government. The agents for New York were Egbert Benson, Richard Varick and James Watson. William Constable and Daniel McCormick, purchasers of land under Alexander Macomb, also signed.

The deputies agreed to "cede, release and quit claim to the people of the state of New-York, forever, all the claim, right, or title" the Seven Nations had to lands within the state. The state proposed payment for "the extinguishment of their claim to all lands within the state."

The nations kept land. A tract "equal to six miles square" at St. Regis stayed reserved for the village. The deputies said St. Regis people had built mills on the Salmon River and the Grass River and needed the Grass River meadows for hay. The treaty therefore also reserved a mile square at each mill and the meadows on both sides of the Grass River.

New York agreed to pay 1,233 pounds, 6 shillings and 8 pence, plus 213 pounds, 6 shillings and 8 pence, on the first payment date. After that it would pay 213 pounds, 6 shillings and 8 pence every year, forever.
