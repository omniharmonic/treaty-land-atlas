On 1 June 1798, at the Oneida village in New York, the Oneida nation agreed to give up a large part of its reservation to the State of New York. A United States commissioner, Joseph Hopkinson, was present. New York sent three agents: Egbert Benson, Ezra L'Hommedieu and John Taylor.

The Oneida had proposed the sale to New York's governor in March, the text says. The governor accepted and advanced them 300 dollars. At the village meeting the nation ceded, released and quit-claimed to the people of New York all its reservation lands west and southwest of a described line near Oneida Lake. It also ceded strips a mile wide along the main Genesee road.

Seven named Oneida families could keep the ground they cultivated, up to fifty acres each, while they lived there. In return for this exception, the Oneida ceded a 1,280-acre tract set aside for those families for when they moved.

New York paid another 200 dollars. It also promised 700 dollars a year, payable at the village each 1 June. President John Adams sent the treaty to the Senate on 30 January 1799, and the page lists it as communicated on 31 January 1799. The transcript does not show a Senate vote or proclamation. It is a treaty between a Native nation and a state, held under United States supervision.
