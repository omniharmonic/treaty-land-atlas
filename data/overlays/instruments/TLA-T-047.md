On August 21, 1805, William Henry Harrison, governor of Indiana Territory, held a treaty at Grouseland, near Vincennes, with the Delaware, Potawatomi, Miami, Eel River and Wea. It followed a dispute that began a year earlier. In the Delaware treaty of August 18, 1804, the United States had promised to treat the Delaware as owners of the land between the White River, the Ohio and Clark's grant.

Article I says the Miami, from whom the Delaware derived their claim, "contend" they never meant to give the Delaware the right to the soil, only to let them occupy it. So, "for the sake of peace and good neighborhood," the Delaware were recorded as giving up their claim and released the United States from its guarantee.

In Article II the Miami, Eel River and Wea were recorded as ceding "all that tract of country" south of a line drawn from the northeast corner of the Fort Wayne tract. The United States added permanent annuities of $600 for the Miami and $250 each for the Eel River and Wea. It gave the Potawatomi $500 a year for ten years, and delivered $4,000 at the signing.

Article IV said the Miami, Eel River and Wea considered themselves one nation. The United States promised to treat them as joint owners of the Wabash country above the Vincennes tract and not to buy any of it without each tribe's consent. Article V had the other tribes acknowledge the Delaware right to sell the 1804 tract.
