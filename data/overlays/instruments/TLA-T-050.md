On November 14, 1805, Secretary of War Henry Dearborn signed a convention in Washington with Creek chiefs and head men, including Oche Haujo and William McIntosh, "duly authorized and empowered by said nation."

In Article I the chiefs agreed, "in consideration of certain sums of money and goods," to cede "all right, title, and interest" in a tract between the Oconee and Ocmulgee rivers. The tract runs from the high shoals of the Apalachee, where the Fort Wilkinson line touches it, to the mouth of the Ulcofauhatche, then down the Ocmulgee to the Oconee. The Creek kept a tract five miles long and three wide at the old Ocmulgee fields. The President could place a military post and trading house on it, and white people got free use of the Ocmulgee for navigation and net fishing between the Oconee junction and the Ulcofauhatche.

Article II gave the United States the right to a horse path through the Creek country from the Ocmulgee to the Mobile. The Creek chiefs would keep boats at the rivers and houses of entertainment for travelers.

The United States agreed to pay $12,000 a year for eight years, then $11,000 a year for ten more, eighteen payments in all, and to supply two blacksmiths and two strikers for eight years. The Charlton County Historical Society says that in 1796 at Colerain the Creek "positively refused to cede" this territory to Georgia. The sources here do not explain what changed.
