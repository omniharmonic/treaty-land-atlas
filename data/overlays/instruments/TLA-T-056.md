On November 25, 1808, William Hull, governor of Michigan Territory, signed a treaty at Brownstown with the sachems, chiefs and warriors of the Chippewa, Ottawa, Potawatomi, Wyandot and Shawnee nations.

The preamble gives the United States' reason. The 1807 Treaty of Detroit had ceded land on the west and north side of the Maumee River. But the land on the southeast side, between the river and the Greenville and Fort Industry lines, still belonged to the nations. So the United States "cannot, of right, open and maintain a convenient road from the settlements in the state of Ohio" to the settlements in Michigan, or connect them.

In Article II the nations "give, grant, and cede" a road 120 feet wide from the foot of the Maumee rapids to the western line of the Connecticut Reserve, along with "all the land within one mile of the said road, on each side thereof, for the purpose of establishing settlements." They were also recorded as ceding a second 120-foot road running south from Lower Sandusky to the Greenville line. The United States could take timber and other materials from adjacent lands to build and repair the roads.

The nations kept the right to hunt and fish on these lands while the United States owned them (Article IV), and again acknowledged U.S. protection (Article V). The text says the road was meant to benefit the nations "as well as" the United States. No payment for the cession appears in the text, which the OSU record shows was ratified March 1, 1809.
