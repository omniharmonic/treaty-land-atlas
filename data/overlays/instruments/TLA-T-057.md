On September 30, 1809, William Henry Harrison, governor of Indiana Territory, signed a treaty at Fort Wayne with leaders of the Delaware, Potawatomi, Miami and Eel River Miami. In Article 1 the Miami and Eel River, "and the Delawares and Putawatimies, as their allies," agreed to cede two tracts. The first lay between the earlier Fort Wayne boundary, the Wabash River and a line from Raccoon Creek, and was to be thirty miles wide at its narrowest. The second began at Fort Recovery and ran south along the Greenville line.

For the cession the United States agreed to annuities of $500 each for the Delaware, Miami and Potawatomi and $250 for the Eel River. The text says those annuities and $5,200 in goods delivered at signing were "full compensation." The tribes kept the Greenville treaty's hunting rights on the land. Article 5 said the consent of the Wea was needed to complete the title to the first tract, and Article 9 offered a cession for the Kickapoo if they agreed.

The National Library of Medicine's Native Voices timeline says Harrison negotiated the treaty to acquire 3 million acres. It says the Shawnee leader Tecumseh told Harrison "that the signers have no authority to sell the land," questioned whether they could sell land held in common with other peoples, and warned Americans not to settle there while organizing fighters.

The Wea and Kickapoo gave their consent in separate October and December 1809 treaties, covered in other atlas records.
