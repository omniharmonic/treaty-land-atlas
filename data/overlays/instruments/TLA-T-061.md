On August 9, 1814, Major General Andrew Jackson signed "articles of agreement and capitulation" at Fort Jackson, Alabama, with the chiefs, deputies and warriors of the Creek nation. The text blames an "unprovoked, inhuman, and sanguinary war" waged by "the hostile Creeks" and says the United States "demand an equivalent for all expenses incurred" by taking Creek territory west, south and southeast of a line beginning on the Coosa River.

The National Park Service says Jackson was the sole U.S. representative and "dictated the terms." It adds that, although styled a capitulation, the treaty was made with the Creek National Council, an American ally, and the proceedings represented 28 Creek towns. When Jackson's Creek allies pointed out that only a faction had attacked Americans, he replied that they were still responsible. The Park Service puts the cession at nearly 22 million acres. The Encyclopedia of Alabama says more than 21 million and that Jackson imposed "much harsher terms than any of the parties envisioned."

Article 1 let chiefs and warriors friendly to the United States apply for reserves of one square mile. The treaty also barred contact with British and Spanish posts, gave the United States military posts, trading houses and roads, and demanded the surrender of the prophets. The Park Service says the Creek protested that some ceded land belonged to towns that stayed friendly; in 1962 the Indian Claims Commission authorized nearly $4 million for a disputed tract of nearly nine million acres.

The Encyclopedia of Alabama says the cession later led to the removal of most of the Creek nation to the West.
