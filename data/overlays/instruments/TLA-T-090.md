On September 29, 1817, Lewis Cass and Duncan McArthur signed a treaty at the foot of the Rapids of the Miami of Lake Erie with the Wyandot, Seneca, Delaware, Shawnee, Potawatomi, Ottawa and Chippewa. The Wyandot ceded a large tract in northwestern Ohio, and the Potawatomi, Ottawa and Chippewa ceded an adjoining tract. The United States agreed to pay the Wyandot annually forever and to patent reserved land to chiefs of the Wyandot, Seneca and Shawnee for named individuals, including a twelve-mile-square tract at Upper Sandusky and a thirty-thousand-acre Seneca tract.

A blog from the Myaamia Center at Miami University says the treaty ceded over 4.5 million acres, and quotes treaty commissioner McArthur that the American goal was "relinquishment of [Native] claim to as much as possible of the lands within the State of Ohio." No Miami leaders signed, though the Miami leader Little Turtle had claimed the land in 1795. The Miami protested that the treaty was unjust.

The Senate objected to the reservations granted in fee simple. The supplementary treaty of September 17, 1818 (TLA-T-097) turned those grants into reservations.
