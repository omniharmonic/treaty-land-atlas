On September 20, 1818, Lewis Cass, commissioner for the United States, signed a treaty at St. Mary's, Ohio, with the chiefs and warriors of the Wyandot tribe. It dealt with land in Michigan Territory.

In Article 1 the Wyandot were recorded as ceding "all the right reserved to them" in two tracts. One included the village of Brownstown and the other the village of Maguaga, "formerly in the possession of the Wyandot tribe." The treaty says the two tracts held "not more than five thousand acres" in all. Under an act of Congress of February 28, 1809, "for the relief of certain Alabama and Wyandot Indians," they had been reserved for the Wyandot and their descendants for fifty years.

In Article 2, "in consideration of the preceding cession," the United States agreed to reserve for the Wyandot a set of numbered sections on the south side of the Huron River, in the fourth township south of the base line. The text gives their total as 4,996 acres. The Wyandot and their descendants would hold the land on the terms the 1809 act set for the Alabama Indians, except that they would keep it only as long as they or their descendants occupied it.

The treaty has two articles. The sources here do not describe the negotiation or later history of the Huron River reservation.
