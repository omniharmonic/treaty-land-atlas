On September 25, 1818, William Clark, governor of Missouri Territory, signed a treaty at St. Louis with a deputation of "considerate men, chiefs, and warriors" of all the bands of the Great and Little Osage nation.

The treaty gives the reason in its first article. It says the Osage "have been embarrassed by the frequent demands for property taken from the citizens of the United States, by war parties, and other thoughtless men." Their chiefs had not been able to recover it. Under the 1808 treaty at Fort Clark, deductions from their annuities would leave them without any for several years, and they were "destitute of funds to do that justice."

To settle this, the Osage ceded a tract beginning on the Arkansas River at Frog Bayou, up the Arkansas and Verdigris rivers to the falls of the Verdigris, then east to the Osage boundary line twenty leagues north of the Arkansas. In Article 2 the United States agreed to pay its own citizens the proven value of property the Osage had stolen or destroyed since 1814, up to $4,000.

The Kansas Historical Society's Kansapedia says the 1818 treaty "was a response to Euro-American and eastern tribes' intrusions into Osage lands" and led to cessions used "to push the Cherokee farther west." It places the 1818 cession between the White, Arkansas and Neosho-Grand rivers.
