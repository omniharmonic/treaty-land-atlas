On September 24, 1819, Michigan Territory Governor Lewis Cass signed a treaty at Saginaw with the Chippewa nation. The Chippewa ceded a large tract of land starting at the Indian boundary line six miles south of the "base line," running west sixty miles, then to the head of the Thunder Bay River, down it to its mouth, and northeast to the Canadian boundary. The treaty reserved about fifteen tracts for the Chippewa, such as eight thousand acres on the Au Sable River and forty thousand acres on the west side of the Saginaw River, plus 640-acre grants to named individuals.

A Michigan State University geography page, citing the Atlas of Michigan, says the incentive for the treaty came from people who wanted Saginaw Valley land "for settlement or speculation." They lobbied in Washington, and Cass was instructed to negotiate. It says Cass proposed to buy "some six million acres," offered $3,000 in cash and $1,000 a year, and distributed five barrels of whiskey after the signing.

The same page says that Michigan's later cessions, including the 1821 Chicago and 1836 Washington treaties, left only a few reserved tracts and western Upper Peninsula land by statehood in 1837.
