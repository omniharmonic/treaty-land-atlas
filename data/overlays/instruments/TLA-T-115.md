On October 18, 1820, Andrew Jackson and Thomas Hinds signed a "treaty of friendship, limits, and accommodation" with the Choctaw Nation near Doak's Stand on the Natchez Road. The preamble says the President wanted to promote Choctaw "civilization" through schools. It also says he wanted to exchange "a small part of their land here" for "a country beyond the Mississippi River." It adds that the state of Mississippi wanted part of Choctaw land.

The Choctaw ceded a tract in Mississippi. In part payment, the United States ceded a tract west of the Mississippi, between the Arkansas and Red rivers. The United States also promised each warrior who wished to move a blanket, a kettle, a rifle and a year's ammunition, plus corn to support him and his family for a year. Fifty-four sections of ceded land were to be sold to pay for Choctaw schools.

Article 4 set the boundaries east of the Mississippi to "remain without alteration" until the Choctaw became citizens of the United States and Congress set aside land for each family. That did not last. The Encyclopedia of Oklahoma History says some families moved into the new land, but "the majority did not" until Andrew Jackson pushed the Indian Removal Act through Congress in 1830. That year Choctaw leaders signed the Dancing Rabbit Creek treaty, ceding their remaining territory in Mississippi.
