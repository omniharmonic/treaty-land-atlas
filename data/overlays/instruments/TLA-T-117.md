On August 29, 1821, Lewis Cass and Solomon Sibley signed a treaty at Chicago with the Ottawa, Chippewa and Potawatomi nations. The tribes ceded a large tract running from the St. Joseph River, near Parc aux Vaches, east along a line from the southern end of Lake Michigan and then north to the Grand River, and down the Grand to Lake Michigan.

The treaty reserved several tracts for the Indians, including six miles square at Mang-ach-qua village and at Mick-ke-saw-be, and a tract at the head of the Kalamazoo River. The Ottawa were promised $1,000 a year forever and $1,500 a year for ten years for a blacksmith, teacher and farming help. The Potawatomi were promised $5,000 a year for twenty years and $1,000 a year for fifteen. The Greenville treaty right to hunt on ceded lands applied. The United States gained the privilege of making a road through Indian country from Detroit and Fort Wayne to Chicago.

A Michigan State University page says by this treaty "most of the land in the southwestern part of the Lower Peninsula south of the Grand River was acquired" in that treaty.
