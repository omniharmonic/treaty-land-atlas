On August 29, 1821, Lewis Cass and Solomon Sibley signed a treaty at Chicago with the Ottawa, Chippewa and Potawatomi nations. The tribes ceded a large tract. Its boundary began on the south bank of the St. Joseph River near the Parc aux Vaches and followed former cession lines to the Grand River and Lake Michigan. A Michigan State University page says that by this treaty "most of the land in the southwestern part of the Lower Peninsula south of the Grand River was acquired."

Article 2 reserved tracts for the Indians. Two were six miles square, at Mang-ach-qua Village and at Mick-ke-saw-be. Others were at Na-to-wa-se-pe and Prairie Ronde, and at the head of the Kalamazoo River. Article 3 granted sections of land to named individuals, who were "Indians by descent," and barred them from selling without the President's permission.

The United States agreed to pay the Ottawa "one thousand dollars in specie annually forever," and the Potawatomi five thousand dollars annually for twenty years. It also funded blacksmiths and teachers for several years. The Greenville treaty's right to hunt on ceded land applied. The United States gained "the privilege of making and using a road through the Indian country" from Detroit and Fort Wayne to Chicago.

The Statutes at Large record a proclamation on March 25, 1822. The MSU page says that, for Michigan treaties generally, "in almost every instance, the Government failed to live up to its obligations."
