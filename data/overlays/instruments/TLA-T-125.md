On February 12, 1825, U.S. commissioners Duncan Campbell and James Meriwether signed a convention at Indian Springs with chiefs of the Creek Nation. The preamble says the commissioners told the Creeks it was U.S. policy that tribes inside states should remove west of the Mississippi, partly to comply with a 1802 compact with Georgia. It records that the chiefs of the Creek towns assented, "those of Tokaubatchee excepted."

The Creeks ceded all their lands in Georgia and a large tract in Alabama. The United States offered land on the Arkansas River "acre for acre" and $400,000 for losses from removal.

The Encyclopedia of Alabama says the Creek National Council had refused to cede more land and had made ceding land a capital offense. It says the commissioners turned to William McIntosh, who "had accepted bribes in the past," and that he and others were executed. A large majority of chiefs objected that he lacked authority. Because of the treaty's "fraudulent nature," it was annulled and replaced by the 1826 Treaty of Washington (see TLA-T-144), though the Creek territory was not restored.
