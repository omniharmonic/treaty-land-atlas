On January 24, 1826, Secretary of War James Barbour signed a treaty in Washington with chiefs and head men of the Creek Nation. Its preamble says that "a great majority of the Chiefs and Warriors" had protested the Indian Springs treaty of February 12, 1825. They said it was signed "by persons having no sufficient authority to form treaties, or to make cessions."

The United States said it was unwilling that cessions of land "should be made to them, unless with the fair understanding and full assent of the Tribe making such cession." Article 1 then declared the 1825 treaty, which the United States had ratified on March 7, 1825, "null and void."

But the new treaty also took land. In Article 2 the Creeks were recorded as ceding all their land in Georgia east of the middle of the Chattahoochee River, plus a second tract on the river's western bank. The United States agreed to pay $217,600 to be divided among the chiefs and warriors, and a perpetual annuity of $20,000. A supplementary article of March 31, 1826 extended the lines of the second tract to fifty miles and forty-five miles. Article 6 provided for the friends and followers of William McIntosh, who wished to move west, to send a deputation to examine land there.

The Encyclopedia of Alabama says: "The Creeks did not, however, have their territory restored in the new treaty." The Statutes at Large record a proclamation on April 22, 1826.
