On February 11, 1828, John Tipton, commissioner for the United States, signed a treaty at the Wyandot village near the Wabash in Indiana. The other party was the Eel River or Thorntown party of the Miami.

In Article 1 the party ceded all their right and claim to a reservation "about ten miles square," at their village on Sugartree Creek. The Miami treaty at St. Mary's, Ohio, on October 6, 1818 had reserved it to them. They were bound by the treaty not to burn or destroy the houses or fences, and to "remove to the five mile reservation on Eel River by the fifteenth day of October next."

The commissioner had delivered $2,000 in goods. If the Senate ratified the treaty, the United States would pay $8,000 more in goods, build twelve log houses, and clear and fence forty acres. It would also furnish a wagon, oxen, horses and $500 worth of provisions. If the treaty were not ratified, the party agreed to pay for the goods by deduction from its annuity for that year.

At the request of the Indians, the United States also agreed to pay Peter Langlois $1,000 in silver and $3,000 in goods for earlier provisions and goods. It appropriated $1,000 a year for five years to educate Miami youths. The Statutes at Large record a proclamation on May 7, 1828.
