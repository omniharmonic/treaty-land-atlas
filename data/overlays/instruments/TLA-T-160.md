On September 27, 1830, commissioners John H. Eaton and John Coffee signed a treaty at Dancing Rabbit Creek for the United States with the Choctaw Nation. The Choctaw ceded their remaining territory east of the Mississippi River. The treaty text gives no acreage; the Encyclopedia of Alabama says the Choctaw ceded more than 10 million acres of communal land in Mississippi and Alabama.

The treaty's preamble says Mississippi had extended its laws over Choctaw land and that the President said he could not protect the Choctaw from them. The Encyclopedia of Alabama says the chiefs twice rejected the terms and the commissioners then met secretly with selected chiefs. It says a supplement gave extra land to signing chiefs, their families, pro-removal Choctaws and traders to whom the Choctaw owed debts.

The treaty set removal for 1831 to 1833 and paid $20,000 a year for twenty years. Article 14 offered 640-acre sections to heads of families who stayed. The Encyclopedia says about 13,000 Choctaws went west, most Article 14 claims went unrecorded, and about 6,000 stayed.
