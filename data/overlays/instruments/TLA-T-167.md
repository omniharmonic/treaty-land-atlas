On March 24, 1832, Lewis Cass, authorized by the President, signed a treaty at Washington City with the Creek tribe. In Article 1 the Creeks ceded "all their land, East of the Mississippi river." The text gives no acreage.

The United States agreed to survey the land and let ninety principal chiefs each select a section, and every other head of a Creek family a half section. These tracts were reserved from sale for five years, and those who stayed would then get patents in fee simple. Other articles added an annuity of $12,000 for five years and then $10,000 for fifteen years. They also gave $100,000 to pay Creek debts and relieve their "present distressed condition," in full payment for improvements. Article 12 said the United States was "desirous that the Creeks should remove to the country west of the Mississippi," would pay removal costs and a year's subsistence, but would not compel any Creek to emigrate.

In Article 14 the United States guaranteed the Creek country west of the Mississippi. It said "nor shall any State or Territory ever have a right to pass laws for the government of such Indians." In McGirt v. Oklahoma (2020), the Supreme Court cited this treaty. Its summary says "Congress has since broken more than a few promises to the Tribe. Nevertheless, the Creek Reservation persists today."

The Statutes at Large record a proclamation on April 4, 1832.
