On 14 February 1833, at Fort Gibson on the Arkansas River, three United States commissioners (Montfort Stokes, Henry Ellsworth and John Schermerhorn) signed a supplementary treaty with chiefs and head men of the Cherokee nation west of the Mississippi. The treaty says it follows the 6 May 1828 convention, which guaranteed the Cherokee seven million acres and a perpetual outlet west.

The preamble explains the problem. The 1828 convention was not to interfere with lands assigned to the Creek, and under a Creek treaty of 24 January 1826, the Creek had the right to select, and did select, part of the same country. The Cherokee and Creek nations west of the Mississippi met in council and agreed on the line between them.

Article 1 therefore restates the pledge to give the Cherokee seven million acres, now within new boundaries, plus the perpetual outlet west. It reserves to the United States the right to let other tribes get salt on a salt plain if it falls within the outlet. In Article 2 the Cherokee quit-claimed any lands from the 1828 grant outside the new limits.

At the Cherokee's request, Article 3 cancelled the 1828 promise of laws and surveys. Article 4 added blacksmith, wagon-maker and wheelwright shops and eight corn mills. The page lists a proclamation on 12 April 1834.
