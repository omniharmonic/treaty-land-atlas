On February 14, 1833, commissioners Montfort Stokes, Henry L. Ellsworth and John F. Schermerhorn signed an agreement at Fort Gibson for the United States. The other parties were the chiefs and head men of the Muskogee, or Creek, nation. The preamble explains the trouble. The 1826 treaty had provided for a country west of the Mississippi, and Creek delegates picked land along the Verdigris, Arkansas and Canadian rivers. But a 1828 Cherokee treaty assigned the Cherokees land that included part of it. Cherokees then settled on land the Creeks claimed, and the two nations appealed to the President.

The commissioners heard both sides in council and fixed boundary lines "acknowledged, in open council, this day, to be mutually satisfactory to both nations." Article 2 described the Creek country by rivers and survey lines. Article 3 promised a patent in fee simple whenever the treaty was ratified. Article 4 made the land the property of the whole Creek nation, including those still east of the Mississippi, and located the Seminoles on part of it.

Improvements the Creeks had to leave because of the new line would be valued and paid for. The United States also agreed to provide $1,000 a year for education, blacksmiths and mills. Article 8 said the new country replaced the one intended under the 1826 treaty.

In McGirt v. Oklahoma (2020), the Supreme Court said this 1833 treaty "fixed borders for a 'permanent home to the whole Creek Nation of Indians.'" It held that the reservation persists.
