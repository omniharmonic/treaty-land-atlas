On May 13, 1833, John F. Schermerhorn, commissioner of Indian affairs west, signed a treaty at New Gascony, Arkansas Territory, with the chiefs and warriors of the Quapaw. The preamble recalls the 1824 treaty, under which the Quapaw were recorded as ceding their Arkansas lands and were "to be concentrated and confined to a district of country inhabited by the Caddo Indians."

It says they did move and settled on Bayou Treache on the Red River, on land the Caddo gave them. But the tract was "subject to frequent inundations," their crops were "destroyed by the water year after year," and "nearly one-fourth of their people died." The Caddo refused to take them in as part of their tribe. The Quapaw "saw no alternative but to perish if they continued there," and chose to return to the Arkansas. There, settlers had taken their "little improvements."

In Article 1 they were recorded as relinquishing the Red River lands. In Article 2 the United States agreed to convey 150 sections of land west of the Missouri state line, between the Seneca and Shawnee lands, "to provide a permanent home for their nation." It would remove them at its expense, with a year's provisions, stock, tools and a farmer and blacksmith. It would pay their debts of $4,180 and $2,000 a year for twenty years.

The Encyclopedia of Arkansas says this was 150 sections (96,000 acres), and that many Quapaw did not go to the new reservation. It says most of the Quapaw Nation lives in northeastern Oklahoma today.
