On September 26, 1833, commissioners George B. Porter, Thomas J. V. Owen and William Weatherford signed a treaty at Chicago for the United States with the united nation of Chippewa, Ottawa and Potawatomi. The Indians ceded their land along the western shore of Lake Michigan, estimated at about five million acres. The United States wanted the land for settlement.

In return the Indians were to receive a tract west of the Mississippi River, with annuities and funds for education, farming and mills. The main treaty sets out roughly $850,000 in payments, including money for claims, goods and annuities, and supplementary articles added $100,000 and land cessions in Michigan. The Indians were to move west at United States expense, with a year's subsistence, though they could stay north of the Illinois boundary for three years.

The United States paid $175,000 in claims against the nation, only if the claimants accepted the amounts in full. The Senate later required a commissioner to review these debts and changed the boundary and payment terms in 1834.
