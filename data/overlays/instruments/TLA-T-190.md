On October 9, 1833, Henry L. Ellsworth, a commissioner for the United States, met the chiefs and head-men of four confederated Pawnee bands at the Grand Pawnee village on the Platte River. The bands were the Grand Pawnees, Pawnee Loups, Pawnee Republicans and Pawnee Tappaye. In Article I they ceded and relinquished to the United States all their right, interest and title to the land south of the Platte. The treaty states no acreage.

In return the United States promised $4,600 in goods each year for twelve years, split among the bands. It promised $500 in farm tools to each band for five years, $1,000 a year for ten years for schools, and two blacksmiths with a shop and tools. Each band was to get a farmer for five years and a horse-mill for grinding corn. Article II kept the ceded land as a common hunting ground at the President's pleasure, where not assigned to a tribe.

Much of this was conditional. Article X says the United States need not provide the schools, the farmers and stock, or the mills until the bands settled in farming districts and stayed there year-round. Article VII held back the oxen and stock until the President judged it safe to hand them over. The treaty was proclaimed on April 12, 1834. The Oklahoma State University treaty database lists the Pawnee Nation of Oklahoma as the successor in interest.
