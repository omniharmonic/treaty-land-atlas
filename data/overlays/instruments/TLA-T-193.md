On 4 December 1834, at a camp on Lake Maxeeniekuekee in Indiana, William Marshall, commissioner for the United States, made a short treaty with Comoza, a chief of the Potawatomi (spelled Potawattimie in the text), and his band.

In Article 1 the chief and his band ceded to the United States the two sections of land reserved for them by the second article of the treaty made with the Pottawattimie Indians on the Tippecanoe River on 26 October 1832. The treaty itself gives no acreage.

In Article 2 they agreed to yield peaceable possession of those sections within three years from the date of ratification of that 1832 treaty.

In Article 3 the United States agreed to pay the chief and his band four hundred dollars in goods at the signing, and an annuity of four hundred dollars for one year. The text acknowledges receipt of the goods.

Article 4 made the treaty binding once the President and Senate ratified it. The page lists a proclamation on 16 March 1835.

The 1832 treaty it modifies was not opened here, so the reserve's origin and any other terms are not described. The text does not say where the band went or why it agreed to cede.
