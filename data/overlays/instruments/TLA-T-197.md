On July 1, 1835, Jehiel Brooks, commissioner for the United States, signed a treaty at the agency house in the Caddo nation, Louisiana. The other party was the chiefs, head men and warriors of the Caddo. In Article I they agreed to cede and relinquish all their land within stated boundaries. The land lay between the Sabine and Red rivers, west of the line then separating the United States from the Republic of Mexico. The text gives no acreage, only "more or less."

Article II says the Caddo voluntarily relinquished the territory and promised to remove, at their own expense, out of the boundaries of the United States within one year of signing. They also promised never to return to live there as a nation, tribe or community. The United States agreed in Article III to pay a total of $80,000: $30,000 in goods and horses at signing, $10,000 in money within a year, and $10,000 a year for the next four years.

Supplementary articles signed the same day confirmed four square leagues of land for the heirs of François Grappe. The text says the Caddo had given a league each to Grappe and his three sons in 1801. They also reserved one section for Larkin Edwards, a longtime interpreter. The treaty was proclaimed on February 2, 1836. The Oklahoma State University database lists the Caddo Nation of Oklahoma as successor in interest. The treaty text does not describe the circumstances of the negotiation.
