On December 29, 1835, commissioners William Carroll and John F. Schermerhorn signed the Treaty of New Echota in Georgia for the United States. Under Article 1 the Cherokee ceded all the lands they owned, claimed or possessed east of the Mississippi River for five million dollars. Article 16 required the Cherokee to remove to their new homes within two years of ratification. Article 8 promised steamboats and baggage-wagons to carry them, and subsistence for one year after arrival.

The treaty's own preamble records the split. It says the Cherokee had authorized a delegation of twenty to negotiate, that this delegation went to Washington though told the President would not receive it, and that a council was called at New Echota for December 21, 1835. The National Park Service says twenty Cherokee men, none of them elected officials of the tribe, signed. It says John Ross, the Principal Chief, had persuaded the Cherokee council not to approve a treaty. It says Ross won the support of the majority of the nation, and that the treaty was widely protested by Cherokee and by whites.

The Oklahoma State University database shows the proclamation on May 23, 1836. The NPS says that in spring 1838 federal troops forced thousands of Cherokee into camps for the journey west, and that the last group arrived in March 1839. It also says the U.S. government never paid the $5 million promised in the treaty.
