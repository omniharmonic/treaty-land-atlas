On December 29, 1835, commissioners William Carroll and John F. Schermerhorn signed a treaty at New Echota, Georgia, for the United States with Cherokee signers including Major Ridge, John Ridge, Elias Boudinot and Stand Watie. Under Article 1 the Cherokee ceded all lands east of the Mississippi River for five million dollars. The United States promised steamboats, wagons and a year's subsistence in the west, and Article 16 required removal within two years of ratification.

The National Park Service says twenty Cherokee men, none of them elected officials of the tribe, signed the treaty, and that John Ross persuaded the Cherokee Council not to approve it. The Senate approved it by a single vote; the treaty was proclaimed on May 23, 1836.

In spring 1838 federal troops forced thousands of Cherokee into camps to prepare for the journey west, and the last group reached its destination in March 1839. The NPS also notes the Indian Removal Act of 1830 gave the President authority to make treaties with the Cherokee.
