On August 5, 1836, Abel C. Pepper, commissioner for the United States, signed a treaty at a camp near Yellow River, Indiana. The other parties were Pe-pin-a-waw, No-taw-kah and Mac-kah-tah-mo-ah, chiefs and headmen of the Potawatomi (spelled Potawattimie in the text), and their bands.

Article 1 ceded twenty-two sections of land reserved for them by the second article of the October 26, 1832 treaty on the Tippecanoe River. The treaty states no acreage. Article 2 had the United States pay $14,080 in specie, meaning coin. Article 4, made at the request of the band, had a commissioner pay the band's debts that he found just, deducted from that sum. In Article 3 the chiefs, headmen and their bands were bound by the treaty to remove west of the Mississippi River within two years. The treaty was proclaimed on February 18, 1837.

The Forest County Potawatomi Community publishes this treaty with an editor's note. It says the treaty is unusual because it carries signatures of people unaffected by it, "Proper chiefs of the Wabash Patawattamies." It says Menominee, Muck-kah-tah-mo-way, Pee-pin-oh-waw and the sons of No-taw-kah wrote at least three letters protesting that none of them had attended the council or signed, and that the treaty was invalid. The note adds that the treaty does not carry Menominee's name. Menominee here is a Potawatomi chief, not the Menominee nation of Wisconsin.
