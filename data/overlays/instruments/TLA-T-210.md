In a convention dated September 10, 1836, Colonel Zachary Taylor, acting as Indian agent, met the chiefs, braves and principal men of the Sioux of Wa-ha-shaw's tribe. The talks concerned land lying between the State of Missouri and the Missouri River.

The treaty explains why. An earlier treaty, Prairie du Chien in 1830, had said this country was to be assigned to tribes living there or to others the President might place there. The 1836 text says it was "desirable" that the lands be attached to Missouri and the Indian title ended, and that the earlier treaty's terms would be kept until the Indians involved agreed.

The signers then agreed to "forever cede, relinquish, and quit claim" all their right, title and interest. The text gives their reasons as a belief that the land could never serve Indian purposes and that settling Indians there would lead to collisions with United States citizens. It also frees the United States from any earlier guarantee about the land.

In return the United States agreed to furnish presents worth four hundred dollars, in goods or in money. The treaty was proclaimed on February 15, 1837. Five chiefs and headmen made their marks.
