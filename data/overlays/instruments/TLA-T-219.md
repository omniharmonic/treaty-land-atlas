On January 14, 1837, Henry R. Schoolcraft, commissioner for the United States, signed a treaty at Detroit with the Saganaw (Saginaw) tribe of the Chippewa nation, through their chiefs and delegates assembled in council. In Article 1 the tribe ceded fifteen named tracts in Michigan, from 640 acres to 40,000 acres, on rivers including the Au Sable, Flint, Cass, Shiawassee and Saginaw. The treaty states the whole contains 102,400 acres, more or less.

Under Article 3 the lands were to be surveyed and sold like other public lands. The net proceeds, after set-aside sums, were to be invested in public stock for the tribe. Article 4 set aside sums from the fund, including $40,000 for debts since the Treaty of Ghent and $10,000 for schools. Article 2 let the tribe live on two of the tracts for five years, with white settlers barred on penalty of $500.

In Article 6 the tribe agreed to remove from the State of Michigan as soon as a proper location could be obtained. A deputation would view country west of the western tip of Lake Superior, and if that failed the United States would help them get a location west of the Mississippi. The costs were to be repaid from land proceeds. Notes on the page say parts of Articles 4 to 6 were later abrogated or revised by a December 20, 1837 treaty. The treaty was proclaimed on July 2, 1838.
