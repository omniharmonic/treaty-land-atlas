On July 29, 1837, Henry Dodge, Governor of Wisconsin Territory and commissioner for the United States, signed a treaty at St. Peters, at the meeting of the St. Peters and Mississippi rivers. The other party was the Chippewa nation, through its chiefs and headmen. In Article 1 they were recorded as ceding a tract of country described by boundaries, running from the junction of the Crow Wing and Mississippi rivers across to the Chippewa and Wisconsin river country. The treaty states no acreage.

In Article 2 the United States agreed to pay annually for twenty years $9,500 in money and $19,000 in goods, with sums for blacksmiths, farmers, provisions and tobacco. Article 5 guaranteed to the Indians "the privilege of hunting, fishing, and gathering the wild rice" on the ceded lands, rivers and lakes, during the pleasure of the President.

The Supreme Court records what followed. In an 1850 Executive Order, President Taylor ordered the Chippewa's removal from the ceded territory and revoked those rights. The Court noted that the 1837 treaty made no mention of removal. In Minnesota v. Mille Lacs Band of Chippewa Indians (decided March 24, 1999), it held that the 1850 order was ineffective and that the Chippewa retain the rights the treaty guaranteed. The treaty was proclaimed on June 15, 1838.
