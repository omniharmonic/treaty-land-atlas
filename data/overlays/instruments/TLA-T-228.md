On November 1, 1837, in Washington, Carey A. Harris and a Winnebago delegation signed a treaty. The Winnebago ceded all their land east of the Mississippi River, gave up the right to occupy part of their land west of the river except for hunting, and agreed to move within eight months of ratification to the neutral ground set aside in 1832.

The United States set aside $200,000, which paid named individuals at the chiefs' request and went to debts, and promised $100,000 to relations with Winnebago blood, up to $7,000 for removal, and goods, provisions, horses and a gristmill. The Minnesota Historical Society says treaties in 1832 and 1837 called for Ho-Chunk removal to northeastern Iowa, that many rejected the 1832 treaty and stayed in Wisconsin, and that after 1837 more made the journey.

Both sources show removal was the central term of the treaty. In 1848 the U.S. government moved the Ho-Chunk again, from Iowa to Long Prairie in Minnesota Territory.
