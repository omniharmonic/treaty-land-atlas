On January 15, 1838, at Buffalo Creek, New York, U.S. commissioner Ransom H. Gillet and chiefs of several New York Indian tribes signed a treaty. The tribes ceded their rights to lands secured at Green Bay in the 1831 Menominee treaty, and the United States set apart 1,824,000 acres west of Missouri as a home for them. Tribes that did not agree to remove within five years would forfeit their interest in those lands.

The treaty's preamble says the President wanted to carry out the policy of removing Indians from east to west of the Mississippi. It also records that Thomas Ogden and Joseph Fellows had bought the Seneca Nation's lands for $202,000 in the presence of a U.S. commissioner.

The Seneca did not accept this sale. Court-history summaries note that the Tonawanda Seneca asserted the treaty was invalid as to their lands and brought cases in New York courts. The New York Courts history site says that, after Seneca representatives met President Buchanan and Congress, the Seneca gave up their Kansas rights and used federal relocation funds to purchase their lands from the Ogden company.
