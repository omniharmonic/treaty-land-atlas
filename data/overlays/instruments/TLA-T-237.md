On February 7, 1839, at Lower Saginaw, Michigan, John Hulbert, acting for the Superintendent of Indian Affairs, and eight chiefs of the Saginaw Chippewa signed two short papers. They supplement earlier treaties.

The first recalls that the tribe had ceded its Michigan reserves in a treaty of January 14, 1837, on the principle that the reserves would be sold at public land offices for the tribe's benefit. It says a later act of Congress required a lighthouse on one reserve, the Na-bo-bish tract at the mouth of the Saginaw River.

The tribe therefore agreed that one-sixteenth of a section of that tract be set apart, for eight dollars an acre, to be disposed of as the President directed. The tribe relinquished all claim to proceeds from it other than that sum. Article 2 says that once the Senate and President approved, possession could be taken at once and the Indians' "usufructory right" would end.

A second paper, listed as 7 Stat. 579, says the site would be forty acres, and lets the President move the location to the forty thousand acre reservation at the river's mouth. Both were proclaimed on March 2, 1839.
