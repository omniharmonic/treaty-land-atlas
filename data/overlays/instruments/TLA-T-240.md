On March 17, 1842, John Johnston, a commissioner appointed under President Tyler, signed a treaty at Upper Sandusky, Ohio, with the chiefs, counsellors and head-men of the Wyandott Nation in full council. In Article 1 the Wyandot ceded the residue of their large reserve in Crawford County, Ohio, all their remaining lands in the state, containing 109,144 acres more or less. They also ceded the Wyandotte Reserve in Michigan, 4,996 acres.

In return the United States granted a tract of 148,000 acres west of the Mississippi River, promised a perpetual annuity of $17,500 in specie, and agreed to pay the full value of their improvements. It also agreed to pay $23,860 of their debts to citizens, support a blacksmith, a sub-agent and an interpreter, and a school at $500 a year. Article 13 has the chiefs agree to remove their whole people west of the Mississippi.

The Wyandot Nation of Kansas says Johnston was sent as agent in 1841 and "firmly believed that removal was in the Wyandot's best interest." It says that after the murder of a Wyandot chief and two others in a hunting party in fall 1841, with no punishment, the Wyandot "realized that they could no longer stay in Ohio." On March 17, 1842, it says, the Wyandot voted to accept most of Johnston's proposals. The nation's history says 664 people left the Grand Reserve on July 12, 1843. The treaty was proclaimed on October 5, 1842.
