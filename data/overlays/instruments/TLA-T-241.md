On May 20, 1842, Ambrose Spencer, commissioner for the United States, signed a treaty at Buffalo Creek, New York, with the chiefs, headmen and warriors of the Seneca nation in council. It recites the January 15, 1838 Treaty of Buffalo Creek, which was amended and proclaimed on April 4, 1840. It also sets out an indenture signed the same day between the Seneca and Thomas L. Ogden and Joseph Fellows. They were assignees under the State of Massachusetts.

The indenture recites that in 1838 the Seneca had granted four tracts to Ogden and Fellows for $202,000: Buffalo Creek (estimated at 49,920 acres), Cattaraugus (21,680), Allegany (30,469) and Tonnewanda (12,800). It says questions and differences had since arisen and the parties agreed to settle them. In Article 1 Ogden and Fellows agreed the Seneca could keep the Cattaraugus and Allegany Reservations, while reserving their right of pre-emption in those tracts. In Article 2 the Seneca granted the Buffalo Creek and Tonnewanda Reservations to Ogden and Fellows. A new payment was figured on the two tracts given up.

The Historical Society of the New York Courts says the Tonawanda Seneca asserted that the 1838 treaty was invalid for their lands because their Sachem had not been a party. It says they won two suits that reached the New York courts and the U.S. Supreme Court, In a separate 1857 treaty, the Tonawanda band gave up its claims to lands west of Missouri, and the United States agreed to pay $256,000, from which the band could buy back its reservation from Ogden and Fellows. The treaty was proclaimed on August 26, 1842.
