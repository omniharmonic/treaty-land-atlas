On October 4, 1842, at La Pointe on Lake Superior, commissioner Robert Stuart and the Chippewa of the Mississippi and Lake Superior signed a treaty. The Chippewa ceded the country east of a line from the American Fur Company post on the Fond du Lac River to the 1837 treaty line, including all the islands in Lake Superior within the boundary.

The Indians stipulated for the right of hunting on the ceded territory, with other usual privileges of occupancy, until required to remove by the President. The United States agreed to pay each year for twenty-five years $12,500 in specie, $10,500 in goods, and further sums for provisions, tobacco, blacksmiths, farmers and schools. Article 3 says that when the Indians were required to remove, the unceded lands of certain bands would be common property and home of all the Indians party to the treaty.

In Lac Courte Oreilles Band v. Voigt (1983), the Seventh Circuit held that rights under the 1837 and 1842 treaties were not terminated by the 1854 treaty.
