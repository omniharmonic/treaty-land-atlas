On October 4, 1842, Robert Stuart, commissioner for the United States, signed a treaty at La Pointe on Lake Superior, in Wisconsin Territory, with the Chippewa Indians of the Mississippi and Lake Superior, through their chiefs and headmen. In Article I they were recorded as ceding the country east of a line running from the American Fur Company's trading post on the Fond du Lac (St. Louis) River to the line of the July 29, 1837 treaty, including all the islands of Lake Superior within the boundary. The treaty states no acreage.

In Article II the Indians stipulated for the right of hunting on the ceded territory, with the other usual privileges of occupancy, until required to remove by the President. In Article IV the United States agreed to pay each year for twenty-five years $12,500 in specie and $10,500 in goods, with further sums for provisions, tobacco, blacksmiths, farmers, carpenters and schools. It also allowed $75,000 for their debts in the ceded district. Article VI made the Indians on the mineral district subject to removal at the President's pleasure. The treaty was proclaimed on March 23, 1843.

A later court case concerned these rights. A 1985 Seventh Circuit opinion recounts that in Lac Courte Oreilles Band v. Voigt (1983) the court held that the rights established by the 1837 and 1842 treaties "were neither terminated nor released by the 1854 treaty." It also notes the district court found the President's 1850 Removal Order was without legal effect.
