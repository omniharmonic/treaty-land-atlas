On January 14, 1846, Thomas H. Harvey and Richard W. Cummins, commissioners for the United States, signed a treaty at the Methodist Mission in the Kansas country with the Kansas tribe of Indians. In Article 1 the tribe ceded two million acres on the east part of its country, across the full thirty-mile width and running west for quantity.

In Article 2 the United States agreed to pay $202,000. Two hundred thousand dollars was to be funded at five per cent, with the interest paid annually for thirty years. Of the interest, $1,000 a year was for education, $1,000 for farming help, and $8,000 was paid to them. The other $2,000 covered the costs of the treaty, $400 to the Methodist Episcopal missionary society for improvements, $600 for a mill, and provisions for the year. Article 4 required the tribe to move from the ceded lands by May 1, 1847. Article 5 provided that if the President found there was not enough timber on the land remaining, he would lay off other land near the western boundary, and the tribe would cede the rest of its 1825 reservation. The treaty was proclaimed on April 15, 1846.

The Kaw Nation's history says the 1825 treaty left a 2 million-acre reservation and that promised annuities were seldom delivered. It says the Treaty of 1846 further reduced Kaw territory to 256,000 acres at present-day Council Grove, and that on May 27, 1872, over the strong protests of Chief Allegawaho and his people, a federal act moved the Kanza to a 100,137-acre site in northern Kay County, Oklahoma.
