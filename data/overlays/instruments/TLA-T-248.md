On August 6, 1846, in Washington, three U.S. commissioners and delegations of the Cherokee Nation under John Ross, the "Treaty Party," and the "Western Cherokees" or Old Settlers signed a treaty. The preamble says serious difficulties had long existed among the different portions of the people, and that the Cherokee Nation and parts of its people had claims against the United States.

Article 1 said the lands then occupied by the nation would be secured to the whole people for their common use and benefit, and a patent would issue for them, including the 800,000 acres purchased and the outlet west. The patent was to follow an 1830 act of Congress and carried the condition that the land would revert to the United States if the Indians became extinct or abandoned it. Article 2 declared a general amnesty and pardoned offenses by one Cherokee against the nation or another Cherokee.

The treaty did not cede land. It reaffirmed the nation's title to its Indian Territory lands and settled disputes within the nation.
