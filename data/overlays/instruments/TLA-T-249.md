On October 13, 1846, in Washington, three U.S. commissioners and a Winnebago delegation signed a treaty. The tribe ceded all lands, wherever situated, that it occupied or claimed within the States and Territories, especially the "neutral ground" assigned to it in the 1832 Fort Armstrong treaty.

The United States agreed to buy and give the Winnebago a home north of the St. Peters and west of the Mississippi of not less than 800,000 acres, if it could be obtained on just and reasonable terms. It agreed to pay $150,000 for the land and $40,000 for the release of hunting privileges. Funds were set for the tribe's engagements, for exploring and selecting the new home, $20,000 for removal and $20,000 for subsistence the first year. The tribe was to remove within one year after the new home was procured.

The Minnesota Historical Society says that in 1848 the U.S. government removed the Ho-Chunk from northeastern Iowa to Long Prairie in Minnesota Territory.
