On April 1, 1850, Ardavan S. Loughery, commissioner for the United States, signed a convention at Washington with the head chief and deputies of the Wyandot tribe. The preamble looks back at the March 17, 1842 treaty, in which the Wyandot had sold their reservations in Ohio and Michigan and agreed to move west. In return the United States had agreed to convey a tract of 148,000 acres west of the Mississippi.

The preamble then says the Wyandot "never did receive the said one hundred and forty-eight thousand acres of land from the United States, but were forced to purchase lands from the Delaware nation of Indians." It adds that this purchase was agreed to and ratified by the United States. This was the treaty's own explanation for the settlement.

In Article 1 the Wyandot released and gave up all claim to the 148,000 acres. In return the United States agreed to pay $185,000, at $1.25 an acre. One hundred thousand dollars was to be invested in United States stocks at five per cent, with the interest paid to help the Wyandot pay their debts, including what they owed the Delawares for their lands. The other $85,000 was to be paid to the Wyandot nation or on its drafts. Article 2 had the United States pay the negotiation and delegation expenses. The convention was proclaimed on September 30, 1850.
