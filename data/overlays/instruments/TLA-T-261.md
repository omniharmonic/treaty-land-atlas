On July 1, 1852, at Santa Fe, Col. E. V. Sumner and Indian agent John Greiner signed a treaty with chiefs acting for "the Apache Nation," including Mangus Colorado. The Apaches acknowledged that they were "lawfully and exclusively under the laws, jurisdiction, and government of the United States." They agreed to perpetual peace, to end raids into Mexico, and to allow free and safe passage through their territory. The United States could build military posts, agencies and trading houses.

The United States wanted peace and control of travel through newly acquired New Mexico. In Article 9 the government promised to "designate, settle, and adjust their territorial boundaries" and to make laws for the prosperity and happiness of the Apaches. The treaty does not cede land or name a boundary.

The promised boundaries never appeared in this treaty. In litigation over Oak Flat in Arizona, a dissent in the Ninth Circuit's 2024 en banc decision says Apache leaders signed it "to make peace" and that conflict continued as settlers, miners and soldiers entered Apache land. The majority held that Congress's statutory land transfer for Oak Flat "clearly abrogates" any contrary treaty obligation.
