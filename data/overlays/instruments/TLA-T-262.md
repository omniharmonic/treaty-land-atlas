On July 27, 1853, Thomas Fitzpatrick, Indian agent and sole commissioner, signed a treaty at Fort Atkinson with the Comanche, Kiowa and Apache, whom the text describes as "inhabiting the said territory south of the Arkansas River." The tribes agreed to perpetual peace with the United States and with each other. They also agreed to stop raids into Mexican provinces, return captives, and make restitution for injuries to travelers.

The United States wanted safe travel across the southern plains. The tribes "fully recognize and acknowledge the right of the United States to lay off and mark out roads or highways" and to make reservations of land for them, locate depots, and establish military and other posts. EBSCO's research summary says the treaty aimed to ease white passage westward and a possible railroad. In return the United States agreed to pay $18,000 a year for ten years, with a possible five more, and to protect the tribes from depredations by U.S. citizens. The United States could withhold annuities for violations.

The treaty ceded no land by name. Its rights of way and posts opened the tribes' territory to American traffic. The input lists no linked violations or cases.
