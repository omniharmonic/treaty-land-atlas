On September 19, 1853, Joel Palmer, superintendent of Indian affairs, signed a treaty on Cow Creek in the Umpqua Valley, Oregon Territory. The other parties were Quin-ti-oo-san, or Bighead, principal chief, and two subordinate chiefs of the Cow Creek band of the Umpqua. In Article 1 the band ceded and relinquished all its right, title and claim to lands bounded by lines along the south fork of the Umpqua River and the ridges around Cow Creek. The treaty states no acreage. In Article 2 the band could occupy a smaller tract on Cow Creek temporarily as an Indian reserve until the President selected a permanent home.

In Article 3 the United States agreed to pay $12,000. One thousand dollars was in goods by October 1, 1854, and the remaining $11,000 in twenty annual installments of $550. In Article 4 it promised two dwelling-houses costing no more than $200 each and a fenced and ploughed field of five acres. In Article 5 the band agreed to give safe conduct to people passing through its reserve. The treaty was ratified on April 12, 1854 and proclaimed on February 5, 1855.

The Confederated Tribes of Siletz Indians' history says this treaty and the Rogue River treaty, signed nine days earlier, went through ratification side by side. It says that in them the Senate delegated the choice of a permanent reservation to the President.
