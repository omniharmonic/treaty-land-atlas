On March 16, 1854, in Washington, Commissioner George W. Manypenny and seven Omaha chiefs signed a treaty. The Omaha agreed to "cede to the United States all their lands west of the Missouri River" south of a line drawn due west from near the Ayoway River. They kept the country to the north as "their future home." If it proved unsuitable, the President could assign a new location of no more than 300,000 acres. They agreed to leave the ceded land within a year of ratification.

The Supreme Court later wrote that the tribe "was destitute and, in exchange for much-needed revenue, agreed to sell a large swath of its land." The Court gave the price as $840,000, paid over 40 years. The Kansas Historical Society says Manypenny promoted allotments to individuals, with excess land bought by the government and sold to railroad companies, white settlers and speculators.

In 1865 the Omaha ceded another 98,000 acres on the north side for a Winnebago reservation. Congress later authorized sales of reservation land in 1872 and 1882. In Nebraska v. Parker (2016) a unanimous Court held that the 1882 Act "did not diminish the Omaha Indian Reservation."
