On May 6, 1854, in Washington, Commissioner George W. Manypenny and Delaware delegates signed a treaty. The Delaware "cede, relinquish, and quit-claim to the United States all their right, title, and interest" in their country in the fork of the Missouri and Kansas Rivers and in the "outlet" to the west, except the part sold to the Wyandot and the land east and south of a line running to Big Island Creek on the Missouri, which they kept as their permanent home. The ceded land was to be surveyed and sold like public land.

The United States agreed to pay $10,000 for the outlet and to pay the Delaware the proceeds of land sales. It also paid $148,000 in two installments in exchange for their permanent annuities. Roads and railroads could cross the reserve; the treaty gave "all roads and highways laid out by authority of law" a right of way. The Kansas Historical Society says Manypenny convinced 11 Kansas tribes to cede nearly 13 million acres between March and June 1854, and that under his allotment plan excess land was to be sold to railroad companies, white settlers and speculators.

The Society adds that the 1887 Dawes Act formalized allotment, and that many tribal holdings were mortgaged, leased or sold to whites. In 1885 Manypenny wrote that had he known what would result from those treaties, "I would be compelled to admit that I had committed a high crime." The OSU treaty database lists ratification on July 11, 1854.
