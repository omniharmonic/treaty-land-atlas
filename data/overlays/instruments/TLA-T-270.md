On May 17, 1854, in Washington, Commissioner George W. Manypenny and four delegates of the Ioway tribe signed a treaty. The Ioway ceded the upper half of the "four hundred sections" strip along the Missouri River assigned to them and the Sac and Fox of Missouri by the 1836 treaty. They kept a tract beginning at the mouth of the Great Nemaha River, which "shall be the future and permanent home of the Ioway Indians." The treaty sets no acreage for it.

Instead of a fixed price, the United States agreed to survey the ceded land, offer it at public auction, and pay the Ioway the proceeds after costs. Land unsold after three years of private entry could be reduced in price by Congress. No white person could settle until the survey was approved. The treaty also granted 320 acres to a mission board.

The Kansas Historical Society says Manypenny convinced 11 Kansas tribes to cede nearly 13 million acres between March and June 1854, and that under his allotment plan excess land was to be sold to railroad companies, white settlers and speculators. It lists an Iowa reservation, in Brown and Doniphan counties, among four federally recognized reservations in Kansas today. The Society does not link today's reservation to this treaty.
