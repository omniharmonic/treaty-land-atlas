On May 18, 1854, in Washington, Commissioner George W. Manypenny and five Kickapoo delegates, including John Kennekuk, signed a treaty. The Kickapoo ceded the country southwest of the Missouri River promised them in the 1832 Castor Hill treaty, described as including "at least twelve hundred square miles." They kept 150,000 acres in the western part "for a future and permanent home," to be set off by metes and bounds. If the delegates could not find a suitable location in the specified western part, they could extend it west of the ceded line over public land.

The United States agreed to pay $300,000: $100,000 to be invested, and $200,000 in installments of $25,000 and then smaller amounts over later years. The Kansas Historical Society says Manypenny pushed tribes toward allotment, with excess land bought and sold to railroad companies, settlers and speculators.

The Society also notes that a Kickapoo reservation in Brown County is one of four federally recognized reservations in Kansas today. Manypenny later wrote that he "committed a high crime." The Society does not link today's reservation to the 1854 reserve, so that connection is not confirmed here.
