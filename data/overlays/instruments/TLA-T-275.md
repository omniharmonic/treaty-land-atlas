On September 30, 1854, at La Pointe, Wisconsin, commissioners Henry C. Gilbert and David B. Herriman signed a treaty with the Chippewa of Lake Superior and the Mississippi. The Lake Superior Chippewa ceded the lands east of a line running from Snake River to the Vermillion River. The United States set apart reserved tracts in Michigan, Wisconsin and Minnesota, including a tract for the La Pointe band and 200 acres on Madeline Island. Article 11 provided that the Indians "shall not be required to remove from the homes hereby set apart for them" and could "hunt and fish therein, until otherwise ordered by the President."

MNopedia says the main U.S. aim was access to copper, minerals and pine. Many Ojibwe owed money to traders and were open to a deal. In 1850 President Taylor had ordered Ojibwe in Wisconsin and Michigan removed, an order later cancelled, and hundreds died after payments were moved to Sandy Lake. The Ojibwe therefore asked for guarantees against removal.

Congress ratified the treaty in January 1855. Timber and mining companies followed, and many Ojibwe fell deeper into debt. A federal court upheld the hunting and fishing rights in the 1983 Voigt decision, according to MNopedia.
