On September 30, 1854, at La Pointe, Wisconsin, commissioners Henry C. Gilbert and David B. Herriman signed a treaty with the Chippewa of Lake Superior and the Mississippi. The Lake Superior Chippewa ceded the lands east of a line running from Snake River to the Vermillion River. The United States set apart reserved tracts in Michigan, Wisconsin and Minnesota, including a tract for the La Pointe band and 200 acres on Madeline Island. Article 11 provided that the Indians "shall not be required to remove from the homes hereby set apart for them," and that those living in the ceded territory "shall have the right to hunt and fish therein, until otherwise ordered by the President."

MNopedia says the main U.S. aim was access to copper, minerals and pine. Many Ojibwe owed money to traders and were open to a deal. In 1850 President Taylor had ordered Ojibwe in Wisconsin and Michigan removed, an order later cancelled, and hundreds died after payments were moved to Sandy Lake. The Ojibwe therefore asked for guarantees against removal.

The Senate ratified the treaty on January 10, 1855. Timber and mining companies followed, and many Ojibwe fell deeper into debt. MNopedia says these hunting and fishing rights never expired, citing the 1983 Voigt decision; that federal case concerned rights reserved in the 1837 and 1842 treaties, which it held the 1854 treaty did not end.
