On December 26, 1854, Governor Isaac Stevens signed the Treaty of Medicine Creek with chiefs and delegates of the Nisqually, Puyallup, Steilacoom, Squaxin Island and related bands. The tribes ceded their lands around the head of Puget Sound. Reserved were an island of about two sections and two tracts of 1,280 acres each. The treaty promised $32,500 over twenty years and secured "the right of taking fish, at all usual and accustomed grounds and stations" in common with citizens.

HistoryLink says Stevens had the treaty read and explained only in Chinook Jargon, a trade vocabulary of a few hundred words. Leaders such as Leschi objected that the Nisqually and Puyallup reservations lacked river access and farmland. HistoryLink concludes that "not all those named actually signed." The National Park Service says Stevens pressured Puget Sound tribes into signing treaties. The Senate ratified it on March 3, 1855.

HistoryLink says dissatisfaction led to armed conflict, and in 1856 Stevens agreed to larger Puyallup and Nisqually reservations. In Puyallup Tribe v. Department of Game (1968) and Washington v. Fishing Vessel Association (1979), the Supreme Court took up the treaty fishing rights.
