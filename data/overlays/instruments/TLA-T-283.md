On January 22, 1855, Governor Isaac Stevens and more than 80 leaders of Puget Sound peoples, including Chief Seattle, signed the Treaty of Point Elliott at Muckl-te-oh, now Mukilteo. The Duwamish, Suquamish, Snoqualmie, Skagit, Lummi, Swinomish and many other bands ceded their lands from Point Pully to the 49th parallel. Small reservations were set apart, and the President could move or consolidate the tribes. The treaty promised $150,000 in declining annual payments and secured "the right of taking fish at usual and accustomed grounds and stations" in common with citizens.

HistoryLink describes the signers as surrendering their lands "for cash, relocation to reservations, and access to traditional fishing and hunting grounds." The National Park Service says Stevens pressured Puget Sound tribes into signing, and the Supreme Court notes that Stevens used a Chinook Jargon of a few hundred words in the treaty councils.

Congress ratified the treaty in 1859, per the input data. In Oliphant v. Suquamish Indian Tribe (1978) the Court cited the Treaty of Point Elliott in holding that tribes lack inherent criminal jurisdiction over non-Indians. Its fishing clause is part of the Stevens-treaty fishing litigation.
