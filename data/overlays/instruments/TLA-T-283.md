On January 22, 1855, Governor Isaac Stevens and more than 80 leaders of Puget Sound peoples, including Chief Seattle, signed the Treaty of Point Elliott at Muckl-te-oh, now Mukilteo. The Duwamish, Suquamish, Snoqualmie, Skagit, Lummi, Swinomish and many other bands ceded their lands from Point Pully to the 49th parallel. Small reservations were set apart, and the President could move or consolidate the tribes. The treaty promised $150,000 in declining annual payments and secured "the right of taking fish at usual and accustomed grounds and stations" in common with citizens.

HistoryLink describes the signers as surrendering their lands "for cash, relocation to reservations, and access to traditional fishing and hunting grounds." The National Park Service says Stevens pressured Puget Sound tribes into signing, and the Supreme Court has noted that the interpreter at Stevens's treaty councils used a "chinook jargon" with a simple 300-word vocabulary.

In Oliphant v. Suquamish Indian Tribe (1978) the Court held that tribal courts lack inherent criminal jurisdiction over non-Indians, noting the Suquamish tribe's Treaty of Point Elliott. The treaty's fishing clause was among those at issue in Washington v. Fishing Vessel Association (1979).
