On January 26, 1855, Governor Isaac Stevens signed the Treaty of Point No Point with chiefs and delegates of the S'Klallam villages and the Skokomish, Toanhooch and Chimakum. The tribes ceded their lands on the Strait of Juan de Fuca and Hood Canal. One reservation of six sections, or 3,840 acres, was set aside at the head of Hood Canal. The United States promised $60,000 in declining annual payments and secured the right of taking fish at usual and accustomed grounds.

HistoryLink says the council opened with the treaty translated into Chinook Jargon for about 1,200 people. Leaders argued against signing; the Skokomish leader Hool-hol-tan said he did not want to sign away his right to the land. By the next morning leaders arrived with white flags, ready to sign. HistoryLink says the document "had been prepared beforehand, with no intention of serving as a basis for negotiation."

The Senate ratified the treaty in 1859, per the input data. The Supreme Court cited the Stevens treaties' fishing clause in Washington v. Fishing Vessel Association (1979), and quoted Stevens at Point No Point comparing the paper to something a man would give his children.
