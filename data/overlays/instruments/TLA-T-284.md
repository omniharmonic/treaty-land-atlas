On January 26, 1855, Governor Isaac Stevens signed the Treaty of Point No Point with chiefs and delegates of the S'Klallam villages and the Skokomish, To-an-hooch and Chimakum. The tribes were recorded as ceding their lands on the Strait of Juan de Fuca and Hood Canal. One reservation of six sections, or 3,840 acres, was set aside at the head of Hood Canal. The United States promised $60,000 in declining annual payments and secured the right of taking fish at usual and accustomed grounds.

HistoryLink says the council opened with the treaty translated into Chinook Jargon for about 1,200 people. Leaders argued against signing; the Skokomish leader Hool-hol-tan said he did not want to sign away his right to the land. By the next morning leaders arrived with white flags, ready to sign. HistoryLink says the document "had been prepared beforehand, with no intention of serving as a basis for negotiation."

HistoryLink says Congress took several years to approve the Stevens treaties after Medicine Creek was ratified in March 1855. In Washington v. Fishing Vessel Association (1979), the Supreme Court quoted Stevens telling the Point No Point council that the paper was such as a man would give to his children.
