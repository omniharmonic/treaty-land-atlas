On January 31, 1855, Governor Isaac Stevens signed the Treaty of Neah Bay with chiefs and headmen of the Makah villages at Cape Flattery. HistoryLink says 42 leaders of four of the five traditional Makah villages gave up most of their ancestral lands, from Cape Flattery east to the Hoko River and south to Ozette. They kept a small reservation near Cape Flattery and a promised $30,000 in payments.

The United States wanted title to the Olympic Peninsula's land. Article 4 secured "the right of taking fish and of whaling or sealing at usual and accustomed grounds and stations." HistoryLink calls it the only U.S. treaty to guarantee whaling, and says that unlike some Stevens treaties it "did not lead to armed conflict." The National Park Service says Stevens pressured Puget Sound tribes into signing treaties in 1854 and 1855.

The reservation was later enlarged to approximately 27,000 acres, according to HistoryLink. It adds that the treaty's rights language has been the subject of many legal battles, beginning in the late 1800s and continuing into the 2010s.
