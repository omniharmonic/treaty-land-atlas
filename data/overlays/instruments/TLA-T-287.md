On February 22, 1855, in Washington, Commissioner George W. Manypenny and chiefs of the Mississippi, Pillager and Lake Winnibigoshish bands of Chippewa signed a treaty. The bands ceded "all their right, title, and interest in, and to, the lands now owned and claimed by them" in Minnesota Territory within a described boundary. Article 2 reserved separate tracts for permanent homes, including townships around Mille Lacs and tracts on the Mississippi. The United States agreed to pay the Mississippi bands $10,000 in goods and $50,000 to settle their present engagements, plus annuities.

Treaties Matter says that with the collapse of the fur trade, the Ojibwe depended more on annuities, and that the U.S. intended them to become farmers on individually owned plots, promising to plow 675 acres for the whole population.

In Minnesota v. Mille Lacs Band (1999) the Supreme Court held that the Chippewa retained the hunting and fishing rights from the 1837 treaty because the 1855 treaty was "devoid of any language expressly mentioning usufructuary rights." Treaties Matter adds that the 1855 reservation tracts were later enlarged, ceded and restored by treaties and acts of Congress.
