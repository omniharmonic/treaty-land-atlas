On February 27, 1855, in Washington, Commissioner George W. Manypenny and Winnebago chiefs signed a treaty. The Winnebago were recorded as ceding the tract granted to them under the 1846 treaty, north of the Minnesota (St. Peter's) River and west of the Mississippi, "estimated to contain about eight hundred and ninety-seven thousand and nine hundred (897,900) acres." Parts of it were to be sold for the tribe's benefit. In exchange the Ho-Chunk got a reservation along the Blue Earth River.

MNopedia says the Ho-Chunk were recorded as ceding 897,900 acres near Long Prairie "in exchange for two hundred thousand acres along the Blue Earth River," a site better suited to farming. They began the move on May 24, 1855. Local white citizens met in Mankato to protest their arrival, and the Ho-Chunk soon held some of the territory's best cropland.

MNopedia reports that settlers pressed for their removal anyway. After the U.S.-Dakota War of 1862, which the Ho-Chunk did not join, a federal act of February 21, 1863 authorized exile from Minnesota. In May 1863, under threat of military force, 1,945 Ho-Chunk were moved to Crow Creek, South Dakota, and more than 550 died.
