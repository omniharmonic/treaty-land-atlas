On June 9, 1855, at Camp Stevens in the Walla Walla Valley, Governor Isaac Stevens signed a treaty with the Yakama, Palouse, Klickitat and other confederated tribes and bands, "to be considered as one nation, under the name of 'Yakama,'" with Kamaiakun as head chief. They ceded the lands they occupied and kept a reservation. The United States agreed to pay $200,000 and secured "the right of taking fish at all usual and accustomed places, in common with the citizens of the Territory."

The National Park Service says Stevens and Palmer broke chief Kamiakin's effort to unite the leaders "by cajoling and threatening the delegates." It also reports that the Yakama treaty joined fourteen tribal bands into one nation. HistoryLink says Stevens used a combination of promised gifts and threats of force.

The Park Service says Stevens opened ceded land to settlers before ratification, and the killing of Indian Agent A. J. Bolon in September 1855 began the Yakama War, which lasted until 1858. Congress ratified the treaty in 1859. In United States v. Winans (1905) the Supreme Court held that the fishing right was "a reservation by the Indians of rights already possessed and not granted away by them." Washington v. Fishing Vessel Association (1979) later listed the Yakama treaty among the six Stevens treaties at issue.
