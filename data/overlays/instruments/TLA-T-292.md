On June 22, 1855, in Washington, Commissioner George W. Manypenny and commissioners of the Choctaw and Chickasaw signed a treaty. Its preamble says the political connection between the tribes had caused "unhappy and injurious dissensions." Article 2 set up a separate Chickasaw District, and Article 9 had the Choctaws relinquish their claim west of the 100th meridian. Both nations leased to the United States their common territory west of the 98th meridian for the settlement of the Wichita and other tribes. The treaty also referred the Choctaws' claim to net proceeds from the Mississippi lands ceded in 1830 to the Senate.

The preamble states that "the United States desire that the Choctaw Indians shall relinquish all claim to any territory west of the one hundredth degree of west longitude." A Wichita State University journal article says three issues drove the talks: the net proceeds claim, Chickasaw demands for political autonomy, and the lease of land for the Wichita. It describes the Choctaws' negotiating position as relatively weak.

The treaty gave the United States use of the western strip and left the Chickasaw with their own district. The OSU treaty database lists ratification on February 21, 1856.
