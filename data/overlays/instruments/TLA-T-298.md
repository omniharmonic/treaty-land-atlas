On August 2, 1855, at Detroit, commissioners George W. Manypenny and Henry C. Gilbert signed an agreement with the Chippewa of Saginaw and the part of the Swan Creek and Black River band still in Michigan. The United States withdrew from sale "six adjoining townships of land in the county of Isabella" for the Indians, and gave 80 acres to each head of a family and 40 acres to each single adult. Article 3 ceded all the lands the bands had owned, and Article 6 dissolved their tribal organization.

A Central Michigan University library history says missionaries pushed for the arrangement, and that individual Indians could select acreage and eventually receive a patent. The Saginaw Chippewa Indian Tribe's own history says the 1855 and 1864 treaties established the Isabella Indian Reservation, totaling over 130,000 acres divided into allotments.

The tribe says conditions were grim, annuity payments were delayed, and "many of our people sold their allotments to lumber sharks for a small fraction of their value." It records that a federal court upheld the reservation's historical boundaries in 2010.
