On February 11, 1856, at Keshena, Wisconsin, the Menominee tribe met in general council with commissioner Francis Huebschmann and agreed to cede "a tract of land, not to exceed two townships in extent," to be selected in the western part of their reservation on its south line. It was to be a place with no permanent Menominee settlements, for locating the Stockbridge and Munsee Indians and other "New York Indians." The agreement came six days after the Stockbridge-Munsee treaty at Stockbridge.

The preamble states the purpose: "the United States desire to locate said Stockbridges and Munsees near the said line in the western part of the said reservation." The United States agreed to pay 60 cents an acre, if the New York Indians were located there. The money was to be spent on Menominee improvements in the way the 1854 Treaty of Wolf River set for the $40,000 earlier set aside for their removal.

The Stockbridge-Munsee Community's timeline dates its Shawano County reservation to the 1856 treaties and records that lumber barons claimed part of the reservation in 1871. The Menominee kept the rest of their Wolf River reservation, which the 1854 treaty had established.
