On April 19, 1858, in Washington, Commissioner Charles E. Mix signed a treaty with chiefs and delegates of the Yankton Sioux. They ceded "all the lands now owned, possessed, or claimed by them, wherever situated, except four hundred thousand acres." The Supreme Court describes this as renouncing a claim to more than 11 million acres of aboriginal lands. The reserve lay on the Missouri River in what is now Charles Mix County.

The National Park Service says Struck-by-the-Ree, the Yankton leader, told his people after returning from Washington: "The white men are coming in like maggots. It is useless to resist them." The Park Service gives about 11.5 million acres in exchange for approximately $1.6 million in annuities over 50 years and a 475,000-acre reservation.

The Senate ratified the treaty on February 16, 1859, per the Park Service. Under the 1887 Dawes Act individual members received allotments and the government negotiated for the surplus. In South Dakota v. Yankton Sioux Tribe (1998) the Supreme Court held that Congress intended its 1894 Act to diminish the reservation, so a landfill site was not Indian country.
