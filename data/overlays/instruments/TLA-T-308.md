On June 19, 1858, in Washington, Commissioner Charles E. Mix signed a treaty with the chiefs and braves of the Mdewakanton and Wahpekute bands of the Dakota. Article 1 made the part of their reservation south of the Minnesota River a reservation, to be surveyed, with 80 acres allotted to each head of a family and the rest held in common. The preamble recites that the 1851 treaty had set aside a strip ten miles wide on either side of the river, and that the Senate struck that provision from the treaty, so the bands were living on the tract only by Presidential permission.

Treaties Matter says "Dakota people were taken to Washington to sign away the northern half of their holdings along the Minnesota River." The ceded land was to be sold to settlers, with up to $140,000 going to pay the Dakota people's "just debts." Railroad promoters and land speculators had interests in the strip.

After the Dakota War of 1862, Treaties Matter says, "the U.S. abrogated all of its treaties with the Dakota, seized the remaining 10-mile wide strip, and exiled them from the State." Dakota lands in Minnesota were restored in part beginning in the 1880s.
