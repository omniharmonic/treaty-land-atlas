On June 19, 1858, in Washington, Commissioner Charles E. Mix signed a treaty with chiefs and headmen of the Sisseton and Wahpeton bands of the Dakota. As in the companion treaty with the Mdewakanton and Wahpekute, Article 1 made the part of their 1851 reservation lying south of the Minnesota River a reservation, to be surveyed, with 80 acres allotted to each head of a family. The preamble recounts that the Senate had amended the 1851 treaty, striking the provision that set the land aside as a home. Article 8 allowed members of the bands to dissolve tribal connections.

Treaties Matter explains that railroad interests and land speculators were drawn to the narrow strip along the river, and that "Dakota people were taken to Washington to sign away the northern half of their holdings along the Minnesota River," with the ceded land to be sold to settlers and up to $140,000 of proceeds paying the Dakota's "just debts."

In 1862, Treaties Matter says, the United States abrogated its treaties with the Dakota after the war and exiled them from Minnesota. A treaty in 1863 with non-combatant Dakota created the Sisseton reservation in present-day South Dakota.
