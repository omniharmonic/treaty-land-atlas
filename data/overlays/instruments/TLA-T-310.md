On April 15, 1859, in Washington, Commissioner Charles E. Mix signed a treaty with Baptiste Lassalleur and other Winnebago (Ho-Chunk) chiefs and delegates. The treaty says "The Winnebago Indians having now more lands than are necessary for their occupancy and use" wished to abolish common ownership. The eastern part of their reservation would be assigned in severalty: up to 80 acres to each head of a family and up to 40 acres to each male over 18. Certain lands could be sold, and the Winnebago's debts were to be paid from the proceeds.

MNopedia says the Ho-Chunk, with old annuities expired, needed money to pay off their debts, and that Mankato-area settlers were jubilant but called for their complete removal. By 1861 the treaty had not been ratified.

After the 1862 U.S.-Dakota War, which the Ho-Chunk did not join, a federal act of February 21, 1863 authorized their exile from Minnesota. In May 1863, "under threat of military force," 1,945 were moved to Crow Creek, South Dakota. More than 550 died, MNopedia reports. They later moved to a reservation in Nebraska.
