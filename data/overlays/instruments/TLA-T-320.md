On June 24, 1862, at Washington, Commissioner William P. Dole signed a treaty with a chief and three councilmen of the united Ottawa bands of Blanchard's Fork and Roche de Boeuf, then living in Franklin County, Kansas. The treaty says the Ottawa "having become sufficiently advanced in civilization" wished to become U.S. citizens. Their organization and relations with the United States as a tribe were to be dissolved five years after ratification.

The reservation was to be surveyed into eighty-acre tracts. Heads of families and chiefs would receive 160 acres, other members eighty acres, and five sections were set aside to reward chiefs and headmen. Twenty thousand acres were to endow a school, and up to $15,000 of tribal debts could be paid.

The wording about "civilization" is the treaty's own, not a finding. The pages opened here do not describe how the allotments or the end of tribal status played out, so later events are not covered.
