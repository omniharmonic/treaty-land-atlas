On July 2, 1863, at Fort Bridger in Utah Territory, the United States and chiefs and warriors of the eastern bands of the Shoshone nation re-established peace. The treaty kept the routes of travel "forever free and safe" for emigrants, allowed military posts and settlements along them, and let the telegraph, overland stage and a future railway operate.

The treaty says the United States knew game had been driven away and destroyed along the routes and by "agricultural and mining settlements." In return it promised $10,000 a year for twenty years. The Shoshone acknowledged the annuities as "full compensation and equivalent for the loss of game." Article 4 records the boundaries of the Shoshone country as the nation described them, but leaves the western boundary undefined because no western Shoshone were present.

The text records no cession of title. Article 7 adds that nothing in it admits any greater Shoshone title than existed under Mexican law when the United States acquired the territory. It was ratified in March 1864 and proclaimed in June 1869. A later 1863 Box Elder treaty adopted its terms.
