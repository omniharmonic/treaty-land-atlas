On October 1, 1863, at Ruby Valley in Nevada Territory, the United States and chiefs of the western bands of the Shoshone signed a treaty of peace and friendship. The bands agreed to end depredations. The treaty made the routes of travel free, allowed military posts and station houses, and permitted the telegraph, stage lines and a railway.

It also allowed the Shoshone country to be prospected for gold, silver and other minerals, with mining and farming settlements and ranches "whenever they may be required." Article 5 records the boundaries as the bands described them. The President could later set aside reservations, and the United States promised annuities as compensation for game driven away.

The text does not say the bands ceded title. In United States v. Dann (1985), the Supreme Court described how the Indian Claims Commission held Western Shoshone aboriginal title extinguished and awarded $26 million. The Court held the claim was settled once the award was placed in a Treasury trust account. The Court noted the Western Shoshone refused to cooperate in distributing it.
