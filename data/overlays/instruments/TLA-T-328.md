The Tabeguache band of the Ute (Utah) people made this treaty with the United States on October 7, 1863, at the Tabeguache Agency at Conejos, Colorado Territory. Governor John Evans, Superintendent Michael Steck and two agents signed as commissioners.

The preamble first sets out the huge area the band claimed as their exclusive land and hunting grounds, in the words of the document. Article 2 then has the band "cede, convey, and relinquish all of their claims" to every other land in United States territory, except a smaller hunting ground reserved around the Uncompahgre and Gunnison Rivers. The treaty adds that it should not be read as giving the Ute any greater title than they had when the Territory was acquired from Mexico.

Article 3 gave the United States the right to build military posts, railroads and mail stations on the reserved hunting ground the band kept, and said any citizen of the United States could mine "in any part of the country hereby reserved" without interference.

In return, Article 8 promised ten years of $10,000 in goods and $10,000 in provisions each year, and Article 9 five stallions. Article 10 offered cattle and sheep to chiefs who began farming or herding. The treaty was ratified March 25, 1864, and proclaimed December 14, 1864.
