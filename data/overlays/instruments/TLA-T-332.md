On October 14, 1864, at Klamath Lake, Oregon, J. W. Perit Huntington and William Logan signed with chiefs and headmen of the Klamath, Modoc and Yahooskin band of Snake. The tribes ceded all the country they claimed, described by boundaries beginning at the 44th parallel on the Cascade summit. A reservation on the east shore of the middle Klamath Lake was set apart, and the tribes agreed to move there immediately after ratification.

White people could not live on it. The tribes kept the exclusive right to fish its streams and lakes and gather roots, seeds and berries. The United States reserved rights of way for roads and railroads. Payments were $8,000 a year for five years, then $5,000, then $3,000, plus $35,000 more. The Senate ratified in 1866, and the treaty was proclaimed in 1870.

In Oregon Department of Fish and Wildlife v. Klamath Indian Tribe (1985), the Supreme Court said the treaty ceded aboriginal title and secured the right to fish within the reservation. It held that a 1901 agreement diminished the reservation, which ended the tribe's special right to hunt and fish on the ceded lands outside it.
