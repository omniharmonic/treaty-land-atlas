On August 12, 1865, in the Sprague River Valley in Oregon, J. W. Perit Huntington, the superintendent of Indian affairs in Oregon, signed with chiefs and headmen of the Woll-pah-pe tribe of Snake Indians. The tribe was recorded as ceding and relinquishing the country it occupied. The treaty describes it as running from Snow Peak in the Blue Mountain range, near the heads of the Grande Ronde River and the north fork of the John Day River, south to Crooked River and Harney Lake.

The tribe was bound by the treaty to "remove forthwith" to the reservation set up by the October 1864 treaty with the Klamath, Modoc and Yahooskin Snake. No member could leave the reservation for any purpose without the written consent of the agent or superintendent. The tribe was also bound by the treaty not to sell arms or ammunition to any tribe hostile to the United States.

The United States agreed to spend $5,000 to fence, break up and cultivate land, and to supply seeds, tools, animals and first-year subsistence. It also promised $2,000 a year for five years and then $1,200 a year for ten years, and the use of the physician, mechanics, farmers, teachers, mills and schools provided under the 1864 treaty. The treaty calls for allotments to each family when the President judges the time right. The sources here give no historical context and do not trace later events.
