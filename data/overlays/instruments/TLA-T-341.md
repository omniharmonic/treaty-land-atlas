On October 14, 1865, on the Little Arkansas River, seven U.S. commissioners signed a treaty with the chiefs and headmen of the Arapaho and Cheyenne of the Upper Arkansas. The commissioners included John B. Sanborn, William S. Harney, Kit Carson and William W. Bent. Black Kettle signed as head chief.

Article 2 set apart a district along the Arkansas and Cimarron rivers for the tribes' "absolute and undisturbed use and occupation." The tribes did not have to settle there until the United States had cleared other Indians' claims to it. Until then they could range between the Arkansas and Platte rivers. They were bound by the treaty to give up claims elsewhere. The treaty names especially the country between the forks of the Platte and the Arkansas, which it says they "claim to have originally owned, and never to have relinquished the title thereto."

Article 6 records the United States' wish "to express its condemnation of, and, as far as may be, repudiate the gross and wanton out-rages" at Sand Creek, Colorado Territory, on November 29, 1864. The treaty itself says the bands attacked there were "at peace with the United States, and under its flag," and that property was destroyed or taken "by the United States troops." It offers 320 acres to each named chief, including Black Kettle, and 160 acres to each widow or person who lost a parent. It also promised payment for property destroyed or taken.

Article 9 abrogated all former treaties. The treaty also promised forty years of annual spending for the tribes. A later treaty, signed at Medicine Lodge in 1867, replaced the money and annuities promised here with yearly deliveries of goods. The sources here do not say what happened to these grants.
