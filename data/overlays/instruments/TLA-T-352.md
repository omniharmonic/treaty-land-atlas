On March 21, 1866, at Washington, the United States and the Seminole Nation signed a new treaty. Its preamble says the Seminoles made a treaty with the "so-called Confederate States" in 1861, which "incurred the liability of forfeiture of all lands and other property held by grant or gift of the United States." It says a treaty of peace was made at Fort Smith in 1865, and that the United States promised a further treaty to settle the consequences.

"In compliance with the desire of the United States to locate other Indians and freedmen thereon," the Seminoles ceded their entire domain, estimated at 2,169,080 acres, for $325,362, about fifteen cents an acre. The treaty has them take a 200,000-acre tract in the former Creek country, at fifty cents an acre ($100,000), deducted from the payment. Article 2 gave persons of African descent living among the Seminoles the rights of native citizens.

The Department of the Interior's 2022 testimony notes that each of the Five Tribes' 1866 treaties handled freedmen differently. In Seminole Nation v. United States (1942), the Supreme Court later considered the Nation's claims under its treaties; that case is separate from the land cession.
