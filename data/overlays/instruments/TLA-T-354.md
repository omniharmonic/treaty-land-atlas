On April 7, 1866, in Washington, the Bois Forte bands of Chippewas signed a treaty with the United States. They ceded and "forever relinquish[ed]" all their claim to lands east of the boundary line set by the 1854 treaty with the Chippewas of Lake Superior and the Mississippi. The treaty singles out the land "claimed and occupied by them at and near Lake Vermillion as a reservation." The band was also recorded as ceding claims to lands west of that line, or anywhere else in the United States.

In return, the United States agreed to set apart a new reservation within one year of ratification. It was to be "not less than one hundred thousand acres" in the Chippewa country. The treaty says to include a lake named Netor As-sab-a-co-na if practicable, and one township on the Grand Fork River at the mouth of Deer Creek.

The United States also agreed to pay $30,000 on ratification, to build a blacksmith shop, a school house, eight houses for chiefs and an agency building, and to spend set sums every year for twenty years. Annuities were to be paid on the reservation if practicable. Nine named people were given the right to select 160 or 80 acres of non-mineral land with patents, for services to the band. Article 7 abrogated inconsistent earlier treaties, and the band were recorded as giving up any claims for arrears of payments due under them. The sources here do not describe how the reservation was laid out or what became of the Lake Vermilion land.
