On June 14, 1866, at Washington, the United States and the Creek Nation signed a treaty. Its preamble says the Creeks made a treaty with the "so-called Confederate States" in 1861 and thereby made themselves "liable to forfeit" benefits they held from the United States. It adds that, "in view of said liabilities," the United States required a portion of their land to settle other Indians.

Article 3 ceded the west half of the Creek domain, estimated at 3,250,560 acres, at thirty cents an acre ($975,168), "to be sold to and used as homes for such other civilized Indians as the United States may choose to settle thereon." The eastern half would remain "forever set apart as a home for said Creek Nation." Article 2 gave persons of African descent the rights of native citizens.

In McGirt v. Oklahoma (2020), the Supreme Court cited this treaty as a restatement that the remaining land was a "reduced Creek reservation," and held the land remains Indian country for federal criminal law.
