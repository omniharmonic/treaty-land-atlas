On July 19, 1866, in Washington, the United States and the Cherokee Nation signed a new treaty. Article 1 declared void the "pretended treaty" the Cherokee Nation made with the "so-called Confederate States" on October 7, 1861, which the national council repudiated on February 18, 1863. Article 2 declared amnesty for crimes a Cherokee had committed against another Cherokee or a U.S. citizen before July 4, 1866.

Article 9 records that the Nation had abolished slavery in February 1863. It says freedmen, and free colored persons living in the country, and their descendants, "shall have all the rights of native Cherokees." Slave owners were to receive no pay for the slaves freed.

Article 16 let the United States settle friendly Indians in the Cherokee country west of the 96th meridian, up to 160 acres for each person. The Cherokee Nation would be paid a price agreed by the parties or fixed by the President, and its jurisdiction over each district would end forever once it was sold and occupied. Article 17 ceded, in trust to the United States, the Kansas land sold to the Cherokees under the 1835 treaty. It was to be appraised at not less than an average of $1.25 an acre and sold.

In The Cherokee Tobacco (December Term 1870), the Supreme Court held that a federal internal revenue law on tobacco applied in the Cherokee Nation despite the treaty. It said treaties with Indian nations "have no higher sanctity" than treaties with foreign nations, which a later act of Congress can override, so that the act "must prevail as if the treaty were not an element to be considered."
