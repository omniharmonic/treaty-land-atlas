On February 27, 1867, in Washington, the United States signed a treaty with the Pottawatomie tribe, represented by chiefs, braves and headmen including Mazhee, Mianco and Shawgwe. The preamble says the Pottawatomies "believe that it is for the interest of their tribe that a home should be secured for them in the Indian country south of Kansas." It says they had funds from land sales to buy such a reservation.

Article 1 provided for a commission to visit the Indian country with delegates from the tribes proposing to move. A Pottawatomie delegation could join to pick a site of up to thirty miles square. If the Secretary of the Interior approved it, the tract would be set apart for the tribe and patented to the Pottawatomie Nation. If the site were land bought from the Creeks, Seminoles or Choctaws, the price could not exceed the government's cost. The Prairie band would have no interest in it but would get its share of land-sale proceeds in money.

Article 2 said that, the Leavenworth, Pawnee and Western Railroad Company having failed to buy, the Atchison, Topeka and Santa Fé Railroad Company could buy the unallotted lands at one dollar an acre. Article 4 called for a register of members who wished to move or to stay and become U.S. citizens. The sources here do not describe the move or the tribe's own account.
