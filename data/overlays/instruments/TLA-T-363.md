On March 19, 1867, in Washington, special commissioner Louis V. Bogy and other U.S. officials signed a treaty with ten chiefs of the Chippewas of the Mississippi, led by Que-we-zance, or Hole-in-the-Day. The preamble explains the reason. The tract set apart for them in 1865 "is not adapted for agricultural purposes" for those who wanted to farm, while some of the bands wanted to stay on part of it and sell the rest.

In Article 1 the Chippewas ceded all their Minnesota lands secured under the 1865 treaty, except for a tract around Leech Lake, Cass Lake and Lake Winnibigoshish. In Article 2 the United States set apart a separate tract "for a farming region," in a square as nearly as possible. It was to include White Earth Lake and Rice Lake and contain thirty-six townships. Any part of it lying outside the 1865 reservation would count as part payment for the cession.

The treaty estimates the ceded land at about two million acres. The United States agreed to pay $5,000 for school buildings and $4,000 a year for ten years and as long as the President saw fit for schools. It also promised $10,000 for a sawmill with a gristmill, $5,000 to help build houses for those who moved, and $5,000 for cattle, horses and farm tools. The pages opened here do not trace what followed.
