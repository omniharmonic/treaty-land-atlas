On October 21, 1867, at Medicine Lodge Creek in Kansas, seven U.S. commissioners, including Nathaniel G. Taylor, William S. Harney and John B. Sanborn, signed with chiefs and headmen of the confederated Kiowa and Comanche. Article 2 set apart a reservation between the Washita River and the Red River for their "absolute and undisturbed use and occupation." Article 11 had the tribes "relinquish all right to occupy permanently the territory outside of their reservation," while keeping the right to hunt south of the Arkansas River "so long as the buffalo may range thereon." They also agreed not to oppose railroads.

Article 12 said no cession of reservation land was valid unless signed by at least three-fourths of all adult male Indians occupying it. Heads of families could select up to 320 acres to farm.

The Oklahoma Historical Society says the treaty settled the Southern Cheyenne, Arapaho, Comanche and Kiowa on reservations, and that tensions followed: buffalo hunters entered the Texas Panhandle and the Red River War broke out in 1874. Kansapedia says the Jerome Commission later obtained signatures for allotment, with fraud alleged. Lone Wolf v. Hitchcock (1903) held Congress could override the treaty.
