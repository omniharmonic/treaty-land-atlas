On May 7, 1868, at Fort Laramie, U.S. commissioners signed a treaty with Crow chiefs and headmen. Article 2 set apart a district of Montana Territory for the Crow's "absolute and undisturbed use and occupation." It ran from the 107th meridian along the mid-channel of the Yellowstone River to the 45th parallel. The Crow agreed to relinquish all title, claims or rights to any other part of the territory of the United States. The United States agreed to build an agency, a physician's residence, a school and other buildings on the south side of the Yellowstone near Otter Creek.

Article 4 made the reservation the Crow's permanent home. It also gave them "the right to hunt on the unoccupied lands of the United States so long as game may be found thereon," so long as peace holds on the borders of the hunting districts.

The Supreme Court, in Herrera v. Wyoming (2019), described the treaty as a promise given "in exchange for most of the Tribe's territory in modern-day Montana and Wyoming." It held that the Crow hunting rights "did not expire upon Wyoming's statehood." In Montana v. United States (1981), the Court held that whatever property rights the 1868 treaty created, its language was "not strong enough" to overcome the presumption against conveying the Big Horn riverbed.
