On May 7, 1868, at Fort Laramie, U.S. commissioners signed with Crow chiefs and headmen. Article 2 set apart a district in Montana Territory, between the 107th meridian, the Yellowstone River and the 45th parallel, for the Crow's "absolute and undisturbed use and occupation." The Crow relinquished all title and claims to any other part of the territory of the United States. Agency buildings and a sawmill were to be built near Otter Creek.

Article 4 made the reservation their permanent home but kept their "right to hunt on the unoccupied lands of the United States so long as game may be found thereon" and peace holds on the borders of the hunting districts.

The Supreme Court, in Herrera v. Wyoming (2019), describes the treaty as given "in exchange for most of the Tribe's territory in modern-day Montana and Wyoming," and held that the hunting right did not expire when Wyoming became a state. In Montana v. United States (1981), the Court held that the 1868 treaty was not strong enough to convey the Big Horn riverbed to the Tribe.
