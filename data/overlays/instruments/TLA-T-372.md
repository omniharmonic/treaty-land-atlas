On June 1, 1868, at Fort Sumner, New Mexico, Lieutenant General William T. Sherman and Colonel Samuel F. Tappan signed a treaty with the Navajo Nation, represented by its chiefs and headmen. The Supreme Court says that in the years of war before it, "the United States forcibly moved many Navajos from their original homeland to a relatively barren area in New Mexico known as the Bosque Redondo Reservation."

Article 1 provided that "all war between the parties" would cease. Article 2 set apart a reservation bounded on the north by the 37th parallel of north latitude. It was to include the whole of Canon de Chelly. Article 12 set aside $50,000 for the cost of removing the tribe from Bosque Redondo, and money to buy 15,000 sheep and goats. In Article 9 the Navajo agreed to relinquish "all right to occupy any territory outside their reservation," while keeping the right to hunt on unoccupied adjoining lands. In Article 13 they agreed to make the reservation their permanent home.

In Arizona v. Navajo Nation (2023), the Court noted that the new reservation "would be on the Navajos' original homeland, not the Bosque Redondo Reservation," and that it spans some 17 million acres today. It held that the treaty reserved the water needed for the reservation but did not require the United States to take affirmative steps to secure water for the Tribe.
