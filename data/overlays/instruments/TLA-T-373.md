On July 3, 1868, at Fort Bridger, U.S. commissioners signed with chiefs of the eastern Shoshone and Bannock. Article 2 set apart the Wind River district in Wyoming for the Shoshone, while the Bannock would receive a reservation selected in their country, including the Portneuf and "Kansas Prairie" countries, when they wanted one or the President decided. Both tribes relinquished claims to other U.S. territory. They kept the right to hunt on unoccupied lands of the United States so long as game may be found there and peace holds.

In Shoshone Tribe v. United States (1937), the Supreme Court says the 1868 treaty had the Shoshone relinquish a reservation of 44,672,000 acres in Colorado, Utah, Idaho and Wyoming for one of 3,054,182 acres.

In Ward v. Race Horse (1896), the Court held that the hunting right ended when Wyoming became a state. In Herrera v. Wyoming (2019), the Court later held a Crow hunting right with identical wording survived statehood.
