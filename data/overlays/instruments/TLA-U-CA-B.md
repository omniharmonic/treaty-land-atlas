On May 30, 1851, at Camp Keyes on the Cah-wai River, commissioner George W. Barbour signed a treaty of peace and friendship with the chiefs, captains and head men of seven tribes. They were the Ko-ya-te, Wo-la-si, Nu-chow-we, Wack-sa-che, Pal-wisha, Po-ken-welle and Ya-wil-chine. Article 3 set apart two tracts to be "forever held for the sole use and occupancy" of the tribes. One began on the Cah-wai River and the other at the northwest end of Tulare or Tache Lake near the mouth of King's River. In Article 4 the tribes would "forever quit claim" to the government to any and all other lands to which they had ever had any claim or title.

Article 5 promised 200 beef cattle and 200 sacks of flour for each of 1851 and 1852. Article 6 promised, after ratification, brood mares, cows and other livestock, tools and clothing. Article 7 promised a farmer, a blacksmith, a wood worker and teachers.

None of it took effect. It was one of 18 treaties made in California in 1851 and 1852. The National Archives' Prologue magazine says the senators found the commissioners' appointments irregular, were unsure whether Mexico had recognized native land title, and heard strong objections from white Californians. The Senate "rejected the treaties and, following Senate rules, imposed an injunction of secrecy on them." With the treaties rejected, Indian title was left unresolved. Prologue adds that Indians of California later sought compensation for lands taken without payment in a case before the Indian Claims Commission. It reports that "the case was decided in favor of the Indians."
