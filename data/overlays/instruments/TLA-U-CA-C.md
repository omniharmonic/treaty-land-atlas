On June 3, 1851, at Camp Burton on Paint Creek, commissioner George W. Barbour signed a treaty of peace and friendship with the chiefs, captains and head men of the Chu-nute, Wo-wol, Yo-lum-ne and Co-ye-tie. Article 3 set apart two districts to be held forever for the tribes' sole use and occupancy. The Chu-nute and Wo-wol would have the land between the head of Tulare Lake and Kern or Buena Vista Lake. The Yo-lum-ne and Co-ye-tie would have the land between the Tule River and Paint Creek and the Sierra Nevada. The United States and California kept the right of way and the right to build military posts. In return the tribes were bound by the treaty to quit claim to all other lands.

Article 4 promised 200 beef cattle a year for two years, and after ratification cows, bulls, ploughs, work oxen, tools and seed. Article 5 promised each district a farmer, a blacksmith, a wood worker and teachers, with a school-house built at government cost.

None of it took effect. It was one of 18 treaties made in California in 1851 and 1852. The National Archives' Prologue magazine says the senators found the commissioners' appointments irregular, were unsure whether Mexico had recognized native land title, and heard strong objections from white Californians. The Senate "rejected the treaties and, following Senate rules, imposed an injunction of secrecy on them." With the treaties rejected, Indian title was left unresolved. Prologue adds that Indians of California later sought compensation for lands taken without payment in a case before the Indian Claims Commission. It reports that "the case was decided in favor of the Indians."
