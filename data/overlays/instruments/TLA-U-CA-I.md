On September 9, 1851, at Camp Colus on the Sacramento River, Indian agent O. M. Wozencraft made a treaty of peace and friendship with the chiefs, captains and head men of eight tribes. They included the Colus, Willays, Co-ha-na and Tat-nah. In Article 1 the tribes acknowledged the United States "to be the sole and absolute sovereign of all the soil and territory" ceded by Mexico. Article 3 set apart districts "forever" for their use and occupancy. They began on the east bank of the Sacramento at the point where the northern line of Sutter's claim is said to strike the river.

Article 4 promised 250 head of beef cattle and 75 sacks of flour within two years, to help the tribes while they moved and made allotments on the reservation. The treaty has no quit-claim article.

None of it took effect. It was one of 18 treaties made in California in 1851 and 1852. The National Archives' Prologue magazine says the senators found the commissioners' appointments irregular, were unsure whether Mexico had recognized native land title, and heard strong objections from white Californians. The Senate "rejected the treaties and, following Senate rules, imposed an injunction of secrecy on them." With the treaties rejected, Indian title was left unresolved. Prologue adds that Indians of California later sought compensation for lands taken without payment in a case before the Indian Claims Commission. It reports that "the case was decided in favor of the Indians."
