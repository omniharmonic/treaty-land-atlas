At the village of Temecula, California, Indian agent O. M. Wozencraft concluded a "treaty of peace and friendship" with the captains and head men of the San Luis Rey nation, the Kah-we-as and the Co-com-cah-ras. The date in the input data is January 5, 1852. The signers acknowledged the United States as "sole and absolute sovereign" of the territory Mexico had ceded, and agreed to stay on a described district of country in southern California. The United States kept all minerals there. The text also has the nations promise never to claim any other lands within the boundaries of the United States.

The United States wanted to settle Indian peoples on defined reserves in exchange for title. The treaty promised beef cattle and flour for two years and later gifts of clothing, tools and livestock. The National Archives' Prologue magazine says the 18 California treaties of 1851 and 1852 were made through three federal commissioners.

None of it took effect. The Senate rejected all 18 in closed session and imposed secrecy. Prologue says senators questioned the commissioners' appointments and Mexican recognition of native land title, and white Californians objected. Without a ratified treaty, Indian title was left unresolved. Small reservations followed by executive order and an 1891 act.
