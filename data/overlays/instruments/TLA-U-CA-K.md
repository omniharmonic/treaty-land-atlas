On January 5, 1852, at the village of Temecula, Indian agent O. M. Wozencraft made a treaty of peace and friendship with the captains and head men of the San Louis Rey nation, the Kah-we-as and the Co-com-cah-ras. In Article 1 they acknowledged the United States "to be the sole and absolute sovereign of all the soil and territory" ceded by Mexico. Article 3 set apart a district "forever" for their sole use and occupancy, "still reserving to the government of the United States all minerals found thereon." It began at the southwest corner of the San Jacinto grant. The nations also engaged that they "will never claim any other lands within the boundaries of the United States."

Article 4 promised 2,500 head of beef cattle and 350 sacks of flour within two years, to help the nations move to and settle on the reservation. The United States kept the right of way and the right to build military posts and agents' houses.

None of it took effect. It was one of 18 treaties made in California in 1851 and 1852. The National Archives' Prologue magazine says the senators found the commissioners' appointments irregular, were unsure whether Mexico had recognized native land title, and heard strong objections from white Californians. The Senate "rejected the treaties and, following Senate rules, imposed an injunction of secrecy on them." With the treaties rejected, Indian title was left unresolved. Prologue adds that Indians of California later sought compensation for lands taken without payment in a case before the Indian Claims Commission. It reports that "the case was decided in favor of the Indians."
