On January 7, 1852, at the village of Santa Ysabel, Indian agent O. M. Wozencraft made a treaty of peace and friendship with the captains and head men of the nation of Dieguino Indians. In Article 1 the tribes of the nation acknowledged the United States as "sole and absolute sovereigns" of the soil and territory ceded by Mexico. Article 3 set apart a district "forever" for the nation's sole use and occupancy, "still reserving to the government of the United States all minerals found thereon." It began at the southern line of the State at the eastern base of the Sierra Nevada and ran north along the base to the reservation set apart for the Kah-we-as, San Luis and Co-con-cah-ra nations.

The nation also engaged that it "will never claim any other lands within the boundaries of the United States." Article 4 promised 1,800 head of beef cattle and 350 sacks of flour within two years, to help the nation move to and settle on the reservation.

None of it took effect. It was one of 18 treaties made in California in 1851 and 1852. The National Archives' Prologue magazine says the senators found the commissioners' appointments irregular, were unsure whether Mexico had recognized native land title, and heard strong objections from white Californians. The Senate "rejected the treaties and, following Senate rules, imposed an injunction of secrecy on them." With the treaties rejected, Indian title was left unresolved. Prologue adds that Indians of California later sought compensation for lands taken without payment in a case before the Indian Claims Commission. It reports that "the case was decided in favor of the Indians."
