At the village of Santa Ysabel, California, Indian agent O. M. Wozencraft signed a "treaty of peace and friendship" with the captains and head men of the Dieguino nation. The input data dates it January 7, 1852. The tribes of the nation acknowledged the United States as "sole and absolute sovereigns" of the territory Mexico had ceded and promised to follow the laws and regulations of the Indian bureau. The treaty set aside a district in southern California, bounded in part by the Temecula reservation, for their "sole use and occupancy." The United States kept all minerals and the right to build military posts. The nation promised never to claim other lands.

The government wanted to concentrate Indian peoples on reserves and clear title. It promised beef cattle and flour for two years and later goods, tools and livestock.

The Senate rejected this treaty with the other 17 California treaties of 1851 and 1852. The National Archives' Prologue magazine says senators found the commissioners' appointments irregular, doubted Mexican recognition of native land title, and heard objections from white Californians. The treaties were placed under an injunction of secrecy. Small reservations came later by executive order and an 1891 act.
