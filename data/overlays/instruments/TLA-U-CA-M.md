On March 19, 1851, at Camp Fremont, near the Little Mariposa River, Redick McKee, George W. Barbour and Oliver M. Wozencraft, acting for the United States, signed a treaty of peace and friendship with chiefs, captains and head men of the Si-yan-te, Po-to-yun-te, Co-co-noon, Apang-as-se, Aplache and A-wal-a-che tribes. It was never ratified, and it is one of 18 negotiated in California in 1851 and 1852. Article 2 has the tribes relinquish and quit claim to the United States all right, title, claim or interest in any lands within California or the United States. Article 3 sets aside the district between the Merced and Tuolumne Rivers, and Article 4 promises 100 steers and 100 sacks of flour in each of 1851 and 1852.

The National Archives' Prologue says these treaties aimed to extinguish Native title: the Indians ceded title and accepted reservations, and the government pledged to pay and to set the reservations aside. The commissioners assumed the groups were tribes; today most are known to have been villages.

The Senate rejected all 18 treaties in closed session in 1852 and imposed an injunction of secrecy. Indian title was left unresolved. The Indian Claims Commission later decided in favor of California Indians who sought compensation for lands taken without payment.
