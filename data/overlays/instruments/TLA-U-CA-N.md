On April 29, 1851, at Camp Barbour, on the San Joaquin River, Redick McKee and others, acting for the United States, signed a treaty of peace and friendship with chiefs, captains and head men of sixteen tribes or bands under three principal chiefs (the How-ech-ees, Chook-cha-nees and others). It was never ratified, and it is one of 18 negotiated in California in 1851 and 1852. In Article 3 the tribes relinquish and quit claim all right and interest in lands or soil in California. Article 4 sets apart a district beginning on the Chonchille River for their sole use and occupancy. Article 5 promises 500 beef cattle and 260 sacks of flour while they moved to the reservation.

The National Archives' Prologue says these treaties aimed to extinguish Native title: the Indians ceded title and accepted reservations, and the government pledged to pay and to set the reservations aside. The commissioners assumed the groups were tribes; today most are known to have been villages.

The Senate rejected all 18 treaties in closed session in 1852 and imposed an injunction of secrecy. Indian title was left unresolved. The Indian Claims Commission later decided in favor of California Indians who sought compensation for lands taken without payment.
