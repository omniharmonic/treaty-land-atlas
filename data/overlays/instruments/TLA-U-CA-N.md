On April 29, 1851, at Camp Barbour on the San Joaquin River, commissioners Redick McKee, George W. Barbour and O. M. Wozencraft made a treaty with sixteen tribes or bands. They acknowledged three principal chiefs: Nai-yak-qua, Tom-quit and Pas-qual. In Article 3 the tribes agreed to "relinquish, and forever quit claim" to the United States all right, title, claim or interest in lands or soil in California. Article 4 set apart a district beginning at the Chonchille River, "for the sole use and occupancy" of the tribes. The tribes could hunt and gather in the country east of it, but could not sell their land except to the United States.

Article 5 promised 500 head of beef cattle and 260 sacks of flour during each of 1851 and 1852. Article 6 promised further goods after ratification.

None of it took effect. It was one of 18 treaties made in California in 1851 and 1852. The National Archives' Prologue magazine says the senators found the commissioners' appointments irregular, were unsure whether Mexico had recognized native land title, and heard strong objections from white Californians. The Senate "rejected the treaties and, following Senate rules, imposed an injunction of secrecy on them." With the treaties rejected, Indian title was left unresolved. Prologue adds that Indians of California later sought compensation for lands taken without payment in a case before the Indian Claims Commission. It reports that "the case was decided in favor of the Indians."
