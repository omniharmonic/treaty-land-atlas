On August 20, 1851, at Camp Lu-pi-yu-ma, on the south side of Clear Lake, Redick McKee, acting for the United States, signed a treaty of peace and friendship with chiefs, captains and head men of the Ca-la-na-po, Ha-bi-na-po, Da-no-ha-bo and other tribes. It was never ratified, and it is one of 18 negotiated in California in 1851 and 1852. In Article 3 the tribes relinquish, cede and quit claim to the United States all their right and interest in lands or soil in California. Article 4 appropriates a tract beginning where a spur from Mount McKee reaches Clear Lake as an Indian reservation, which could also receive other tribes later removed from the Russian River valley or elsewhere.

The National Archives' Prologue says these treaties aimed to extinguish Native title: the Indians ceded title and accepted reservations, and the government pledged to pay and to set the reservations aside. The commissioners assumed the groups were tribes; today most are known to have been villages.

The Senate rejected all 18 treaties in closed session in 1852 and imposed an injunction of secrecy. Indian title was left unresolved. The Indian Claims Commission later decided in favor of California Indians who sought compensation for lands taken without payment.
