On October 6, 1851, at Camp Klamath, at the junction of the Klamath and Trinity Rivers, Redick McKee, acting for the United States, signed a treaty of peace and friendship with chiefs, captains and head men of the Poh-lik or lower Klamath, Peh-tsick or upper Klamath and Hoo-pah or Trinity River Indians. It was never ratified, and it is one of 18 negotiated in California in 1851 and 1852. Article 3 has the tribes relinquish and cede their interest in lands in California. Article 4 appropriates a tract beginning at John's Creek on the Trinity River as a reservation, and Article 5 has them move onto it within three years or when required. Article 7 supplies tools to rebuild houses recently destroyed by whites.

The National Archives' Prologue says these treaties aimed to extinguish Native title: the Indians ceded title and accepted reservations, and the government pledged to pay and to set the reservations aside. The commissioners assumed the groups were tribes; today most are known to have been villages.

The Senate rejected all 18 treaties in closed session in 1852 and imposed an injunction of secrecy. Indian title was left unresolved. The Indian Claims Commission later decided in favor of California Indians who sought compensation for lands taken without payment.
