On November 4, 1851, Indian agent Redick McKee signed a "treaty of peace and friendship" at a camp in Scott's Valley, Shasta County, California. Six named bands of Upper Klamath, Shasta and Scott's River people, from villages the text counts as 24, 19 and 7, put themselves under the "exclusive jurisdiction, authority and protection" of the United States. They agreed to give up claims to "all other lands or soil in California." In return the treaty would have set aside a reservation around Scott's Valley.

The United States made 18 such treaties in California in 1851 and 1852 through three commissioners. The National Archives' Prologue magazine explains that treaties of this kind typically required Indians to reduce their land holdings or move to areas that were not desired by whites. The commissioners treated the groups as tribes, although Prologue notes that most were simply villages.

The treaty never took effect. Prologue reports that senators found the commissioners' appointments irregular, doubted whether Mexico had recognized native land title, and heard strong objections from white Californians. The Senate rejected all 18 and placed them under an injunction of secrecy. Indian title was left unresolved. Small reservations came later through executive orders and an 1891 act of Congress.
