On March 30, 1867, in Washington, the United States and the Emperor of Russia signed a treaty by which Russia ceded Alaska. The price was $7.2 million in gold. The Statutes at Large record that it was ratified on May 28, 1867, and proclaimed on June 20, 1867. The parties were the two governments; no Alaska Native nation signed.

Article III let inhabitants of the ceded territory return to Russia or stay. Those who stayed would be admitted to the rights of U.S. citizens "with the exception of uncivilized native tribes." The treaty said those tribes would be subject to such laws and regulations as the United States might adopt for aboriginal tribes of that country.

In Tee-Hit-Ton Indians v. United States (1955), a group of 60 to 70 Alaska Indians, a clan of the Tlingit, claimed compensation for timber taken from land in the Tongass National Forest. The Court of Claims held the group's interest was an Indian right of occupancy that Congress had never recognized. The Supreme Court affirmed, holding that "the taking by the United States of unrecognized Indian title is not compensable under the Fifth Amendment."

In 1971 Congress passed the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act. It declared that all aboriginal titles and claims based on use and occupancy in Alaska, including hunting or fishing rights, "are hereby extinguished."
