On March 30, 1867, Russia and the United States signed a treaty by which Russia ceded Alaska for $7.2 million in gold. Alaska Native peoples were not parties. Article III let inhabitants stay and receive U.S. citizenship rights, "with the exception of uncivilized native tribes." It added that the "uncivilized tribes will be subject to such laws and regulations as the United States may from time to time adopt in regard to aboriginal tribes of that country."

The treaty did not set reservations or recognize Native title. In Tee-Hit-Ton Indians v. United States (1955), the Court of Claims held the Tlingit group's interest was "original Indian title" and that, even if it survived the 1867 treaty, it was not a sufficient basis for suit without recognition by Congress. The Supreme Court affirmed, holding that taking unrecognized Indian title is not compensable under the Fifth Amendment.

In the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act of 1971, Congress extinguished "all aboriginal titles" and claims based on use and occupancy in Alaska, including aboriginal hunting or fishing rights.
