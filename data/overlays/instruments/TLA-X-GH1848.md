This is a treaty between the United States and Mexico, not with a Native nation. It was signed on February 2, 1848. By its terms, according to the National Archives, Mexico ceded 55 percent of its territory, including the present-day states of California, Nevada, Utah, New Mexico, most of Arizona and Colorado, and parts of Oklahoma, Kansas and Wyoming.

The United States paid Mexico $15 million and agreed to pay American citizens' claims against Mexico. The Senate ratified the treaty 34 to 14 on March 10, 1848, after removing Article X. Article XI bound the United States to restrain raids by Indians living inside the new U.S. boundary into Mexico, and said the government must take special care not to place its Indian occupants under the necessity of seeking new homes.

Native nations were not parties, but the transfer shaped their status. The 1849 Navajo treaty text recites that, by virtue of this treaty, the Navajo were lawfully placed under the exclusive jurisdiction and protection of the United States. The National Archives's Prologue notes that it was unclear whether Mexico had recognized Native land titles in California.
