The Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act (ANILCA), Public Law 96-487, was signed December 2, 1980. It is a large lands law. The Department of the Interior says it "established more than 100 million additional acres of Federal land in Alaska," raising federal conservation holdings there to more than 131 million acres.

For Alaska Natives, the key section is Title VIII. Congress found that "the continuation of the opportunity for subsistence uses by rural residents of Alaska, including both Natives and non-Natives" on public lands and "by Alaska Natives on Native lands is essential to Native physical, economic, traditional, and cultural existence." It said it needed to act "in order to fulfill the policies and purposes of the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act and as a matter of equity."

Section 804 (16 U.S.C. 3114) gives the taking of fish and wildlife for "nonwasteful subsistence uses" priority over other uses on public lands. When limits are needed, the criteria are customary and direct dependence, local residency and the availability of alternative resources. The priority rests on rural residency, not Native status.

Interior says Alaska ran the program until 1989, when the Alaska Supreme Court held in McDowell v. State of Alaska that a priority based on rural residency violated the state constitution, and that the federal government has managed subsistence on federal public lands and waters since 1990.
