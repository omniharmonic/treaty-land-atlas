The Indian Citizenship Act was approved on June 2, 1924. It is one short paragraph in the Statutes at Large, under the title "An Act To authorize the Secretary of the Interior to issue certificates of citizenship to Indians." Its text declares that "all non-citizen Indians born within the territorial limits of the United States be, and they are hereby, declared to be citizens of the United States."

A proviso protects tribal property: "the granting of such citizenship shall not in any manner impair or otherwise affect the right of any Indian to tribal or other property." Today's citizenship statute, 8 U.S.C. 1401(b), carries the same idea, saying citizenship for a person born to a member of an Indian, Eskimo, Aleutian or other aboriginal tribe does not impair the right to tribal or other property.

Citizenship did not by itself guarantee a vote. The Library of Congress notes that earlier laws had tied citizenship to land allotments, including the 1887 Dawes Act and the 1906 Burke Act, and that a 1919 act gave citizenship to Native veterans of World War I. It quotes a 1929 Senate document saying Native citizens could be denied the vote if they failed state registration, poll tax or education requirements. It adds that "well over 40 years passed before every state complied" with the 1924 Act and enfranchised Native Americans.
