The Indian Civil Rights Act was approved April 11, 1968. Title II, now 25 U.S.C. 1302, bars an Indian tribe "in exercising powers of self-government" from violating a list of protections, such as unreasonable searches, double jeopardy and denial of equal protection or due process. It does not include everything in the Bill of Rights. The Supreme Court noted in Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez (1978) that "the statute does not prohibit the establishment of religion, nor does it require jury trials in civil cases."

Original Section 202 capped tribal criminal punishment at six months or $500. The 1986 amendment raised it to one year and $5,000, and a 2010 law lets tribal courts impose up to three years or $15,000 for certain defendants. Section 203 extends the writ of habeas corpus to test "the legality of his detention by order of an Indian tribe."

Title IV changed how states could take over criminal and civil jurisdiction in Indian country. States could assume it only "with the consent of the Indian tribe occupying the particular Indian country." The same title repealed section 7 of the 1953 Act (Public Law 280), though earlier cessions of jurisdiction under that section were not affected.

In Santa Clara Pueblo the Court held that the Act's only express remedy is habeas corpus and that tribal sovereign immunity bars suits against the tribe under it. It reasoned that Congress was committed to tribal self-determination.
