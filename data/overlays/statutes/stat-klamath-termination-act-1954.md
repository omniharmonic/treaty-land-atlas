The Klamath Termination Act of August 13, 1954 (Public Law 587) set out to end "Federal supervision over the trust and restricted property" of the Klamath Tribe, made up of the Klamath and Modoc Tribes and the Yahooskin Band of Snake Indians. It also ended federal services furnished to them "because of their status as Indians."

The law pushed members to choose. Each adult could elect to withdraw from the tribe and have a share of tribal property "converted into money and paid to him," or remain in the tribe under a management plan. The Secretary of the Interior was to appraise the tribal property and sell enough of it to pay out those who withdrew.

The Act did not erase every treaty right. It says that nothing in it "shall abrogate any fishing rights or privileges of the tribe or the members thereof enjoyed under Federal treaty."

The Klamath Tribes' own history says tribal representatives consistently opposed termination, that a Bureau of Indian Affairs report concluded the Tribes were not ready for it, and that their reservation land base of approximately 1.8 million acres was taken by condemnation. The Tribes' history gives that figure; other sources give different acreages.

Congress reversed course in the Klamath Indian Tribe Restoration Act, Public Law 99-398, approved August 27, 1986. It extended federal recognition to the tribe and restored rights under any federal treaty, order, agreement or statute that termination had diminished or lost.
