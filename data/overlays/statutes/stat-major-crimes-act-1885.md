In 1883 the Supreme Court decided Ex parte Crow Dog. Crow Dog, a Brule Sioux, had been sentenced to death by a Dakota district court for killing Spotted Tail, also a Brule Sioux, in Indian country. The Court asked whether an existing statute that excluded such crimes from United States jurisdiction had been repealed. It found no clear expression of congressional intent to repeal it, held the trial court "without jurisdiction," and ordered the writs of habeas corpus issued.

In the Indian Appropriation Act of March 3, 1885, Congress added a section, section 9, that went the other way. It provided that Indians who commit murder, manslaughter, rape, assault with intent to kill, arson, burglary or larceny against another Indian or other person, within a Territory, "either within or without an Indian reservation," would be subject to the laws of that Territory and tried in the same courts as everyone else.

In United States v. Kagama (1886), the Supreme Court held that section valid and constitutional. It reasoned that, from the Indians' weakness and helplessness, "there arises the duty of protection, and with it the power."

The Act survives as 18 U.S.C. 1153, which today lists a longer set of offenses. It matters on the ground: in McGirt v. Oklahoma (2020) the Court held that land reserved for the Creek Nation remains "Indian country" for purposes of the Major Crimes Act.
