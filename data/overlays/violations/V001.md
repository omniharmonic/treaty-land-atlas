On September 17, 1778, at Fort Pitt, commissioners Andrew and Thomas Lewis signed articles of agreement with Delaware leaders White Eyes, John Kill Buck Jr. and Pipe. In Article VI the United States engaged "to guarantee to the aforesaid nation of Delawares, and their heirs, all their territorial rights in the fullest and most ample manner, as it hath been bounded by former treaties." The same article contemplated inviting other friendly tribes "to form a state whereof the Delaware nation shall be the head, and have a representation in Congress." It provided that nothing in the article was "conclusive until it meets with the approbation of Congress."

The clause was a proposal that depended on Congress's approval. The territorial guarantee met a different fate. In 1795 at Greeneville, General Anthony Wayne made a treaty with twelve tribes, the Delawares among them. In Article 3 the tribes were recorded as ceding "all their claims to the lands lying eastwardly and southwardly" of a boundary line that began at the mouth of the Cuyahoga River. Article 5 promised that the United States would protect the tribes in the quiet enjoyment of their lands against all U.S. citizens.

In 1866, at the Delaware Agency in Kansas, another treaty recited that Congress had made it the President's duty to provide for the removal of Kansas tribes. It said the Delawares "have expressed a wish" to move to the Indian country, and it let the Secretary of the Interior sell the remaining reservation to a railroad company or other buyers. The database lists the Delaware Nation and the Delaware Tribe of Indians as successors.
