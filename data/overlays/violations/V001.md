At Fort Pitt on September 17, 1778, the United States promised in Article VI to "guarantee to the aforesaid nation of Delawares, and their heirs, all their territorial rights," as bounded by earlier treaties. The promise lasted as long as the Delaware nation held to the friendship. The same article also said other friendly tribes might join and "form a state whereof the Delaware nation shall be the head," with representation in Congress. That idea was not "conclusive" until Congress approved.

The state never came into being, so that clause is best read as a proposal that lapsed. The land guarantee had a different fate. In the Treaty of Greenville (1795), the Delawares and other nations accepted a boundary line giving up much of Ohio. Article 5 of that treaty promised that the United States would "protect all the said Indian tribes in the quiet enjoyment of their lands" against its own citizens. Later treaties moved the Delawares farther west. In 1866 a treaty recited that Congress had made it the President's duty to provide for removing tribes from Kansas. It authorized the Secretary of the Interior to sell the Delaware reservation to a railroad company or other buyers.

The sources read here show no court case over the Fort Pitt guarantee. The 1866 treaty recited a Delaware wish to move to Indian Territory. Today the Delaware Tribe of Indians and the Delaware Nation, both in Oklahoma, are the successor nations listed by the treaty database.
