The Treaty of Hopewell (Nov. 28, 1785, 7 Stat. 18) set a boundary and, in Article V, said any citizen of the United States who tried to settle west or south of it, or who would not leave within six months, "shall forfeit the protection of the United States." The Cherokees could punish such a person "or not as they please." The article had one exception. It did not cover people already settled between the French Broad and Holston rivers, whose situation was left for Congress to decide.

That exception was where the trouble grew. The Tennessee Encyclopedia describes the unrecognized State of Franklin, founded by settlers in that region. In June 1785, it says, a "token number" of Cherokee chiefs signed the Dumplin Creek Treaty with Franklin, allowing settlement south of the French Broad River. Cherokee delegates then met United States commissioners at Hopewell and agreed a line north of Greeneville. The encyclopedia says the two conflicting treaties soon produced open warfare. The sources read here do not show the United States removing the settlers.

A new treaty settled the matter. The Treaty of Holston (July 2, 1791, 7 Stat. 39) drew a new boundary in Article IV. In Article VII the United States "solemnly guarantee to the Cherokee nation, all their lands not hereby ceded." The sources show no court remedy. The boundary was reset by treaty, not reversed.
