At Fort Stanwix on October 22, 1784, commissioners Oliver Wolcott, Richard Butler and Arthur Lee gave "peace to the Senecas, Mohawks, Onondagas and Cayugas" on conditions. Article II secured the Oneida and Tuscarora in the lands where they were settled. Article III drew a boundary line and said the Six Nations "shall and do yield to the United States, all claims to the country west of the said boundary." East and north of it they were to be secured in peaceful possession of their lands, except six miles square around the fort of Oswego.

The National Park Service says the federal negotiators "dictated the terms of the treaty" to the Six Nations, treating four of the nations as defeated enemies, which it says they were not. It says individuals selected by the federal representative "may have been coerced into signing." The Park Service adds that the United States ratified the treaty in 1785, "but the Six Nations never ratified the treaty." The New York Almanack reports that Six Nations leaders refused on the grounds the delegates lacked the authority to agree to the terms.

On January 9, 1789, at Fort Harmar, Governor Arthur St. Clair made a treaty with the Six Nations to "renew and confirm all the engagements and stipulations" made at Fort Stanwix. The database lists the Cayuga, Oneida, Onondaga, Seneca, Mohawk and Tuscarora nations among successors. The sources here show no court remedy for the 1784 cession.
