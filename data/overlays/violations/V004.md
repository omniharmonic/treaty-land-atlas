The Treaty of Greenville (August 3, 1795) drew a boundary between United States land and Indian land and gave up the area south and east of it, in exchange for goods and yearly payments. Article 5 promised that the United States "will protect all the said Indian tribes in the quiet enjoyment of their lands against all citizens of the United States." Lands could be sold only to the United States.

In 1809, Indiana Territory Governor William Henry Harrison negotiated the Treaty of Fort Wayne with Delaware, Potawatomi, Miami, Eel River and other leaders. The treaty ceded tracts along the Wabash and near the Greenville line, in return for yearly payments. Article 5 required the Wea tribe's separate consent for part of it. The National Library of Medicine says Harrison sought 3 million acres. It says the Shawnee leader Tecumseh told him the signers had no authority to sell land held in common with other peoples. Tecumseh then warned Americans not to settle on the land and organized fighters against incursions.

The sources read here do not show whether the Army enforced the Greenville line, so the claim that settlers crossed it unchecked is not confirmed. They also show no court case or later claim. The conflict continued, with the Battle of Tippecanoe in 1811, which these sources do not cover.
