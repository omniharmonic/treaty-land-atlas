Georgia passed laws extending its authority over the Cherokee Nation's territory and declaring Cherokee laws "null and void." Samuel Worcester, a missionary living there, was convicted under those laws and sentenced to four years of hard labor. He argued that treaties between the United States and the Cherokee recognized the nation's right to govern its territory, and that the Constitution makes treaties part of the supreme law of the land.

In Worcester v. Georgia (1832), the Supreme Court held that "the Cherokee nation, then, is a distinct community occupying its own territory," in which "the laws of Georgia can have no force." It called the Georgia act "void" and said the judgment against Worcester should be reversed. The New Georgia Encyclopedia reports that Georgia ignored the ruling and refused to release the missionaries. It says President Jackson "did not enforce the decision against the state," and instead urged the Cherokees to relocate or fall under Georgia's jurisdiction. That source says Jackson's widely quoted line about Marshall enforcing his decision does not match his actual words to a general.

In 1835 a dissident faction signed the New Echota treaty, and in 1838 the Army removed almost all Cherokees. In Oklahoma v. Castro-Huerta (2022), the Court said the view in Worcester "has yielded to closer analysis."
