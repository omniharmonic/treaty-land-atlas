The Treaty of Dancing Rabbit Creek (September 27, 1830; 7 Stat. 333; proclaimed February 24, 1831) opens by explaining why it was made. It says Mississippi had extended its laws over "persons and property within the chartered limits" of the Choctaw Nation, "and the President of the United States has said that he cannot protect the Choctaw people from the operation of these laws."

In Article 3 the Choctaw ceded "the entire country they own and possess, east of the Mississippi River." In Article 2 the United States promised land west of the river, "in fee simple," for as long as the Choctaw lived on it as a nation. Article 14 let a Choctaw head of family who wanted to stay and become a citizen claim a 640-acre section, but he had to tell the agent within six months of ratification.

The Encyclopedia of Oklahoma History says Choctaw leaders signed after President Jackson "pushed his Indian Removal Act through Congress in 1830." It says more than six thousand Choctaw reached Indian Territory in the winter of 1831-32, and that the removal "had been costly in terms of loss of life."

The Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians' own history says some 4,000 Choctaw chose not to leave, "despite a great deal of coercion." It says that by 1850 "virtually none of the Choctaw who had received land in Mississippi still retained it." This is context, not a court-decided violation.
