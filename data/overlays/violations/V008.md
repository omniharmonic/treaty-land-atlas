The Treaty of Dancing Rabbit Creek (Sept. 27, 1830; 7 Stat. 333) begins by explaining why it was made. It says Mississippi had extended its laws over "persons and property within the chartered limits" of the Choctaw Nation, "and the President of the United States has said that he cannot protect the Choctaw people from the operation of these laws." The Choctaw therefore agreed to sell their lands east of the Mississippi. Article 2 promised land west of the Mississippi, "in fee simple," for as long as they lived on it as a nation. Article 14 let a Choctaw head of family who wished to remain and become a citizen claim a 640-acre reservation, with smaller shares for children.

The Encyclopedia of Oklahoma History says Choctaw leaders signed the treaty after President Jackson "pushed his Indian Removal Act through Congress in 1830." It adds that more than six thousand Choctaw arrived in Indian Territory in the winter of 1831-32, and about five thousand more in 1832 and 1833.

Wikipedia, a tertiary source, says the treaty ceded about 11 million acres, that about 2,500 died on the journey, and that the 5,000 to 6,000 who stayed in Mississippi faced "legal conflict, harassment, and intimidation." These figures are not confirmed in a second source here. Whether Article 14 grants were actually delivered was not checked. The Choctaw Nation of Oklahoma, the Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians and the Jena Band of Choctaw Indians are listed as successors.
