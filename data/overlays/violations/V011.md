On October 26, 1832, a treaty on the Tippecanoe River reserved twenty-two sections of land in Indiana for Potawatomi bands. On August 5, 1836, near the Yellow River, United States commissioner Abel C. Pepper and three Potawatomi "chiefs and headmen," Pe-pin-a-waw, No-taw-kah and Mac-kah-tah-mo-ah, signed a new treaty (7 Stat. 505). In Article 1 they "cede to the United States twenty two sections of land reserved for them." The United States agreed to pay $14,080 in specie. In Article 3 the bands agreed to move west of the Mississippi within two years.

An Indiana Historical Society document quotes historian James H. Madison. He wrote that some Potawatomi resisted removal in 1838. Led by Menominee, they "claimed that they had not agreed to removal," and an armed militia company rounded up Menominee and his band.

The Citizen Potawatomi Nation says that from Sept. 4 to Nov. 4, 1838, "the United States forcibly removed a band of 859 Potawatomi" from northern Indiana to present-day Kansas. It says this was to make way for squatters who wanted Potawatomi lands. It gives the trip as 660 miles and says 41 Potawatomi died. Madison's account counts forty-two dead, most of them children. An Indiana Historical Bureau marker says some 800 people were collected near Twin Lakes in August 1838.

The treaty was proclaimed on February 18, 1837, so this is context for a contested cession and removal, not a court-found violation. The Citizen Potawatomi Nation says a remembrance is held every five years.
