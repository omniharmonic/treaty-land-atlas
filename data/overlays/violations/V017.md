In the Treaty of Canandaigua (1794), the United States acknowledged the land within set boundaries as the property of the Seneca Nation. Article III says the United States "will never claim the same, nor disturb the Seneka nation" in its "free use and enjoyment" of that land, which would stay theirs until they chose to sell it.

In the 1950s Congress funded the Kinzua Dam on the Allegheny River, a flood-control project for the Pittsburgh region. The Army Corps of Engineers began surveying Seneca land in 1956 over the Nation's objections. The Seneca argued in court that the treaty barred the taking and hired engineer Arthur E. Morgan, who proposed an alternative plan. A federal judge ruled that Congress had known it was overriding a treaty when it paid for the dam, and that a treaty "could not rise above the power of Congress to legislate." The Seneca's appeals failed. The judge relied on Lone Wolf v. Hitchcock (1903), which let Congress override Indian treaties.

According to an environmental-journalism history, the Nation lost nine communities and 10,000 acres of the Allegany Territory, and around 600 Seneca people lost their homes. A Bureau of Indian Affairs release says legislation in August 1964 awarded the Senecas $15 million for relocation and rehabilitation. That law also required a plan to end federal supervision of the Nation, which the Nation opposed and which did not happen. The dam still operates.
