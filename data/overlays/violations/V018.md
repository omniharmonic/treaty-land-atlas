In the Treaty of Canandaigua (1794), Article II acknowledged the reservations of the Oneida, Onondaga and Cayuga as their property. The United States said it "will never claim the same" and would not disturb them in its "free use and enjoyment," and the land would stay theirs until they chose to sell it to people of the United States. A federal law, the Nonintercourse Act of 1793, added a rule: no one could purchase Indian land without the federal government's approval.

In 1795 New York State negotiated with the Oneida and bought most of their remaining land, paid for in annual cash payments. According to the Supreme Court's 1985 opinion, Secretary of War Pickering warned New York's governors that federal commissioners had to oversee such deals. The trial court found that "no United States Commissioner or other official of the federal government was present." The State ignored the warnings, the Court says. The Oneida ancestors conveyed 100,000 acres in that 1795 agreement, according to the Court's 2005 opinion.

The Oneida sued in 1970. In County of Oneida v. Oneida Indian Nation (1985), the Supreme Court held that the Oneida could sue over the sale, based on federal common law, and rejected the counties' defenses. In City of Sherrill v. Oneida Indian Nation (2005), the Court ruled 8-1 that the Nation could not revive sovereignty over parcels it had bought back on the open market, citing the long passage of time.
