In the Treaty of Fort Laramie (Horse Creek), signed on September 17, 1851, the United States recognized the territories of the Plains nations, including a shared Cheyenne and Arapaho area. In Article 3, the United States bound itself "to protect the aforesaid Indian nations against the commission of all depredations by the people of the said United States." The nations in turn agreed that the United States could build roads and military posts. The U.S. Senate ratified the treaty with an amendment cutting the annuity term in Article 7 from fifty years to ten, subject to the tribes' acceptance. The Oklahoma State University database note says the assent of all tribes except the Crows was procured.

Gold was found near present-day Denver in 1858 and 1859 and, according to the National Park Service, more than one hundred thousand emigrants crossed the Plains. Many stayed and built settlements on land the 1851 treaty recognized as Cheyenne and Arapaho. The same NPS timeline says pressure on the government to acquire the land and remove the Indians increased. In February 1861 the United States formed Colorado Territory, much of it on land the 1851 treaty had recognized.

None of the sources opened here describe a federal effort to remove the settlers, and none record a remedy for the invasion. This record is context, not an adjudicated violation. Instead the Fort Wise treaty of February 1861 ceded all Cheyenne and Arapaho lands except a reserve on the Arkansas River.
