Article 12 of the Medicine Lodge treaty with the Kiowa and Comanche (concluded in 1867) said no cession of any part of their reservation would be valid "unless executed and signed by at least three-fourths of all the adult male Indians occupying the same."

On October 6, 1892, 456 male adult members of the confederated tribes signed an agreement with three U.S. commissioners. It provided for allotments to individuals and two million dollars for the leftover "surplus" land. In 1899 the Secretary of the Interior told the Senate that "less than three fourths of the male adults appear to have so signed." Plaintiffs also alleged fraud and that Congress amended the deal without resubmitting it to them. Congress gave the agreement effect in an act of June 6, 1900, which allotted land and opened the rest to settlement. The plaintiffs' bill describes the land opened to settlement as two million acres.

In Lone Wolf v. Hitchcock (decided January 5, 1903), the Supreme Court held that Congress had "plenary authority over the tribal relations of the Indians," that this power was political and not controlled by the courts, and that "the power exists to abrogate the provisions of an Indian treaty." The Court presumed Congress acted in good faith and said courts could not question its motives. The case remains the best-known precedent for Congress overriding Indian treaties.
