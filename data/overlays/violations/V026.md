In 1851, after the treaties of Mendota and Traverse des Sioux, the United States removed most of the Dakota people in Minnesota Territory to a reservation on either side of the Minnesota River, the Minnesota Historical Society (MNHS) says. The Treaty of Washington in 1858 confined them to a smaller area south of the river.

MNHS writes that the United States "failed to fulfill its treaty obligations." By the summer of 1862, it says, many Dakota on the reservation were starving: the corn crop had been meager, annuity payments were late, and traders refused credit. War broke out in August 1862. On November 5, after what MNHS calls rushed and unauthorized trials, a military commission sentenced 303 Dakota men to death. President Lincoln pardoned all but thirty-eight, who were hanged at Mankato on December 26, the largest mass hanging in American history. MNHS says about 1,600 Dakota non-combatants were imprisoned that winter in a concentration camp at Fort Snelling.

Then Congress passed the Act of February 16, 1863 (12 Stat. 652). Its preamble says the Sisseton, Wahpeton, Mdewakanton and Wahpekute bands had forfeited their annuities by making war. The Act declared their treaties "abrogated, and annulled" as to any future obligation on the United States. It forfeited their Minnesota lands and annuities to the United States, and said the money should help pay settlers' damage claims.

MNHS says the Dakota at the fort were removed in spring 1863 to a reservation in Dakota Territory and later Nebraska. The government let a few who had supported peace stay.
