In the Treaty with the Ponca (signed March 12, 1858; ratified March 8, 1859; 12 Stat. 997), the Ponca were recorded as ceding most of their homeland. In Article 2 the United States agreed "to protect the Poncas in the possession of the tract of land reserved for their future homes, and their persons and property thereon," during good behavior on their part. The National Park Service (NPS) says the reserved tract was about 58,000 acres.

The NPS says the United States "did little to protect them" from attacks by the Brule Sioux. When the government created the Great Sioux Reservation in 1868, "the Ponca Reservation was included within its boundaries, depriving them of title to their remaining lands." In 1877 the federal government decided to remove the Ponca to Indian Territory, and federal troops enforced the orders. The Encyclopedia of the Great Plains says no lands had been reserved for them in Indian Territory.

The NPS says that by the summer of 1878 one-third of the tribe had died and nearly all the survivors were sick or disabled. After his son died, Chief Standing Bear led a group home in early 1879. The Army arrested them, and in United States ex rel. Standing Bear v. Crook, Judge Elmer Dundy ruled on May 12, 1879 that they were "persons" under the law and could not be held. The Encyclopedia says the decision "provided no lands for them"; they secured allotments on their former reservation in 1890.
