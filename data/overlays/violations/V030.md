This entry is context for the atlas, not a court-decided treaty violation. The 1868 Treaty of Fort Laramie set up the Great Sioux Reservation "for the absolute and undisturbed use and occupation" of the Sioux, according to the treaty text. Harvard's Environmental and Energy Law Program (EELP) notes that the reservation's boundaries include significant portions of where the Dakota Access Pipeline now runs, and that the pipeline runs within a half-mile of the Standing Rock Reservation through land Congress took from the Tribe in 1958.

The Army Corps of Engineers controls only 37 miles of the 1,100-mile pipeline, where it crosses federal lands and waters. The Corps denied an easement for the Lake Oahe crossing on December 4, 2016, pending further review, and approved it on February 8, 2017, after a presidential memorandum directing an expedited review. Standing Rock Sioux Tribe sued. In 2020 a federal court ordered a full environmental impact statement and vacated the easement. In January 2021 the D.C. Circuit held that the Corps violated the National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA) by issuing the easement without that statement but reversed the shutdown order.

The Corps released the final environmental impact statement on December 19, 2025 and signed a Record of Decision on May 21, 2026 selecting Alternative 4, an easement with added conditions. The Tribe's challenge to the pipeline's ongoing operation was voluntarily dismissed on June 23, 2026. The pipeline continues to operate. The sources I read show the courts ruling on NEPA, not on treaty claims.
