The 1868 Fort Laramie Treaty said no cession of reservation land would be valid unless signed by "at least three-fourths of all the adult male Indians occupying or interested in the same." The Act of March 2, 1889 then set aside the Rosebud Reservation, which the Supreme Court says contained somewhat over 3.2 million acres in five South Dakota counties.

In 1901, Indian Inspector James McLaughlin obtained the written consent of three-fourths of adult men on Rosebud to cede about 416,000 acres of Gregory County for $1,040,000, but Congress never ratified it because it required the government to pay outright. In 1903 McLaughlin sought a new agreement with a different payment method; he got a majority but not three-fourths. Congress then passed acts in 1904, 1907 and 1910 opening unallotted land in Gregory, then Tripp and Lyman, then Mellette counties to homesteaders and towns. The Court called these "unilateral Acts of Congress without the consent of three-fourths" of the Tribe's adult male members, but said that fact did not decide whether Congress meant to shrink the boundaries.

In Rosebud Sioux Tribe v. Kneip (April 4, 1977), the Court held 6 to 3 that the language and history of the Acts "clearly" showed congressional intent to diminish the reservation's boundaries. Justice Marshall, joined by Justices Brennan and Stewart, dissented. The three Acts disposed of all unallotted land in Gregory, Tripp, Lyman and Mellette counties; only Todd County was unaffected, and under the ruling the opened areas are outside the reservation's boundaries.
