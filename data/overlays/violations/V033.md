These cases show that opening a reservation to settlers did not always shrink it. In each case the question was whether a later act of Congress took that land out of the reservation.

For the Cheyenne River Sioux, the Act of May 29, 1908 let the Secretary of the Interior "sell and dispose" of about 1.6 million acres to non-Indian homesteaders, with proceeds credited to the Tribe. For the Omaha, an 1882 Act authorized the Secretary to survey, appraise and sell roughly 50,000 acres west of a railroad right-of-way, opening it to settlement. Congress acted in both cases, and the land passed into many non-Indian hands.

The Supreme Court held in Solem v. Bartlett (1984) that the 1908 Act opened the land but did not diminish the reservation. It stated that "only Congress can divest a reservation of its land and diminish its boundaries," and it will not lightly infer that Congress did so. In Nebraska v. Parker (2016) the Court unanimously held that the 1882 Act did not diminish the Omaha Reservation, so the town of Pender remains within it. The Court said later demographics and the way the land was treated could not outweigh the text of the law.
