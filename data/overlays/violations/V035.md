The Treaty of Ruby Valley was signed on October 1, 1863, and proclaimed on October 21, 1869. Its title is "Peace and Friendship." In it the Western Bands of the Shoshone agreed to stop attacks on emigrants, mail and telegraph lines. Article 2 said routes of travel through their country "shall be forever free." Article 4 let the country be explored for gold and silver and used for mining and farming settlements. Article 5 lists the boundaries the bands claimed. Article 7 promised $5,000 a year for twenty years as compensation for lost game. The treaty text has no article that cedes land.

A 1994 statement to a United Nations working group by the Western Shoshone National Council, posted by the Center for World Indigenous Studies, says "the treaty is still today in full force and effect." That is the Council's argument, not a court finding.

Later the United States took a different path. The Indian Claims Commission decided that Western Shoshone aboriginal title had been extinguished in the late 19th century and awarded more than $26 million, according to the Supreme Court. The award was placed in a Treasury trust account. The Court says no distribution plan has been made because of the Tribe's refusal to cooperate. In United States v. Dann (1985), the Court held that "once the money was deposited into the trust account, payment was effected," which closed the claim under the Claims Commission Act. The case began when the United States sued Tribe members for grazing livestock on 5,120 acres without a permit. The Court did not decide their individual aboriginal-title claim.
