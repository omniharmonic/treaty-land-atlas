The Treaty of Ruby Valley was signed on October 1, 1863. Accounts agree it was a treaty of peace and friendship that allowed travel routes through Western Shoshone country. A 1994 statement to a United Nations working group, posted by the Center for World Indigenous Studies, argues the United States sought only safe passage and says "the treaty is still today in full force and effect." That is one party's argument, not a court finding.

Later the United States took a different path. The Indian Claims Commission decided that Western Shoshone title had been extinguished in the late 1800s and awarded more than $26 million, according to the Supreme Court. The award was certified in 1979 and placed in an interest-bearing Treasury trust account. The Tribe did not cooperate with a distribution plan, and the Western Shoshone statement objects that this award is now read as ending their land rights.

In United States v. Dann (1985), the Court held that "once the money was deposited into the trust account, payment was effected," which closed the claim under the Claims Commission Act. The case began when the United States sued two Western Shoshone sisters for grazing livestock on 5,120 acres without a permit. The Court did not decide their individual aboriginal-title claim. By 1985 the fund had grown to about $43 million, according to the opinion.
