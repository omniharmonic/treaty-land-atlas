The Treaty of Box Elder was signed on July 30, 1863, a few months after the January 1863 Bear River attack on Shoshone people. The Supreme Court describes it as declaring peace and friendship and securing rights of travel across Shoshone country. It described only "the country claimed by Pokatello," between the Raft River and the Portneuf Mountains. An ICT News article says the signers gave up about two-thirds of their hunting grounds and were promised $5,000 a year, which it says was not enough to feed the people.

The Senate added a clause saying the treaty did not grant any greater title than the Shoshone held under Mexican law when the United States acquired the territory. In 1929 Congress passed a law letting the Northwestern Bands sue for claims tied to this treaty. They sought about $15 million for roughly 15 million acres held under aboriginal title.

In Northwestern Bands of Shoshone Indians v. United States (March 12, 1945), a 5-4 Court held that the treaty did not recognize or acknowledge Indian title, so the claim did not arise under the treaty. The Bands recovered nothing in this case. The dissenters argued that negotiating rights of passage implied the United States recognized Shoshone title. Justice Jackson wrote that the government's obligations were moral and that Congress was the right body to address them.
