Under the Lame Bull Treaty of 1855, the Blackfeet reservation included the eastern slope of what is now Glacier National Park. In 1895 the Blackfeet agreed to cede about 800,000 acres of mountain land. A tribal-history page says they sold the land, now Glacier Park and part of the Lewis and Clark Forest, for $1.5 million. Accounts summarized in search results say the agreement reserved hunting, fishing and timber rights for as long as the land remained public land of the United States.

Congress created Glacier National Park by the act of May 11, 1910. According to the accounts found, that act did not mention the rights reserved in 1895. Park rules prohibit hunting. Federal courts in the 1930s ruled that the park's creation ended the reserved rights, and a Court of Claims decision in 1935 reached a similar result, according to a search summary. Later district court decisions in the 1970s disagreed with each other on whether the rights survive, and the Momberg court did not resolve the conflict.

Blackfeet scholar Jim Kipp has argued that tribal leaders agreed only to a 50-year lease, not to a sale, according to the same search summary. This entry rests mostly on secondary sources and should be confirmed with the Blackfeet Nation and the court opinions.
