In September 1895 the Blackfeet agreed to sell the mountain land along the western edge of their reservation to the United States. The tribal-history site trailtribes.org says it was 800,000 acres and the price was $1.5 million. Federal commissioners wanted the minerals. The Indians told them they wanted to keep rights to hunt, fish and cut timber.

The agreement reserved those rights. It said the Indians kept "the right to hunt upon said lands and to fish in the streams thereof so long as the same shall remain public lands of the United States." Congress ratified it on June 10, 1896. On May 11, 1910, Congress created Glacier National Park and included the sold land.

The courts then disagreed. The Court of Claims decided in 1935 that the reserved rights "were authoritatively terminated by the limitations of the act of May 11, 1910," a federal judge wrote in 1974. In United States v. Kipp (January 1974), a Blackfeet man was charged for entering the park without paying. Judge Russell Smith wrote that the Indians would not have understood the words "public lands" to let the United States end their rights by changing how it owned the land. He held that "the reserved rights were not extinguished by the act creating Glacier Park" and dismissed the charge.

Six months later, in United States v. Momberg, the same judge convicted a man for cutting wood but did not decide whether the rights survive. He noted the conflict between the 1935 decision and Kipp.
