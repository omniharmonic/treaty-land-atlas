The 1855 treaty set aside a large reservation for the Nez Perce. On June 9, 1863, at the Lapwai council ground, the United States concluded a new treaty with "chiefs, headmen, and delegates" of the tribe, changing the 1855 treaty. In Article I the Nez Perce were recorded as relinquishing "the lands heretofore reserved for the use and occupation of the said tribe," except for a new, smaller reservation described in Article II.

The ceded land was to be surveyed, appraised and sold. In Article IV the United States agreed to pay $262,500, out of the proceeds of those sales if they were enough. Most of it was for moving onto the new reservation, plowing and fencing land, farm tools, a mill and boarding schools. The Tribal Treaties Database lists the treaty as 14 Stat. 647, ratified April 17, 1867 and proclaimed April 20, 1867.

The National Park Service says Lawyer and other pro-treaty headmen signed on June 9, 1863. Chief Joseph, Chief White Bird and other "non-treaty" headmen thought they would not be held to any agreement they had not agreed to, but "the US government assumed they had reached an agreement with all Nez Perce." The Park Service states that the 1863 treaty "planted the seeds of conflict that would eventually grow to the 1877 Nez Perce War."
