The 1868 treaty with the Ute (15 Stat. 619; proclaimed November 6, 1868) set apart a large district of western Colorado "for the absolute and undisturbed use and occupation" of the named bands. In it the United States "solemnly" agreed that no persons except those authorized "shall ever be permitted to pass over, settle upon, or reside in the Territory described."

In the Brunot Agreement of September 13, 1873, the confederated Ute bands relinquished a portion of that reservation. Congress ratified it on April 29, 1874 (18 Stat. 36). The United States permitted hunting on the ceded land "so long as the game lasts and the Indians are at peace with the white people," and set up a trust fund paying $25,000 a year.

Aspen Journalism says that the government brought Ute representatives to Washington "to ink a capitulation — not a treaty — on March 6, 1880." Under it, the Uncompahgre and White River Utes were directed to move to the Uintah and Ouray reservations in Utah. In September 1881 troops under Gen. Ranald Mackenzie and Gen. Wesley Merritt moved against them. The same article says the Ute Commission oversaw $75,000 in payments for their surrendered lands.

Land opened in Utah later shrank too. In Hagen v. Utah (1994) the Supreme Court held, 7 to 2, that Congress diminished the Uintah Reservation, so the town of Myton is not Indian country.
