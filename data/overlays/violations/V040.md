The Navajo removal in 1864 was not a broken treaty. It was a military campaign and forced march during a war. Northern Arizona University's Cline Library says the military forced about 11,468 Navajo from Fort Canby (today's Fort Defiance, Arizona) on a march of more than 300 miles to Bosque Redondo in New Mexico. It adds that General James Carleton led the relocation and that Kit Carson carried out a campaign that included burning cornfields and poisoning water sources.

The library states that "the weaker people who could not keep up pace with the military were either left to die or were shot." At Bosque Redondo it lists contaminated water, disease, crop failure and a shortage of wood. It estimates several hundred deaths on the march and "close to 2000" at Bosque Redondo. Estimates of deaths vary by source.

The government released the Navajo in 1868. A treaty signed at Fort Sumner on June 1, 1868 ended the war. The Smithsonian's teaching materials, citing the Kappler compilation, give Article XIII as the Navajo agreeing to make the reservation "their permanent Home." The library says the treaty let the Navajo return to a parcel of about 3.5 million acres that included part of their original homeland. This treaty is unusual because it returned people to their homeland, though to less land than before.
