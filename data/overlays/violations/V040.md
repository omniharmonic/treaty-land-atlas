The Navajo removal in 1864 was a military campaign during a war, not a broken treaty. Northern Arizona University's Cline Library says General James Carleton led the forced relocation, and Kit Carson attacked the Navajo with cavalry, "burning Navajo cornfields and poisoning water sources." In 1864, it says, the military forced 11,468 Navajos from Fort Canby (today's Fort Defiance, Arizona) to walk more than 300 miles to Bosque Redondo in New Mexico.

The library states that "the weaker people who could not keep up pace with the military were either left to die or were shot." It says several hundred Navajo died on the march and "close to 2000" more at Bosque Redondo, where the river water was contaminated, disease was rampant, the corn crop failed and wood became scarce.

The Treaty of Fort Sumner was signed on June 1, 1868 (15 Stat. 667; ratified July 25, 1868; proclaimed August 12, 1868). In Article 13 the Navajo agreed "to make the reservation herein described their permanent home" and not to settle elsewhere as a tribe. The library says the treaty let them move back onto about 3.5 million acres that included part of their ancestral land. The Supreme Court wrote in 2023 that the treaty set aside a reservation in their original homeland.

This is context for how a reservation was created after forced removal, not a record of a broken treaty promise.
