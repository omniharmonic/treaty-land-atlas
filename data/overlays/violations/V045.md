Many treaties had set aside reservations as shared tribal land. The General Allotment Act of February 8, 1887, often called the Dawes Act, changed that policy. As summarized by Nebraska Studies, each head of a family was to get 160 acres of farmland or 320 acres of grazing land. The Act's own text, quoted on an archives.gov page, said the government would "hold the land thus allotted, for the period of twenty-five years, in trust" for the allottee.

Land left over after allotment was declared "surplus" and opened to white settlement. The archives.gov page says the Act's results included poor allotments, such as desert or near-desert land, inheritance that split plots into parcels too small to farm, and sales to non-Native buyers that "caused American Indian communities to lose vast acreage of their tribal lands." Nebraska Studies reports that Indians held about 150 million acres before the Act and that "within twenty years, two-thirds of their land was gone."

Figures differ by source. Other commonly cited totals, such as 138 million acres in 1887 and 48 million in 1934, were not confirmed here. The Indian Reorganization Act of 1934 ended allotment. Courts have found that allotment alone does not shrink a reservation, but surplus-land acts with clear cession language can.
