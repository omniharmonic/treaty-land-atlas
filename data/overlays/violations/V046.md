Many tribes had treaties or agreements creating a lasting relationship with the United States, including trust protection of their land. House Concurrent Resolution 108, adopted August 1, 1953, declared that Congress wanted to end that relationship. According to the National Archives, it called for termination "at the earliest possible time" for all tribes in California, Florida, New York and Texas, and it named specific tribes: the Flathead Tribe of Montana, the Klamath Tribe of Oregon, the Menominee Tribe of Wisconsin, the Potawatomi Tribe of Kansas and Nebraska, and members of the Chippewa Tribe on the Turtle Mountain Reservation.

Public Law 280, enacted August 15, 1953, "conferred jurisdiction over criminal offenses and civil cases arising on most Indian reservations" to state governments in California, Minnesota, Nebraska, Oregon and Wisconsin. The Archives states that "from 1953 until 1970, Congress initiated 60 separate termination proceedings against American Indian tribes" and that "over three million acres of tribal lands were relinquished as a result." The Nixon administration repudiated termination in 1970.

A Carleton College course page says the National Congress of American Indians knew the policies would "destroy tribal governments." The Archives figure of 60 refers to termination proceedings; it is not a count of tribes. This is about federal policy, not a court finding. The Menominee Restoration Act of 1973 (see the Menominee record) is one example of Congress later undoing a termination.
