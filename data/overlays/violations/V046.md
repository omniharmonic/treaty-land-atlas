Many tribes had treaties or agreements creating a lasting relationship with the United States, including trust protection of their land. House Concurrent Resolution 108, adopted August 1, 1953, declared that Congress wanted to end that relationship. According to the National Archives, it called for termination "at the earliest possible time" for all tribes in California, Florida, New York and Texas, and it named specific tribes including the Flathead, Klamath, Menominee, Potawatomi and Turtle Mountain Chippewa.

Public Law 280, enacted August 15, 1953, "conferred jurisdiction over criminal offenses and civil cases arising on most Indian reservations" to state governments in several states. The Archives states that "from 1953 until 1970, Congress initiated 60 separate termination proceedings against American Indian tribes" and that "over three million acres of tribal lands were relinquished as a result." The Nixon administration repudiated termination in 1970.

At the time, the National Congress of American Indians warned that these policies would "destroy tribal governments," according to a Carleton College course page. Other sources give different counts of terminated tribes, such as over one hundred, so the 60 figure refers only to proceedings. Congress later restored several terminated tribes by statute, though this entry did not verify the individual dates.
