In the 1830s the United States made treaties with the Creek Nation after its removal west. The Supreme Court's opinion in McGirt quotes the promise that "The Creek country west of the Mississippi shall be solemnly guarantied to the Creek Indians." An 1833 treaty fixed the borders of a "permanent home to the whole Creek nation."

Later, Congress ran an allotment program in Indian Territory. The Curtis Act abolished tribal courts and moved their cases to federal courts. Oklahoma became a state in 1907. The State then prosecuted many crimes in eastern Oklahoma as though the Creek reservation no longer existed. The Court looked for a clear act of Congress ending the reservation and found none. It wrote that "only Congress can divest a reservation of its land and diminish its boundaries," and that allotment was a "first, not final, step toward disestablishment."

On July 9, 2020, the Court ruled 5-4, in an opinion by Justice Gorsuch, that "land reserved for the Creek Nation since the 19th century remains" Indian country for purposes of the Major Crimes Act. That means certain serious crimes involving Native defendants there fall under federal, not state, authority. The ruling is about criminal jurisdiction, not land ownership. The Court noted Congress remains free to add to its statutes about these lands. Two years later, Oklahoma v. Castro-Huerta (see V048) gave the State some added power over crimes by non-Indians.
