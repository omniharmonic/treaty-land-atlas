The Stevens treaties of the 1850s secured to Western Washington tribes the "right of taking fish at usual and accustomed grounds and stations . . . in common with all citizens of the Territory," as the Supreme Court quoted in 1979. Tribes ceded land and kept this right as part of the bargain.

The Washington Secretary of State's history says "For decades, the State of Washington ignored this promise." Conflicts on the rivers, often called the Fish Wars, grew as tribal members fished and the State saw the treaties as outdated. In United States v. Washington, Judge George Boldt ruled on February 12, 1974, that the promises of the treaties remained in effect, "guaranteeing tribes up to half of the harvest," according to the Secretary of State's history.

On July 2, 1979, the Supreme Court largely upheld the ruling. It held that an equitable measure of the common right should initially divide the harvestable portion of each run, and said "the 50% figure imposes a maximum but not a minimum allocation." The Court noted that "the Game Department simply refused to comply" with the federal court's orders, so the District Court directly supervised parts of the state fisheries. Disputes over salmon habitat and other fishing questions have continued.
