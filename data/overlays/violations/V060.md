Alaska passed from Russia to the United States by the 1867 Treaty of Cession, which, a Congressional Research Service report notes, did not otherwise address aboriginal property holdings. In Tee-Hit-Ton Indians v. United States, decided February 7, 1955, the Supreme Court held that "the taking by the United States of unrecognized Indian title is not compensable under the Fifth Amendment."

In 1971 Congress passed the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act (ANCSA). Congress said the settlement should be "in conformity with the real economic and social needs of Natives" and happen "without litigation." The law extinguished "all aboriginal titles, if any, and claims of aboriginal title in Alaska based on use and occupancy," including "any aboriginal hunting or fishing rights that may exist." This was a negotiated settlement, though set in a law passed by Congress rather than a treaty.

The CRS report says ANCSA "extinguished claims by Alaska Natives to over 360 million acres of land." In return, regional and village corporations shared "a settlement payment of approximately $962.5 million" and "approximately 45 million acres." Land transfers have continued. Subsistence protections came later under other laws, which this entry did not verify.
