# B3. Counterexamples, Land Returns and What the United States Has Formally Acknowledged

*Corrections note (2026-10-16): verification corrections (V1 legal, V2 facts and figures, V3 donations and licensing) were applied to this file where a source URL was given. See `verification/CORRECTIONS-APPLIED.md` for each change and for what was not applied. No lawyer or tribal government has reviewed this file.*

**What this file is.** The Atlas should not tell only a story of loss. This file records (1) land returned to Native nations, by mechanism; (2) settlements that paid money or land instead of restoring a treaty; (3) the federal remedies that did exist (Indian Claims Commission, Court of Claims, Tucker Acts); and (4) a ledger of what the United States has **formally acknowledged** versus what it has not. Violation entries (V001-V062) are in `B2-violation-catalog.md`; terminology is in `B1-violation-typology.md`. Another agent's file, `F3-land-returns-and-campaigns.md` (and `seed-data/campaigns-seed.csv`), holds the detailed verified record on current campaigns and returns; **this file summarizes and cross-references F3 and does not replace it.** Where F3 and this file differ, check the primary source and record the dispute.

**Tag convention.** `[verified]` = confirmed in a source read this session (tier in the Sources list: P primary, A authoritative secondary, T tertiary/news); `[from-memory]`; `[disputed]`; `[unverified]`.

**Important framing.** A "land return" is rarely a restoration of a treaty. Most are one of: (a) an act of Congress transferring federal land back to trust (Blue Lake, Havasupai, Bison Range); (b) a purchase by a tribe, a land trust or conservancy and a later transfer; (c) a state or local grant or gift; (d) a court-ordered or negotiated settlement paying money and/or land. Each mechanism has different legal consequences (trust vs fee status, reserved rights, tax and jurisdiction). The Atlas should label the mechanism and never imply that a return "cures" a historical treaty violation.

**Why this matters.** In the cases above Congress or a court gave money instead of land and often required release of land claims. The Indian Claims Commission (section 4) paid for the *price of land at the time of taking*, without interest, and could not order land back. Several nations have refused the money precisely because acceptance would extinguish title; the Atlas should present this as a political position, not a failure to claim.

## 4. Ratified versus claimed: what remedies existed and what the US has formally acknowledged

### 4.1 Indian Claims Commission (1946-1978)

- **Statute.** Indian Claims Commission Act, 60 Stat. 1049, approved Aug. 13, 1946 `[verified]`. It created a three-member (later five-member) commission to hear tribal claims accruing before Aug. 13, 1946 `[verified]`.
- **Five categories of claim** `[verified]`: (1) claims in law or equity arising under the Constitution, laws or treaties; (2) other claims in law or equity; (3) claims that would justify revision of a treaty on grounds of fraud, duress, unconscionable consideration or mistake; (4) takings of land without compensation; (5) claims based on "fair and honorable dealings not recognized by any existing rule of law or equity."
- **Filing window.** Five years from the Act (to Aug. 13, 1951) `[verified]`.
- **Scale.** The final report records 370 petitions separated into 617 dockets, running from Aug. 13, 1946 to Sept. 30, 1978 `[verified]`. Wikipedia reports 546 dockets completed with awards of $818,172,606.64, and 170 cases transferred to the Court of Claims under Pub. L. 94-465 `[verified, tertiary]`. The National Archives says awards totaled over $800 million `[verified]`. The three figures are consistent in magnitude and differ in what they count; the Atlas should cite a range and the source.
- **Money only.** The ICC could award money damages, never land `[verified]`. Awards were generally the value of the land at the date of taking, without interest, less offsets for government expenditures `[from-memory; confirm against ICC final report]`.
- **Finality.** *United States v. Dann*, 470 U.S. 39 (1985): payment of an ICC award, by entry into a Treasury account, met the payment requirement and barred further claims to the same land, even if the tribe refused the money `[verified]`. This is why the Western Shoshone and Sioux funds are legally contested.
- **Treaty authority.** The Act placed treaty-revision grounds (fraud, duress, unconscionable consideration, mistake) in the Commission's remit. In practice relief for "unconscionable consideration" meant extra payment, not rescission `[from-memory]`.
- **Remaining claims.** On the Commission's termination, pending cases moved to the Court of Claims `[verified, tertiary]`.

### 4.2 Court of Claims, Tucker Act and Indian Tucker Act

- **Tucker Act**, 28 U.S.C. 1491: claims against the United States founded on the Constitution, a statute, a regulation or a contract `[verified]`. Takings claims for *recognized* title were brought under it (*Sioux Nation*) `[verified]`.
- **Indian Tucker Act**, 28 U.S.C. 1505: gives the Court of Federal Claims (successor to the Court of Claims) jurisdiction over tribal claims accruing after Aug. 13, 1946 `[verified]`. Claims before that date went to the ICC.
- **Jurisdictional limit on treaty claims.** A treaty claim for money damages needs a money-mandating provision (the "fair interpretation" test) `[from-memory]`. Tribes won on specific trust-accounting claims (*United States v. Mitchell II*, 463 U.S. 206 (1983) `[from-memory]`) and lost others (*United States v. Navajo Nation*, 556 U.S. 287 (2009); *Jicarilla Apache*, 564 U.S. 162 (2011) `[from-memory]`). Verify in `C1-case-law-survey.md` before citing.
- **Sioux Nation (1980).** The Court of Claims found a taking; the Supreme Court affirmed, upholding the principle that the Black Hills were taken in violation of the Fifth Amendment and awarding the 1877 value ($17.5 million) plus 5% simple interest from 1877 `[verified, tertiary; interest detail [from-memory]]`.

## 5. Patterns across the ledger

1. **Money, not land, was the standard remedy.** Of the federal remedies in section 4, the ones that returned land (Blue Lake, Havasupai, Menominee, Siletz, Bison Range, Land Buy-Back) are all statutory or program-based. None came from a court holding that a treaty required return.
2. **Courts recognized takings only for recognized title.** *Sioux Nation* (recognized by the 1868 treaty) paid; *Tee-Hit-Ton* and *Northwestern Shoshone* distinguish unrecognized title.
3. **Jurisdictional rulings are not title rulings.** Diminishment and disestablishment cases (*Solem* line; *McGirt*) decide who has criminal and civil jurisdiction. They do not by themselves transfer ownership of any acre.
4. **Where the federal record is silent, the Atlas should say so**, not infer. "None found" means the sources read this session contain no federal acknowledgment; confirm against ICC docket listings (see Indian Claims Commission decisions, Clearwater Publishing) and Court of Federal Claims records before publishing.
5. **Recent trend.** 2026 shows both returns through tribal and land-trust purchase (Penobscot, Mi'kmaq, Pe' Sla) and federal reversals in places that tribes had treated as protected (Bears Ears, Oak Flat, DAPL ROD). The Atlas should show these as events with sources, not as a trend line.

## 7. Display guidance for the Atlas

- Always pair a return with the **mechanism** and **legal status** (trust, fee, conservation easement). Never use "returned by treaty" unless a treaty did it.
- Pair every violation with its **acknowledgment level** from section 4.4, shown as a short badge (Court, Statute, ICC/CoC, Apology, None found).
- Where a nation has refused a settlement, say that the refusal is the nation's own position, with a link to the tribal source if available.
- Mark small and unfinalized returns (headline-only items) with `unverified` and do not map them until confirmed.
- Do not publish exact locations of sacred or ceremonial sites (Blue Lake, Pe' Sla, Bears Ears, Oak Flat, Mount Rushmore area) beyond the polygons already public in statute or tribal materials.
