# Statutes and Policy Eras (C2)

*Corrections note (2026-10-16): verification corrections (V1 legal, V2 facts and figures, V3 donations and licensing) were applied to this file where a source URL was given. See `verification/CORRECTIONS-APPLIED.md` for each change and for what was not applied. No lawyer or tribal government has reviewed this file.*

Treaty Land Atlas, Research Agent C. This file organizes the federal statutes, resolutions, regulations, and proclamations that shaped Native land rights into **policy eras** for use on the Atlas timeline. The machine-readable list is `seed-data/statutes-seed.csv` (51 rows); the index is reproduced at the end of this file. Cases that interpret these laws are in `C1-case-law-survey.md`; the doctrine map is in `C3-doctrine-map.md`.

**Tags and conventions.** `[verified]`, `[from-memory]`, `[disputed]`, `[unverified]` as defined in the research brief. A statute row is marked `verified` when its name, year, and Statutes at Large or Public Law citation were confirmed against a page read this session (Cornell LII's U.S. Code pages, Wikipedia's article citations, or a Federal Register page); individual sub-details that were not confirmed carry inline tags. Wikipedia is used only as a secondary source for citation lookups and for figures flagged as secondary; it is not a substitute for the Statutes at Large, which a verification pass should check against govinfo.gov or the Library of Congress.

**Periodization.** Federal Indian law is commonly divided into periods such as the formative era to 1871, allotment and assimilation, the Indian Reorganization era, termination, and self-determination (the framing used in Cohen's *Handbook of Federal Indian Law* `[from-memory]`). Scholars disagree about exact boundaries, and tribal histories often do not follow federal policy labels, so these eras are for orientation only.

## Era 1. Formative period, treaties, and removal (1763-1871)

| Year | Instrument | What it did | Land effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1763 | Royal Proclamation | Reserved western lands for Indians and barred private purchases `[from-memory]` | Not a U.S. statute; the model for later federal pre-emption. Not in CSV. |
| 1787/1789 | Northwest Ordinance, 1 Stat. 50 `[from-memory]` | Promised that Indian land "shall never be taken from them without their consent" and "utmost good faith" `[phrases from-memory]` | Rhetorical protection; contradicted by cessions |
| 1790-1802 | Trade and Intercourse (Nonintercourse) Acts `[Stat. cites from-memory]` | No sale of Indian land valid without federal treaty or convention | The legal basis of *Oneida II* and *Passamaquoddy*; still codified at 25 U.S.C. 177 |
| 1819 | Civilization Fund Act, 3 Stat. 516 `[from-memory]` | Federal money for missionary schools | Roots of assimilation and boarding-school policy |
| 1830 | **Indian Removal Act**, 4 Stat. 411 (Pub. L. 21-148) `[verified]` | Authorized exchanges of eastern land for land west of the Mississippi | Removal of the Cherokee, Muscogee (Creek), Seminole, Choctaw, Chickasaw, and others |
| 1834 | **Trade and Intercourse Act**, 4 Stat. 729 `[verified]` | Defined Indian country, restated the bar on private purchases (section 12, 4 Stat. 730 = 25 U.S.C. 177 per LII) | Basis of Nonintercourse claims |
| 1851 | Indian Appropriations Act, 9 Stat. 574 `[from-memory]` | Funded concentration of tribes on defined reservations | Start of the western reservation system |
| 1871 | **Act of March 3, 1871**, 16 Stat. 544, 566 (25 U.S.C. 71) `[verified]` | "No Indian nation or tribe ... shall be acknowledged or recognized as an independent nation, tribe, or power with whom the United States may contract by treaty," but earlier treaties remain valid `[quotation verified at LII]` | Ended treaty-making; later land transactions became "agreements" ratified by both houses (a difference of procedure, not necessarily of effect) |

**Legal notes**
- The 1871 Act did not repeal existing treaties and expressly preserved them; it changed the form of future dealings. The Atlas should present post-1871 instruments as "agreements" or "executive orders," not "treaties," and treat the count of ratified treaties as a separate question for the treaty-research agents (about 370 ratified treaties is a commonly cited figure; this file does not verify it `[unverified]`).
- Executive-order reservations (1855 to 1919) created many reservations without a treaty or statute; later cases treat them as real but more vulnerable (see *Santa Fe Pacific*, *Mattz*, *Idaho v. United States*). The 1919 statute that limited their creation in some states is `[unverified]`.

## Era 2. Allotment and assimilation (1885-1934)

| Year | Instrument | What it did | Land effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1885 | **Major Crimes Act**, 23 Stat. 362, now 18 U.S.C. 1153 `[verified; section/page pin from-memory]` | Federal jurisdiction over enumerated serious crimes by Indians in Indian country; passed after *Ex parte Crow Dog* | Federal criminal jurisdiction, central to *McGirt* |
| 1887 | **General Allotment (Dawes) Act**, 24 Stat. 388 (Pub. L. 49-105) `[verified]` | Divided tribal land into individual allotments; sold "surplus" to non-Indians | Secondary sources cite about 138 million acres in 1887 and about 48 million in 1934 (Steven Gunn, via Wikipedia); other estimates of land lost range around 90-100 million acres. Treat as `[disputed]` and attach the source to every figure used. |
| 1898 | **Curtis Act**, 30 Stat. 495 (Pub. L. 55-517) `[verified]` | Extended allotment to the Five Tribes, abolished tribal courts and governments, authorized town sites | Dissolution of the Five Tribes' communal land; statehood 1907; the reservations at issue in *McGirt* |
| 1906 | Burke Act, 34 Stat. 182 `[date and cite verified; description from-memory]` | Let the Secretary end trust restrictions early on allotments ("forced fee patents") | Accelerated alienation |
| 1906 | Antiquities Act, 34 Stat. 225 (54 U.S.C. 320301) `[verified]` | President may proclaim national monuments | Later the vehicle for Bears Ears |
| 1906 | Five Tribes Act and Oklahoma Enabling Act `[from-memory; not in CSV]` | Ended Five Tribes governments' land role and set up Oklahoma statehood | Central to *McGirt* |
| 1924 | **Indian Citizenship Act**, 43 Stat. 253 `[verified]` | Declared Indians born in the U.S. citizens, without impairing tribal property rights | Political status; voting barriers persisted in states into the mid-twentieth century |
| 1924 | Pueblo Lands Act, 43 Stat. 636 `[verified]` | Board to resolve non-Indian claims on Pueblo land | Mixed result for Pueblos |

**Legal notes**
- Allotment is the biggest documented land loss tied to a single policy; the figures above vary by source, as the brief's principle 7 requires. Surplus-land acts opening particular reservations are the "acts" examined in the diminishment cases (C1, Part 6).
- Allotment also created the fractionation problem (hundreds of heirs holding tiny undivided interests), addressed by the Indian Land Consolidation Act (1983), the American Indian Probate Reform Act (2004), and the Land Buy-Back Program (2012-about 2022).

## Era 3. Indian Reorganization and the New Deal (1934-1945)

| Year | Instrument | What it did | Land effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1934 | **Indian Reorganization Act (IRA)**, 48 Stat. 984 (Pub. L. 73-383), 25 U.S.C. 5101 et seq. `[verified]` | Ended allotment; section 5 (25 U.S.C. 5108, formerly 465) lets the Secretary take land into trust; section 16 encourages tribal constitutions | Secondary sources report more than two million acres returned in the first twenty years `[secondary]`. *Carcieri* (2009) limited trust authority to tribes "under federal jurisdiction" in 1934. |
| 1936 | Oklahoma Indian Welfare Act and Alaska Reorganization Act `[from-memory; not in CSV]` | Extended IRA-type authority | Alaska trust acquisition is contested (see Era 9, M-37087) |
| 1944 | Flood Control Act (Pick-Sloan), 58 Stat. 887 `[from-memory]` | Missouri River dams | Flooded reservation bottomlands; compensation by later acts `[example tribes from-memory]` |

## Era 4. Claims and termination (1946-1968)

| Year | Instrument | What it did | Land effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1946 | **Indian Claims Commission Act**, 60 Stat. 1049 `[verified]` | Forum for tribal claims against the U.S.; money damages only | Secondary sources report 546 dockets completed and about $818 million in judgments; the Commission could not return land, and acceptance of a money award barred the claim. The Western Shoshone dispute (*United States v. Dann*, 1985) shows the cost. |
| 1953 | **House Concurrent Resolution 108**, 67 Stat. B132 `[from-memory]` | Declared a policy to end federal supervision of tribes | Start of termination |
| 1953 | **Public Law 280**, 67 Stat. 588 `[verified]` | Transferred criminal and some civil jurisdiction in several states from the federal government to the states | Weakened tribal jurisdiction; amended by the Indian Civil Rights Act of 1968 (tribal consent for future assumption; retrocession) |
| 1954 | **Menominee Termination Act** (68 Stat. 250 `[from-memory]`) and **Klamath Termination Act** (Pub. L. 83-587; 68 Stat. 718 `[from-memory]`) | Ended federal recognition and supervision; forced sales of land | The Klamath reservation shrank from about 762,000 to about 145,000 acres in one secondary account; treaty hunting and fishing rights survived in *Menominee* (1968) and *Kimball v. Callahan* (9th Cir. 1974) `[from-memory]` |
| 1956 | Indian Relocation Act, 70 Stat. 986 (Pub. L. 84-959) `[verified]` | Funded relocation to cities | Reduced reservation population |

**Legal notes**
- How many tribes were terminated is reported differently by source (figures such as "more than 100 tribes and bands" and "109 tribes" appear in the literature `[unverified]`; this file does not choose among them). Restorations began with the Menominee Restoration Act (1973) and continued case by case.

## Era 5. Self-determination (1968-1988)

| Year | Instrument | What it did | Land effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1968 | **Indian Civil Rights Act**, 82 Stat. 73 (Pub. L. 90-284), 25 U.S.C. 1301-1304 `[verified]` | Applied many Bill of Rights guarantees to tribal governments; PL 280 consent | Strengthened self-government indirectly |
| 1970 | **Blue Lake Return Act**, Pub. L. 91-550 `[Stat. cite and date from-memory]` | Returned about 48,000 acres of the Carson National Forest to Taos Pueblo in trust | A rare statutory return of sacred land |
| 1971 | **Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act (ANCSA)**, 85 Stat. 688 (Pub. L. 92-203) `[verified]` | Extinguished aboriginal title in Alaska for about 44 million acres and $962.5 million, delivered through corporations | Land held by corporations, not tribes; *Venetie* (1998) held ANCSA lands are not Indian country |
| 1973 | Menominee Restoration Act, 87 Stat. 770 `[from-memory]` | Restored recognition | Reversal of termination |
| 1975 | **Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act (ISDEAA)**, Pub. L. 93-638 `[date and PL verified; 88 Stat. 2203 from-memory]` | Tribal contracting and compacting of federal programs | Basis of *Ramah*, *Chehalis*, and *Becerra* |
| 1978 | **American Indian Religious Freedom Act**, 92 Stat. 469 (Pub. L. 95-341) `[verified]`; 1994 amendments Pub. L. 103-344 | Federal policy to protect Native religious practices | No enforceable rights (*Lyng*) |
| 1978 | **Indian Child Welfare Act**, 92 Stat. 3069 (Pub. L. 95-608) `[verified]` | Tribal jurisdiction over Native child custody; placement preferences | Upheld in *Brackeen* (2023) |
| 1978 | Rhode Island Indian Claims Settlement Act `[from-memory]` | Settled the Narragansett claim | The land at issue in *Carcieri* |
| 1979 | **Archaeological Resources Protection Act**, 93 Stat. 721 (Pub. L. 96-95) `[verified]` | Protects archaeological resources on public and Indian lands | Sacred-site protections |
| 1980 | **Maine Indian Claims Settlement Act**, 94 Stat. 1785 (Pub. L. 96-420) `[verified]` | Extinguished claims for about $81.5 million and up to 300,000 acres `[secondary]` | Subjected the Maine tribes to state law; the First Circuit has read it to limit their authority relative to other tribes `[secondary]` |
| 1980 | ANILCA, Pub. L. 96-487 `[PL and date verified]` | Alaska conservation lands and rural subsistence priority | Ongoing subsistence conflicts |
| 1983 | Indian Land Consolidation Act, Pub. L. 97-459 `[from-memory]` | Tried to reduce fractionation; the escheat section was struck down in *Hodel v. Irving* (1987) and *Babbitt v. Youpee* (1997) | See AIPRA |
| 1988 | **Indian Gaming Regulatory Act (IGRA)**, 102 Stat. 2467 (Pub. L. 100-497) `[verified]` | Framework for tribal gaming on "Indian lands" | The "Indian lands" definition links gaming to trust land |

## Era 6. Repatriation, recognition, and tribal justice (1990-2012)

| Year | Instrument | What it did | Land effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1990 | **NAGPRA**, 104 Stat. 3048 (Pub. L. 101-601), 25 U.S.C. 3001 et seq. `[verified]` | Repatriation of remains and cultural items; tribal ownership of items found on federal or tribal land | Cultural, not title, protection; regulations revised 2023-24 (below) |
| 1993 | Catawba Indian Tribe of South Carolina Land Claims Settlement Act, Pub. L. 103-116 `[from-memory]` | Settled the claim that remained after *South Carolina v. Catawba* (1986) | Money and land acquisition authority `[amounts from-memory]` |

| 2010 | **Claims Resolution Act of 2010**, Pub. L. 111-291 `[PL from-memory; enactment in Dec. 2010 verified in DOI release]` | Funded the Cobell settlement ($3.4 billion) with a $1.9 billion Land Consolidation Fund | Land Buy-Back Program (secondary: about $900 million paid for about 1.7 million acres by 2016) |
| 2010 | **Tribal Law and Order Act**, 124 Stat. 2258 (Pub. L. 111-211; tit. II at 124 Stat. 2261) `[verified]` | Expanded tribal sentencing authority and federal accountability | Jurisdictional capacity |

The 2017 reduction is described as 83 percent (KUER) or about 87 percent (the Coalition). Record the range and the source for each figure.
