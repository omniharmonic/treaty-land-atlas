## Part 1. The doctrinal arc in one page

The chain asked for in the task is **discovery, domestic dependent nations, plenary power, trust responsibility, canons of construction, self-determination, then recent erosion and expansion**. It is a useful teaching sequence, but the reality overlaps: the canons and reserved-rights doctrine appear as early as 1832 and 1905, and trust and plenary power developed together. The Atlas should present the arc as **layers** that coexist and are invoked selectively, not as a straight line.

| Layer | Core idea | Anchor authority | Still operative? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. **Discovery** (1823) | European sovereigns, then the U.S., hold ultimate title; tribes hold a right of occupancy that only the sovereign can extinguish | `case-johnson-v-mintosh-1823`; `case-fletcher-v-peck-1810` (precursor) | Yes, as the baseline for all U.S. land title; criticized and cited in a footnote in `case-city-of-sherrill-v-oneida-2005` `[footnote from-memory]` |
| 2. **Domestic dependent nations and inherent sovereignty** (1831-32) | Tribes are distinct political communities, not foreign states and not subject to state law on their land | `case-cherokee-nation-v-georgia-1831`; `case-worcester-v-georgia-1832` | Yes. *Worcester*'s preemption principle appears in `case-mcclanahan-v-arizona-tax-1973`, `case-white-mountain-apache-v-bracker-1980`, and `case-williams-v-lee-1959`; its force against states was cut back by `case-oklahoma-v-castro-huerta-2022` |
| 3. **Plenary power** (1871-1903) | Congress has unreviewable authority over tribes, including the power to override treaties | `stat-indian-appropriations-act-1871`; `case-cherokee-tobacco-1871`; `case-kagama-1886`; `case-lone-wolf-v-hitchcock-1903` | Yes, but the Court uses it increasingly to affirm federal authority that protects tribes (`case-haaland-v-brackeen-2023`, `case-us-v-lara-2004`); takings are compensable (`case-us-v-sioux-nation-1980`) |
| 4. **Recognized vs. aboriginal title** (1935-1955) | Land held by treaty or statute is compensable when taken; aboriginal title may be extinguished without compensation | `case-us-v-creek-nation-1935`; `case-shoshone-tribe-v-us-1937`; `case-tee-hit-ton-v-us-1955` | Yes; ANCSA (`stat-alaska-native-claims-settlement-act-1971`) settled Alaska claims on this footing |
| 5. **Trust responsibility** (1942-) | The United States owes fiduciary duties to tribes | `case-seminole-nation-v-us-1942`; later narrowed in `case-us-v-navajo-nation-2003`, `case-us-v-jicarilla-apache-nation-2011`, `case-arizona-v-navajo-nation-2023` | Yes, but enforceable for damages only where a statute, treaty or regulation creates a specific duty |
| 6. **Reserved rights and canons of construction** (1832-) | Treaties reserve rights tribes did not give up; treaties are read as the Indians understood them; ambiguities favor the Indians; abrogation needs clear intent | `case-us-v-winans-1905`; `case-winters-v-us-1908`; `case-jones-v-meehan-1899`; `case-carpenter-v-shaw-1930`; `case-menominee-tribe-v-us-1968`; `case-wa-fishing-vessel-1979`; `case-minnesota-v-mille-lacs-1999`; `case-herrera-v-wyoming-2019` | Yes. This is the strongest treaty-protective line at the Supreme Court in the 2010s-2020s |
| 7. **Self-determination** (1968-) | Federal policy favors tribal governance; Indian-specific laws rest on tribes' political status | `case-morton-v-mancari-1974`; `stat-indian-self-determination-act-1975`; `case-becerra-v-san-carlos-apache-2024` | Yes, with a dual track: statutory expansion (VAWA, ISDEAA) alongside judicial limits on tribal jurisdiction |
| 8. **Judicial limits on tribal sovereignty** (1978-) | Implicit divestiture; Montana rule; state jurisdiction over non-Indians | `case-oliphant-v-suquamish-1978`; `case-montana-v-us-1981`; `case-nevada-v-hicks-2001`; `case-plains-commerce-bank-v-long-family-2008`; `case-oklahoma-v-castro-huerta-2022` | Yes, but Congress has reversed parts (`stat-vawa-2013-special-domestic-violence-jurisdiction`, `stat-vawa-2022-special-tribal-criminal-jurisdiction`) |
| 9. **Boundary and land-title law** (1962-) | Reservations remain until Congress clearly ends them; land into trust requires statutory authority | Diminishment cases (C1, Part 6); `case-mcgirt-v-oklahoma-2020`; `case-carcieri-v-salazar-2009`; `case-city-of-sherrill-v-oneida-2005` | Yes; live in `case-mille-lacs-band-v-madore-2025` |
| 10. **Executive and legislative swings** (2021-2026) | Large policy changes by proclamation and opinion | `stat-bears-ears-proclamation-2016`, `stat-bears-ears-grand-staircase-proclamations-2026`, `stat-alaska-trust-land-solicitor-opinion-m-37087-2026`, `stat-lumbee-recognition-ndaa-2025` | In flux; litigation announced but not yet found |

### Why the arc is not a simple decline or ascent

- **Eighteenth to early twentieth century:** doctrine moved from recognition of tribal nationhood (*Worcester*) toward federal control (*Kagama*, *Lone Wolf*), while a parallel line (*Winans*, *Winters*) protected rights reserved in treaties.
- **1930s-1960s:** trust and claims doctrines expanded (*Seminole Nation*, *Creek Nation*, *Menominee*), but the Termination Era and *Tee-Hit-Ton* show the limits.
- **1970s:** the Court's self-determination decisions (*Mancari*, *Wheeler*, *McClanahan*, *Boldt*/*Fishing Vessel*) were favorable, and the Court's own *Oliphant* (1978) began a counter-trend.
- **1980s-2000s:** the Court tended to restrict tribal civil and criminal jurisdiction (*Montana*, *Brendale*, *Hicks*, *Plains Commerce*) and equitable remedies for land (*Sherrill*), while Congress acted on gaming, repatriation, and probate.
- **2010s-2020s:** treaty-text cases (*Herrera*, *Cougar Den*, *McGirt*) restored force to the canons, authored by Justices from across the Court (Sotomayor, Breyer, Gorsuch), while *Castro-Huerta* (2022) and *Arizona v. Navajo Nation* (2023) pulled back on practical remedies.
- **2024-2026:** no Indian-law merits decisions at the Supreme Court in October Terms 2024 and 2025 (NARF Tribal Supreme Court Project, `[verified]`); change comes from agencies, presidents, and circuit courts.

## Part 2. Doctrine entries (for graph nodes)

### 2.1 Discovery and Indian title

- **What it says:** On "discovery," the European sovereign (and then the United States) acquired the exclusive right to obtain Indian land; the tribe retained a right of occupancy. *Johnson v. M'Intosh* (1823) is the source.
- **Linked rules:** Only the United States can extinguish Indian title (*Oneida I*, `case-oneida-i-1974`); extinguishment by Congress of unrecognized title needs no compensation (*Tee-Hit-Ton*); recognized title is compensable (*Creek Nation*, *Shoshone Tribe*, *Sioux Nation*).
- **Statutory counterpart:** Nonintercourse Acts (`stat-trade-intercourse-acts-1790-1802`, `stat-trade-intercourse-act-1834`).
- **Criticism and change:** Marshall's racial language is now widely repudiated; *Sherrill* (2005) applied the doctrine's logic, and no court has overruled *Johnson*. Papal statements of repudiation exist (the Vatican's March 2023 statement `[from-memory]`) and tribal and academic critique is extensive.
- **Atlas use:** Show *Johnson* at the root of the graph, with edges to the Nonintercourse Acts (protection) and to every land cession (the mechanism of extinguishment).

### 2.2 Domestic dependent nations, inherent sovereignty, and preemption of state law

- **Source:** *Cherokee Nation v. Georgia* and *Worcester v. Georgia* (1831-32).
- **Modern forms:** The "infringement" test (*Williams v. Lee*, `case-williams-v-lee-1959`), preemption balancing (*Bracker*, `case-white-mountain-apache-v-bracker-1980`; *McClanahan*, `case-mcclanahan-v-arizona-tax-1973`), tribal taxation (*Merrion*, `case-merrion-v-jicarilla-1982`), and dual sovereignty (*Wheeler*).
- **Limits:** *Oliphant* (implicit divestiture), *Montana* (nonmember civil authority), *Castro-Huerta* (concurrent state criminal jurisdiction over non-Indians).
- **Statutory overlay:** Public Law 280 (`stat-public-law-280-1953`) shifted jurisdiction to some states; the ICRA (`stat-indian-civil-rights-act-1968`) added consent and retrocession.

### 2.3 Plenary power

- **Source:** The *Cherokee Tobacco* (last-in-time rule, 1871), *Kagama* (power rooted in guardianship, 1886), *Lone Wolf* (power to abrogate treaties; political question, 1903).
- **Statutory counterpart:** The Act of 1871 (`stat-indian-appropriations-act-1871`) ended treaty-making.
- **Constraints:** Compensation (*Sioux Nation*), Eleventh Amendment (*Seminole Tribe v. Florida*, `case-seminole-tribe-v-florida-1996`), constitutional limits implicit in the Fifth Amendment, and the clear-statement canons.
- **Contemporary use:** The Court uses plenary power to uphold laws protecting tribes (*Brackeen*, *Lara*), and the Atlas should show both sides of the doctrine.

### 2.4 Trust responsibility

- **Source:** *Seminole Nation* (1942) describes a strict fiduciary standard. *United States v. Mitchell* (Mitchell I, 445 U.S. 535 (1980) and Mitchell II, 463 U.S. 206 (1983)) `[from-memory; not in CSV]` separated the "bare" trust from a trust with specific duties that support money damages.
- **Narrowing:** *Navajo I* (2003), *Jicarilla* (2011), and *Arizona v. Navajo Nation* (2023).
- **Remedies in practice:** The Cobell litigation (`case-cobell-v-salazar-2009`) produced a $3.4 billion settlement, but the Court rarely enforces open-ended trust duties.
- **Atlas use:** Distinguish a **moral/historical trust responsibility** (frequently cited by agencies and Congress) from an **enforceable fiduciary duty** (narrow). Do not equate them.

### 2.5 Reserved rights (including water and fishing)

- **Source:** *Winans* (1905, off-reservation fishing as a reserved right) and *Winters* (1908, implied water right).
- **Developments:** *Arizona v. California* (1963; quantification by practicably irrigable acreage `[from-memory]`), *Cappaert v. United States*, 426 U.S. 128 (1976) `[verified]` (federal reserved water rights extend to groundwater needed for the purpose of a federal reservation), *Boldt* and *Fishing Vessel*, *Mille Lacs*, *Herrera*, *Cougar Den*, and the culverts case (`case-washington-v-us-culverts-2018`).
- **Limits:** *Race Horse* (1896, since undercut), *Oregon v. Klamath* (1985, cession ends exclusive off-reservation rights), *Arizona v. Navajo Nation* (2023, no affirmative duty to secure water).

### 2.6 Abrogation

- **Rule:** Congress may abrogate a treaty (*The Cherokee Tobacco*, *Lone Wolf*), but courts require clear evidence of intent (*Menominee*, *Mille Lacs*, *Herrera*; *United States v. Dion*, 476 U.S. 734 (1986) `[from-memory]`).
- **Related terms:** The Atlas must keep *abrogation* (ending a treaty term by statute), *diminishment* (shrinking a reservation), *disestablishment* (eliminating a reservation), and *termination* (ending federal recognition) distinct. See C1's terminology section.

### 2.7 Diminishment and disestablishment

- **Test (*Solem*, 1984):** Clear congressional intent, examined through the statutory text (most important), contemporaneous history, and later events. *Parker* (2016) and *McGirt* (2020) reaffirmed that only Congress may diminish or disestablish and that practical consequences or demographics cannot substitute for clear intent.
- **Outcome list:** See C1, Part 6 (nine decisions, five holding the reservation intact or restored, four diminishing).
- **Open question:** `case-mille-lacs-band-v-madore-2025` leaves the Mille Lacs status unresolved; Oklahoma's other Five Tribes reservations were affected by *McGirt*.

### 2.8 Nonintercourse, land claims, and equitable bars

- **Statutes:** Nonintercourse Acts, 25 U.S.C. 177.
- **Cases:** *Oneida I* and *II*, *Passamaquoddy*, *Mashpee*, then *Sherrill* and *Cayuga*; settlement acts in Maine (`stat-maine-indian-claims-settlement-act-1980`), Rhode Island, and South Carolina.
- **Pattern:** Strong title rights, limited remedies; settlement is common.

### 2.9 Land into trust and recognition

- **Statutory base:** IRA section 5 (`stat-indian-reorganization-act-1934`), now 25 U.S.C. 5108.
- **Limits:** *Carcieri* (1934 jurisdiction), *Sherrill* (the route to restore sovereignty over repurchased land), Alaska restrictions (`stat-alaska-trust-land-solicitor-opinion-m-37087-2026`), and *Chinook* (no judicial route to recognition, `case-chinook-indian-nation-v-burgum-2026`).
- **Expansion:** 2023 fee-to-trust rule (`stat-interior-part-151-land-acquisitions-rule-2023`), Mashpee litigation (`case-mashpee-wampanoag-carcieri-litigation-2016-2024`), Lumbee recognition (`stat-lumbee-recognition-ndaa-2025`).

### 2.10 Religion, sacred places, and cultural property

- **Case law:** *Lyng*, *Smith*, *Navajo v. Forest Service*, *Apache Stronghold* (all weak for tribes).
- **Statutes:** AIRFA, RFRA `[from-memory]`, NHPA Section 106, ARPA, NAGPRA, Blue Lake Return, the Antiquities Act, and Bears Ears co-management.
- **Pattern:** The Constitution protects belief but, as read, not access to sacred places on federal land; protection comes from statute, consultation, and executive action, which are reversible.

## Part 3. The canons of treaty construction and the cases that established them

The "canons" are interpretive rules courts use when reading Indian treaties and statutes. They derive from the unequal bargaining conditions of treaty-making and from the trust relationship.

| Canon | What it says | Founding and major cases | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Ambiguities are resolved in favor of the Indians** | Where the text is uncertain, courts choose the reading that favors the tribe | *Worcester* (1832) `[principle from-memory; pin cite unverified]`; *Jones v. Meehan* (1899) `[verified]`; *Carpenter v. Shaw* (1930), "Doubtful expressions are to be resolved in favor of the weak and defenseless people who are the wards of the nation" `[quotation verified]`; *Winters* (1908) ("ambiguities occurring will be resolved from the standpoint of the Indians," 207 U.S. at 576-77 `[quotation and pin from-memory]`); *McClanahan* (1973) | Active. Applied in *Herrera* (2019), *Cougar Den* (2019) and *McGirt* (2020) |
| **2. Treaties are read as the Indians would have understood them** | Because treaties were negotiated in unfamiliar languages and conditions, courts look to the Indians' understanding, not technical legal meaning | *Jones v. Meehan* (1899) (treaty "must therefore be construed not according to the technical meaning of its words to learned lawyers") `[partial quotation verified]`; *Choctaw Nation v. United States*, 119 U.S. 1 (1886) `[case verified; pin cite from-memory]`; *Tulee v. Washington* (1942) `[verified]`; *Choctaw Nation v. Oklahoma*, 397 U.S. 620 (1970) `[verified]`; *Fishing Vessel* (1979); *Mille Lacs* (1999) | Active; central to *Cougar Den* (Yakama understanding of "right to travel") and *Herrera* |
| **3. Reserved-rights doctrine** | A treaty is a grant of rights *from* the tribe, not *to* it; rights not ceded are retained, including implied ones needed for the reservation's purpose | *Winans* (1905); *Winters* (1908); *Menominee* (1968); *Boldt* line; *Arizona v. California* (1963); *Cappaert* (1976) | Strong for off-reservation rights (fishing, hunting, travel) and water; weaker for affirmative duties (*Arizona v. Navajo Nation*, 2023) |
| **4. Abrogation requires clear congressional intent** | Courts do not infer that Congress broke a treaty from vague or general language | *Menominee* (1968) `[verified]`; *Mille Lacs* (1999); *Herrera* (2019); *Dion* (1986) `[from-memory]`; for boundaries, *Solem* (1984), *Parker* (2016), *McGirt* (2020) | Active, but decisive evidence has been found where the statute is explicit (*Hagen*, *Yankton*, *Rosebud*, *DeCoteau*) |
| **5. Statutes are construed liberally for Indians** | Statutes passed for Indians' benefit are read in their favor | *Montana v. Blackfeet* (1985) `[verified]`; *Bryan v. Itasca County* (1976) `[verified]`; *Oneida II* (1985) | Active but limited where a statute clearly burdens a tribe (*South Carolina v. Catawba Indian Tribe*, 476 U.S. 498 (1986) `[verified, Justia; not in CSV]`) |
| **6. Treaties are the supreme law of the land; state law is preempted** | Treaty-reserved rights can override state regulation, subject to conservation necessity | *Worcester* (1832); *Winans*; *Tulee*; *Puyallup I* (1968); *Fishing Vessel* (1979) | Active |

### Counter-rules and limits that the Atlas should show beside the canons

- **Later-in-time rule:** A later statute overrides an earlier treaty (*Cherokee Tobacco*, 1871; *Lone Wolf*, 1903).
- **Statehood and cession:** *Race Horse* (1896) and *Oregon v. Klamath* (1985) found treaty rights ended by statehood or cession; *Mille Lacs* and *Herrera* narrowed *Race Horse* sharply.
- **Remedies and duties:** A treaty right does not by itself give an enforceable fiduciary duty or damages (*Navajo I*, *Arizona v. Navajo Nation*).
- **Dissents and counter-currents:** Recent treaty-text decisions were 5-4 (*Herrera*, *Cougar Den*, *McGirt*); the practical significance of the canons depends on the Court's makeup.
- **Pragmatic limits:** Equitable defenses (laches, impossibility) block remedies even where title is clear (*Sherrill*, *Cayuga*).

## Part 5. Atlas usage notes and risks

1. **Show both directions.** For every doctrine node, show cases that advanced and cases that eroded tribal rights. The `land_rights_effect` label is an editorial judgment; display it with its definition.
2. **Label legal categories exactly.** Use "abrogated" only when a court or statute ended a treaty term; "diminished" or "disestablished" only for reservation boundaries; "terminated" only for the 1950s policy; and a plain-language "broken" for U.S. non-performance. When a court case found no breach (for example *Arizona v. Navajo Nation* found no enforceable duty), say what was decided, not what a nation or advocate alleges.
3. **Do not imply a right is enforceable when it is not.** Several holdings recognize rights without remedies (*Sherrill*, *Cayuga*, *Arizona v. Navajo Nation*, *Sioux Nation*'s money-only award). The Atlas's counterfactual maps should note this.
4. **Respect tribal voice.** Present tribal positions (for example, the Sioux Nation's refusal of payment for the Black Hills; the Seneca Nation on Kinzua; the Apache on Oak Flat) in the nations' own words with attribution.
5. **Sacred places.** Do not geolocate ceremonial or burial sites. Where a case concerns a named sacred landscape, link to the nation's own statements and flag that publication needs tribal consultation.
6. **Currency.** Items dated 2025-2026 may change quickly (monument litigation, Alaska trust policy, Oak Flat). Show an "as of" date and a source link on each.
7. **Verification pass.** The verification agent should (a) re-check all `[from-memory]` pin cites and quotations, (b) check U.S. Reports page numbers for 2017-2023 decisions against the Supreme Court's website, and (c) run an ID integrity check on the graph edges above.
