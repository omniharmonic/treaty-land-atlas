## 0. A note on names: "Bureau of Indian Affairs," not "Bureau of Indigenous Affairs"

The commissioning request called the agency the "Bureau of Indigenous Affairs." The official name is the **Bureau of Indian Affairs (BIA)**, an agency of the U.S. Department of the Interior. The National Archives states that the office "was not formally called the Bureau of Indian Affairs until 1947" and that it was established under that name effective September 17, 1947 `[verified]` (National Archives, Record Group 75 guide). The BIA sits under the **Assistant Secretary for Indian Affairs (AS-IA)**, and the Department collectively refers to its Indian-affairs bureaus as "Indian Affairs."

Recommendation for the site: use the statutory and agency names exactly as they appear in law and records ("Bureau of Indian Affairs," "Bureau of Indian Education," "Indian Claims Commission," "Indian Country"), because readers will search records and statutes under those names. Use "Native," "Indigenous," and each nation's own name in the site's own voice. A short "why the old word appears" note in the glossary (see D4) handles the tension.

## 2. War Department Indian Office (1824) and the move to Interior (1849)

**What happened.** From 1789 until 1824, the Secretary of War supervised federal Indian affairs directly. On March 11, 1824, Secretary of War John C. Calhoun ordered the creation of an Office of Indian Affairs inside the War Department, headed by the former Supervisor of Indian Trade. It operated informally under that order until Congress created the position of Commissioner of Indian Affairs by the act of July 9, 1832 (4 Stat. 564). On March 3, 1849 (9 Stat. 395), the office moved to the new Department of the Interior, where it remains `[verified]` (National Archives, "Records of the Bureau of Indian Affairs" and "Native American Heritage: BIA").

**Why it matters for land.** The 1824 office was created in the years when federal policy turned from treaty-bargaining toward removal. It was the administrative machine behind the Indian Removal Act of 1830 (4 Stat. 411, signed May 28, 1830) `[verified-secondary]`. The 1849 transfer put the Indian office in the same department as the General Land Office (and later the BLM), which managed the sale and settlement of ceded land. This co-location is part of why the same department handled both "trust" over Indian land and disposal of the public domain. Historians commonly describe this as a structural conflict of interest. That interpretation is widely held but is an interpretive claim, not a statutory finding, so the site should attribute it rather than state it as fact `[unverified as a single sourced claim]`.

**Field structure.** The office created agencies (local offices) and, in the nineteenth century, superintendencies overseeing agents in a state or region `[verified]` (NARA).

**Naming.** See section 0. The office had no formal "Bureau" title until 1947 `[verified]`.

## 3. Board of Indian Commissioners (1869-1933)

Created by Congress on April 10, 1869 (16 Stat. 40), the Board authorized the President to appoint up to ten unpaid men "eminent for their intelligence and philanthropy." It advised on Native policy and inspected supplies delivered to agencies to check that treaty obligations were met. It was an all-Protestant body until 1902 (when two Catholics were appointed) and was terminated in 1933 under Commissioner John Collier, with no funding in the New Deal `[verified-secondary]` (Wikipedia, "Board of Indian Commissioners," citing the Statutes at Large). Its reform politics were closely tied to the Peace Policy and to the argument that allotment and schooling would "civilize" Native people, which links the Board to the allotment and boarding-school logic discussed in D2 `[from-memory]`. A primary-source check (Annual Reports of the Board; 16 Stat. 40) is needed.

## 5. General Land Office and Bureau of Land Management

- The **General Land Office** was established in 1812 within the Treasury Department to oversee disposition of acquired and ceded lands `[verified]` (BLM timeline).
- The **Homestead Act (1862)** let settlers claim 160 acres of public land after five years of residence and farming `[verified]` (BLM timeline). Much of that "public land" was land the United States had acquired by treaty or cession from Native nations, which is the factual basis for the Land Grab Universities and homestead-on-ceded-land arguments. Note that Royce cession polygons (see D3) identify the ceded areas; the tie between a specific homestead patent and a specific cession requires a GLO patent record search `[unverified for any individual tract]`.
- In 1946 the GLO and the Grazing Service merged to form the **Bureau of Land Management** in Interior `[verified]`.
- The **Federal Land Policy and Management Act of 1976** repealed the homestead laws and set a policy of retaining public land in federal ownership `[verified]` (BLM timeline).
- Practical atlas use: GLO patent records (BLM General Land Office Records) are the primary source for how ceded land was transferred to private owners. They are a candidate data source for a later wave `[unverified: licensing and API not checked]`.

## 6. Bureau of Reclamation and U.S. Army Corps of Engineers

**Bureau of Reclamation.** The Reclamation Act was passed June 17, 1902; Interior Secretary Hitchcock created the Reclamation Service within USGS in July 1902; it became the Bureau of Reclamation in 1923 `[verified]` (USBR history). Irrigation projects often drew on water that tribes held rights to under *Winters v. United States*, 207 U.S. 564 (1908), which held that the 1888 agreement creating the Fort Belknap Reservation implicitly reserved enough Milk River water for the tribes' needs and that Montana's statehood did not cancel that right `[verified]` (Justia). The Bureau historically built projects before tribal water rights were quantified, creating long-running disputes `[from-memory]`.

**Army Corps of Engineers and Pick-Sloan.** The Flood Control Act of December 22, 1944 (58 Stat. 887; Section 9 at 58 Stat. 891) authorized the Pick-Sloan Missouri Basin program `[verified]` (S. Rept. 111-357). In 1946 the Corps "entered the Fort Berthold Reservation, without notice, and began construction of the 212 foot-high Garrison dam." Garrison flooded about one-fourth of the reservation, about 94 percent of the Three Affiliated Tribes' agricultural land (about 156,000 acres), and forced 480 families to move. Across seven reservations the Pick-Sloan dams flooded about 356,254 acres and relocated more than 900 Indian families. Standing Rock lost 55,994 acres. Compensation came in many tranches (about $5 million, then about $7 million, then fourteen acts of Congress in total, including the Fort Berthold and Standing Rock Equitable Compensation Act of 1992) `[verified]` (S. Rept. 111-357). Dam construction ran to 1966 `[verified]`.

This is the best-documented example of **federal infrastructure extraction without a cession treaty**. The land was taken under the federal power of eminent domain and by acts of Congress rather than by a new treaty, and the Congress-appropriated compensation is on the record.

## 7. National Park Service, Forest Service, and other agencies on treaty-territory land

A cross-cutting pattern: federal land agencies administer land that was once reserved to, ceded by, or claimed by Native nations. The site should treat each as its own documented case, not as a generalization.

| Case | What is documented | Tag |
|---|---|---|
| **Glacier National Park / Blackfeet** | In 1895 the Blackfeet agreed to sell the mountain portion of their reservation for $1.5 million (reported as about 800,000 acres) while understanding they would keep hunting and other rights as long as the "ceded strip" remained public land. The park was established May 11, 1910. The U.S. position, confirmed by the Court of Claims in 1935, was that those rights ended; some Blackfeet dispute this. Ratification act not confirmed this session (believed 29 Stat. 321, 353, 1896) | Price and area `[verified-secondary]` (Wikipedia; a student-authored Union College page gives the same price); ratification citation `[from-memory]` |
| **Blue Lake / Taos Pueblo (Forest Service)** | A 1903 temporary forest withdrawal was made permanent in 1906, placing Blue Lake in a national forest. The Indian Claims Commission could award only money (nearly $300,000 in 1965); the Pueblo offered to waive it for the land. The Blue Lake bill (H.R. 471) passed the Senate 70-12 on Dec 2, 1970; President Nixon signed it Dec 15, 1970, returning 48,000 acres | `[verified]` (National Archives blog) |
| **National Bison Range / Confederated Salish and Kootenai Tribes (Fish and Wildlife Service)** | Range created 1908 on reservation land. Public Law 116-260 (Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2021) approved the transfer by repealing the range's founding statute; Interior announced June 23, 2021 the transfer of about 18,800.22 acres into trust for the Tribes | `[verified]` (DOI) |
| **Black Hills / Mount Rushmore (Forest Service, NPS)** | Act of Feb 28, 1877 took the Black Hills; *United States v. ## 8. Indian Claims Commission and the Court of Claims

**Indian Claims Commission (ICC).** Created by act of August 13, 1946 (60 Stat. 1049 `[from-memory]`), constituted April 10, 1947; tribes had five years to file `[verified]` (NARF text of ICC Final Report; Wikipedia). It could award **money only**: "it had no power to return any land" `[verified]` (EBSCO Research Starters); compensation was net acreage times market value at the time of taking `[verified-secondary]`. It closed under Pub. L. 94-465 on September 30, 1978, transferring about 170 unresolved cases to the U.S. Court of Claims `[verified]` (EBSCO; Wikipedia).

| Figure | Value | Source | Tag |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dockets filed | 617 (from 370 petitions) | ICC Final Report preface, NARF copy | `[verified]` |
| Dockets completed | 546 | Wikipedia; EBSCO | `[verified-secondary]` |
| Judgments | $818,172,606.64 | Wikipedia; EBSCO ("$818,172,606") | `[verified-secondary]` |
| Other totals | Some secondary sources cite higher totals (for example about $1.3 billion) | not found in a source read | `[disputed]` / `[unverified]` |

The ICC is best presented as the legal system's attempt to put a price on land it would not return. Claims were valued as of the date of taking, with no interest unless the taking was found unconstitutional. The Black Hills case shows the consequence: the ICC awarded $17.5 million without interest for the 1877 cession, and in 1980 the Supreme Court held the taking unconstitutional, entitling the Lakota to interest from 1877. The Lakota have declined the money, wanting the land `[verified]` (Justia; UNL Plains Humanities). The UNL source reports the fund grew to about $600 million by the late 1990s; later totals were not checked `[unverified]`.

**Court of Claims / Court of Federal Claims.** The Court of Claims received the ICC's pending docket on Sept 30, 1978 `[verified]`. The Court of Claims also heard the Sioux Nation case. Its successor is today's U.S. Court of Federal Claims (renamed from the Claims Court in 1992) `[from-memory]`. The Tucker Act (1887) opened the Court of Claims to some contract claims; Indian tribes' claims were largely barred from it before 1946 without special jurisdictional acts `[from-memory]`.

## 9. Cobell, trust reform, and the Office of Special Trustee

*Cobell v. Babbitt* (later *Cobell v. Salazar*) was filed June 10, 1996 as a class action on behalf of IIM account holders (about 300,000 as filed, per CRS), alleging the government failed to collect and disburse trust land revenues. A $3.4 billion settlement was announced Dec 8, 2009, and funded by the Claims Resolution Act of 2010. The district court approved it Aug 4, 2011, and it became final Nov 24, 2012. It created a $1.5 billion fund for accounting and mismanagement claims (distributed to nearly 500,000 class members) and a $1.9 billion Trust Land Consolidation Fund for buying fractionated interests, with up to $60 million for a scholarship fund `[verified]` (DOI press releases of Dec 2010 and Nov 26, 2012; CRS IF13173; Wikipedia for the filing and announcement dates). Details of the buy-back results are in D3.

## 10. Apologies and Interior investigations

### 10.1 Kevin Gover, September 8, 2000

On September 8, 2000, Assistant Secretary for Indian Affairs Kevin Gover (Pawnee) spoke at a ceremony marking the BIA's 175th anniversary, to roughly 300 people, mostly BIA employees `[verified]` (BIA press release "Gover Apologizes for BIA's Misdeeds"). Quoted language:

- "On behalf of the Bureau of Indian Affairs, I extend this formal apology to Indian people for the historical conduct of this agency." `[verified]`
- "As the nation looked to the West for more land, this agency participated in the ethnic cleansing" `[verified]` (full-speech copy on a public listserv archive; check against the BIA original).
- "Never again will we seize your children, nor teach them to be ashamed of who they are." `[verified]` (same listserv copy)
- He said he did not speak for the United States as a whole and that "we cannot yet ask your forgiveness." `[verified]` (listserv copy)

Scope: this is an agency apology, not a presidential or congressional one. The BIA's own page for the release does not quote land-specific language, so for the "land" element rely on the "ethnic cleansing" passage, and verify the quotation against the BIA original before the site prints it.

### 10.3 President Biden's apology, October 25, 2024

On Friday, October 25, 2024, at Gila Crossing Community School in the Gila River Indian Community (Laveen Village, near Phoenix, Arizona), President Biden said, "I formally apologize as president of the United States of America for what we did," called the apology "long overdue," and noted that the federal government had never formally apologized before `[verified]` (Alaska Public Media/AP). Note: this is a presidential apology, not an Act of Congress. An apology resolution passed by Congress for Native Americans exists in a different context (Pub. L. 111-118, Section 8113, 2009, the "Apology to Native Peoples of the United States") `[from-memory]`; verify before use.
