# D2. Federal Indian Policy Eras: A Timeline Spine for the Atlas

Research Agent D, Treaty Land Atlas. Tags: `[verified]`, `[verified-secondary]` (only a secondary or tertiary source read), `[from-memory]`, `[disputed]`, `[unverified]`. Companion files: D1 (institutions), D3 (statistics), D4 (glossary), and `seed-data/policy-eras-seed.csv`.

## 0. How to use this framework, and its limits

The nine eras below are a **teaching scaffold**, not a legal periodization. Scholars and agencies draw the boundaries differently (for example, FEMA's training material dates "Allotment and Attempted Assimilation" to 1871-1928, while the Indian Land Tenure Foundation traces federal allotment policy to 1798 `[verified-secondary]`). Eras overlap: treaty-making continued during removal; reservations were created while allotment was being designed; termination and self-determination overlapped in the late 1960s; Public Law 280 jurisdiction, boarding schools, and trust mismanagement run across several eras.

Recommended UI treatment for the timeline spine:

- Show eras as **overlapping bands**, not mutually exclusive boxes, with a short "why the overlap" tooltip.
- Each era card shows: dates, federal goal in the government's own words where possible, key acts (with Statutes at Large cites), effect on land (with a range if sources disagree), and a "Tribal voices" placeholder that requires a consulted, tribe-authored source before launch (brief principle 4).
- Keep "federal goal" separate from "Native response." The site should not present resistance, diplomacy, and cultural continuity only as reactions to federal policy.

Statutory language note: agency and statute names use the word "Indian." The site's own voice should use each nation's endonym where available.

## 2. Era 1: Colonial contact and early treaty-making (1763-1829)

**Legal frame.** European and colonial powers claimed title by "discovery" and negotiated with Native nations as diplomatic powers. After 1776 the United States adopted the practice: the Constitution gives Congress power "to regulate Commerce... with the Indian Tribes" (Art. I, sec. 8, cl. 3) `[from-memory]`. Early statutes, the **Trade and Intercourse Acts** (first 1790, 1 Stat. 137; permanent version 1834, 4 Stat. 729) `[from-memory]`, barred private and state purchases of Indian land without federal approval.

**Case law.** *Johnson & Graham's Lessee v. McIntosh*, 21 U.S. (8 Wheat.) 543 (1823), decided March 10, 1823, held that deeds private buyers obtained directly from tribes are not recognized in U.S. courts: discovery gave the government the exclusive right to acquire the land and left tribes with a right of occupancy `[verified]` (Justia). This is the doctrinal base of the "Doctrine of Discovery" in U.S. law.

**Treaties.** The BIA states that between 1778 and 1871 "the United States Senate ratified 370 treaties" and that at least 45 more were negotiated but not ratified `[verified]` (BIA FAQ). The National Archives also says "approximately 370" treaties, "several dozen" unratified `[verified]`. Date of the first ratified treaty: the BIA gives 1778 (the Delaware treaty), but a National Archives page as summarized by a fetch tool read "1788" `[disputed]`; most references place the Delaware treaty at Fort Pitt in 1778 `[from-memory]`; flag for the treaty team.

**Institutions.** Secretary of War directs Indian affairs; Office of Indian Trade 1806-1822; Office of Indian Affairs created March 11, 1824 `[verified]` (see D1).

**Land effect.** Royce's *Indian Land Cessions in the United States* (Eighteenth Annual Report of the Bureau of American Ethnology, Part 2, 1899; 67 maps; schedule runs "from the organization of the Federal Government to and including 1894") is the standard compilation `[verified]` (Library of Congress; USFS dataset page). Totals in acres are not stated in the compilations I read; see D3 for the range of published estimates.

**Tribal voice flag.** Treaty negotiation records are written by the United States. Oral accounts of what was agreed (for example, the Lakota understanding of the 1868 treaty) often differ; the site should flag treaty entries that have a known tribal-side account.

## 3. Era 2: Removal (1830-1850)

**Key act.** Indian Removal Act, signed May 28, 1830, 4 Stat. 411, Pub. L. 21-148: "An Act to provide for an exchange of lands with the Indians residing in any of the states or territories" `[verified-secondary]` (Wikipedia). It authorized exchanging land west of the Mississippi for land in the East. Removal treaties followed: the Treaty of Dancing Rabbit Creek (Choctaw, signed Sept 27, 1830) and the Treaty of New Echota (1835), which resulted in the Cherokee removal (the "Trail of Tears," 1838-1839) `[verified-secondary]`. More than 60,000 people from at least 18 tribes were forced west during the Jackson and Van Buren presidencies; about 3,000 Seminoles were removed in the Second Seminole War (1835-1842) `[verified-secondary]`.

**Cases.** *Cherokee Nation v. Georgia*, 30 U.S. (5 Pet.) 1 (1831) (tribes as "domestic dependent nations") `[from-memory]`; *Worcester v. Georgia*, 31 U.S. (6 Pet.) 515 (1832), decided March 3, 1832, held Georgia's law void because the Cherokee Nation is a self-governing territory outside Georgia's authority and the federal government alone controls relations with it `[verified]` (Justia). The Court's ruling was not enforced against Georgia in the way the decision implied `[from-memory]`; cite a historian for this.

**Institutional.** Commissioner of Indian Affairs created by statute July 9, 1832 (4 Stat. 564) `[verified]`; Gover's 2000 apology names removal: "these great tribal nations were made to march 1,000 miles to the west" `[verified]` (full-text copy).

**Land effect.** Eastern homelands ceded or vacated; ceded land entered the public domain administered by the General Land Office. A systematic acreage for removal-era cessions requires Royce schedule queries `[unverified]`.

**Terminology.** "Removal" was a federal policy, usually accomplished through treaties of cession (often signed under pressure or by unauthorized signatories, as with New Echota) and military force. Do not label removal-treaty cessions "voluntary," and do not label them "illegal" without a source for a specific legal finding. Cherokee scholarship disputes the legitimacy of New Echota (signed by a minority faction) `[from-memory]`; consult Cherokee Nation sources.

## 4. Era 3: Reservation (1851-1886)

**Goal.** Confine nations to defined reservations to open land for settlement, railroads and mining, and to pursue "civilization." The Indian Appropriations Act of 1851 (9 Stat. 574) is often cited as the start of the reservation system `[from-memory]`. Many reservations were established by treaty (to 1868 especially) and, after 1871, by **executive order** or by Congress.

**End of treaty-making.** The Appropriations Act rider of March 3, 1871 (16 Stat. 566), now 25 U.S.C. § 71, states: "No Indian nation or tribe within the territory of the United States shall be acknowledged or recognized as an independent nation, tribe, or power with whom the United States may contract by treaty; but no obligation of any treaty lawfully made and ratified with any such Indian nation or tribe prior to March 3, 1871, shall be hereby invalidated or impaired." `[verified]` (Cornell LII). After 1871 the United States made **agreements** ratified by both houses (statutes rather than treaties) with many nations, and used executive orders `[from-memory]`. The BIA states "Congress ended treaty-making with Indian tribes in 1871" `[verified]`.

**Peace Policy and the Board of Indian Commissioners (1869).** See D1.

**Case law.** *Lone Wolf v. Hitchcock*, 187 U.S. 553 (1903), decided Jan 5, 1903: Congress's plenary power over tribal affairs allowed it to pass a law allotting tribal lands and selling the surplus despite a treaty requirement that three-fourths of adult male members consent, and courts could not review Congress's motives `[verified]` (Justia). It is a keystone for how "treaty versus statute" conflicts were resolved in the federal courts (and the site's "broken versus abrogated" distinction). The Supreme Court has also decided that a later statute can abrogate a treaty only with clear intent, a principle reaffirmed in cases like *United States v. Dion* (1986) `[from-memory]`; verify.

**Black Hills.** The Act of Feb 28, 1877 took the Black Hills from the Sioux; *United States v. Sioux Nation of Indians*, 448 U.S. 371 (1980) held it a taking without just compensation `[verified]`. The Court affirmed $17.1 million (the 1877 fair market value) plus 5% annual interest from 1877; the earlier ICC finding of $17.5 million had been without interest `[verified]` (Justia). The Lakota have declined the money; the award, with interest, was reported at about $600 million by the late 1990s `[verified-secondary]` (UNL).

**Land effect.** Wilson's 1935 Land Planning Report (Table VII) reports the **original acreage of Indian reservations** at 130,730,190 acres, plus 9,106,312 acres "subsequently added," for a "maximum total tribal acreage" of 139,836,502 acres up to September 1934, with 38,299,109 acres of "ceded lands" (lands "to which title was formally relinquished by treaty") deducted from reservations `[verified]` (University of Virginia Land Sales site). This is a different measure from the "138 million acres in 1887" figure (see 5.3), and the two should not be conflated.

## 5. Era 4: Allotment and assimilation (1887-1933)

### 5.1 Key acts

| Act | Date | Cite | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| General Allotment (Dawes) Act | Feb 8, 1887 | 24 Stat. 388, Pub. L. 49-105 | President authorized to allot reservation land: 160 acres to heads of families, 80 to single people over 18 and orphans, 40 to those under 18 (as enacted; an 1891 amendment changed shares) `[verified-secondary]`. "Surplus" land opened to non-Indian settlement. |
| Curtis Act | 1898 | 30 Stat. 495 `[from-memory]` | Extended allotment to the Five Tribes (Cherokee, Chickasaw, Choctaw, Muscogee (Creek), Seminole) in Indian Territory, abolished tribal courts and communal land holding `[verified-secondary]` (Wikipedia). |
| Dead Indian Act | 1902 | `[from-memory]` | Allowed sale of inherited allotments (heirship land) |
| Burke Act | 1906 | 34 Stat. 182 `[from-memory]` | Allowed early fee patents; trust protection ended earlier for "competent" allottees |
| Indian Citizenship Act | 1924 | 43 Stat. 253 `[from-memory]` | Citizenship for Native people born in the U.S. who were not already citizens |
| Meriam Report | 1928 | | Institute for Government Research survey, "The Problem of Indian Administration"; criticized allotment and boarding schools `[verified-secondary]` |

### 5.2 Assimilation machinery

- The Federal Indian Boarding School Initiative identified **408 schools** in Volume I (May 11, 2022) and **417 institutions** (451 sites) in Volume II (July 30, 2024), operating from 1819 to 1969; at least **973** child deaths documented; at least **74** burial sites at 65 school sites; more than **$23.3 billion** (FY2023 dollars) in appropriations 1871-1969 `[verified]` (see D1 §10.2 and the section 12 below).
- Volume II notes Interior issued land patents to religious institutions tied to school or mission work, with a 1922 law capping such patents at 160 acres and providing for reversion to the Indian owner when no longer used for mission or school purposes `[verified]` (Volume II, pp. 49-50).

### 5.3 Acreage lost: the "138 million to 48 million" figure

Claim as widely circulated: Indian land holdings fell from **138 million acres in 1887** to **48 million acres in 1934**, a loss of about **90 million acres**.

| Source | What it says | Tag |
|---|---|---|
| UNL Great Plains Studies encyclopedia | "By the time the allotment process was stopped in 1934, the amount of Indian-held land in the United States had dropped from 138 million acres to 48 million acres." (1887 baseline implied) | `[verified-secondary]` |
| Indian Land Tenure Foundation (iltf.org) | "138 million acres in 1887 to 48 million acres by 1934"; "90 million acres" removed; "60 million acres were either ceded outright or sold" as surplus; "27 million acres" lost under the 1902 to 1908 acts | `[verified-secondary]` (arithmetic: 60 + 27 = 87, close to but not equal to 90) |
| Wikipedia, "Dawes Act" | Same 138 to 48 figures; about 90 million acres, "roughly two-thirds" of the 1887 base | `[verified-secondary]` |
| NativeGov (Native Governance Center) | Same figures; says "more than 90 million acres" stolen during the allotment era; figures not attributed | `[verified-secondary]` |
| Wilson, 1935 Land Planning Report, Table VII | Maximum total tribal acreage 139,836,502; acreage allotted 40,848,172; acreage "alienated" from trust status by allotment 23,225,472; other deductions 64,468,984 (ceded 38,299,109; surplus 22,694,658; misc. 3,475,217). "Alienated" means "removed from the 25-year-trust status," including land held in fee by Indians, so it is not equal to land loss | `[verified]` (primary-adjacent; the exact edition and author were not displayed on the page) |
| Farrell et al., *Science* (2021) | Cites the 90 million acre figure as "oft-cited" and attributes it to prior work | `[verified-secondary]` |

**Assessment.** The 138 and 48 million figures are repeated consistently by secondary and advocacy sources, but **I did not find a primary source (such as a Commissioner of Indian Affairs report or Cohen's Handbook table) that supplies both numbers for exactly 1887 and 1934** `[unverified at primary level]`. The Wilson table gives a similar 1934-era upper bound (about 140 million acres) and a different decomposition. The site should present the figure as "about 138 million acres to about 48 million acres, widely cited," link to the Wilson table, and state that it measures Indian-held land (tribal plus allotted, including some land under fee patent). Verification agent: look for the Commissioner of Indian Affairs annual reports, 1934 Collier statements (Senate hearings on the IRA), and Cohen's Handbook.

**Tribal-specific effect.** Some nations lost far more than average. The Native Governance Center cites Cris Stainbrook (Indian Land Tenure Foundation) saying the Leech Lake Band of Ojibwe owns "4% of their original reservation," and says the Yurok Tribe "owns about a quarter of its original land" `[verified-secondary]`. These are anecdotal and not for use without checking.

**Fractionation begins.** Allotments passed to heirs, whose numbers multiplied each generation (see D3, section on fractionation).

## 6. Era 5: Indian New Deal / Indian Reorganization (1934-1944)

**Key act.** Indian Reorganization Act (IRA, "Wheeler-Howard Act"), June 18, 1934, ch. 576, 48 Stat. 984, Pub. L. 73-383; now 25 U.S.C. § 5101 et seq. `[verified]` (Cornell LII; Wikipedia).

- **Ended allotment.** 25 U.S.C. § 5101: "on and after June 18, 1934, no land of any Indian reservation, created or set apart by treaty or agreement with the Indians... shall be allotted in severalty to any Indian" `[verified]`.
- **Section 5 (25 U.S.C. § 5108)** authorizes the Secretary to acquire land and hold it in trust for tribes and individuals; this is the statutory root of modern fee-to-trust `[verified]` (cited in the 2023 Federal Register rule).
- Tribal constitutions and councils; tribes voted to accept or reject the Act. Counts differ: 172 accepted and 75 rejected (Wikipedia) versus 174 and 78 (an Indian Country Today footnote that Wikipedia cites) `[disputed]`.
- Wikipedia reports "more than two million acres... were returned to various tribes in the first 20 years after passage" `[verified-secondary]`; not checked against a primary BIA source. A 1957 BIA press release shows a different measure: over 1,000,000 acres added to tribal holdings in the three preceding years (1954-1956), led by 818,277 acres restored to the Colville Tribe in 1956 `[verified]`.

**Why the era boundary is 1944, not "now."** The IRA is still law. The "era" ended in federal practice with the shift toward termination, but IRA-based trust acquisition continues, as does the *Carcieri* problem: *Carcieri v. Salazar*, 555 U.S. 379 (2009), decided Feb 24, 2009, held that "now under Federal jurisdiction" in the IRA refers to tribes under federal jurisdiction in 1934, so the Secretary could not take the Narragansett Tribe's 31-acre parcel into trust `[verified]` (Justia).

**Critiques the site should include.** IRA constitutions imposed a federal template on many nations; some nations rejected the Act; Native resistance to reorganization and to Collier's grazing reductions (for example Navajo livestock reduction) is documented `[from-memory]`; find tribal sources.

## 7. Era 6: Termination (1953-1968, with 1945 run-up and 1970 repudiation)

**Goal.** End the federal trust relationship and special status, and move Native people into mainstream society.

**Key acts.**
- House Concurrent Resolution 108, announced Aug 1, 1953 (67 Stat. B132) `[verified]` (NARA).
- Public Law 280, Aug 15, 1953 (67 Stat. 588): transferred criminal and civil jurisdiction over most reservations in California, Minnesota, Nebraska, Oregon and Wisconsin (and Alaska later) to those states `[verified]` (NARA; later states by opting in `[from-memory]`).
- Menominee Termination Act (1954; termination finalized April 30, 1961); Klamath Termination Act (Aug 13, 1954); Western Oregon Indian Termination Act (1954; terminated 61 tribes); California Rancheria Termination Act (1958; 41 rancherias, 7 more added in 1964); Indian Relocation Act (1956) `[verified-secondary]` (Wikipedia).
- Indian Civil Rights Act (1968) is generally treated as a bridge to the next era `[from-memory]`.

**Scale: figures disagree.**

| Measure | Value | Source | Tag |
|---|---|---|---|
| Termination proceedings | "60 separate termination proceedings" from 1953 to 1970 | NARA | `[verified]` |
| Tribes and bands | "more than 100 tribes and bands" terminated 1953-1964 (a commonly cited count of 109 `[from-memory]`) | Wikipedia | `[verified-secondary]` |
| Acres out of trust | "Approximately 2,500,000 acres" | Wikipedia | `[verified-secondary]` |
| Acres relinquished | "over three million acres of tribal lands were relinquished" | NARA | `[verified]` |
| People affected | about 12,000 (about 3% of the Native population) | Wikipedia | `[verified-secondary]` |

The gap between 2.5 million and 3-plus million acres should be shown as a range. The count of "terminated tribes" differs because one act (Western Oregon) terminated 61 tribes and because the unit (tribe, band, rancheria) varies.

**Repudiation and restoration.** President Nixon's message of July 8, 1970 called termination "an incorrect policy" `[verified-secondary]`. Restorations include the Menominee Restoration Act (1973), Klamath Restoration Act (1986), Alabama-Coushatta and Ysleta del Sur Pueblo (Pub. L. 100-89, Aug 18, 1987), Ponca (Oct 31, 1990), and rancherias restored through litigation such as *Hardwick* (1983); of 46 terminated rancherias, 31 restored and 6 seeking restoration `[verified-secondary]` (Wikipedia).

**Also in this era (infrastructure).** Pick-Sloan dams (1944-1966) and Garrison Dam flooding of Fort Berthold (1946 onward); about 356,254 acres flooded across seven reservations `[verified]` (S. Rept. 111-357). See D1 §6.

**Also in this era (claims).** The Indian Claims Commission (1946-1978) paid money for land; see D1 §8.

## 8. Era 7: Self-determination (1970-1987)

**Turning point.** President Nixon's "Message from the President of the United States Transmitting Recommendations for Indian Policy," July 8, 1970, recommended self-determination as a goal and rejected termination `[verified-secondary]`.

**Key acts.**
- **Blue Lake Return Act**, signed Dec 15, 1970: returned 48,000 acres taken into a national forest in 1906 to Taos Pueblo `[verified]` (National Archives).
- **Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act (ANCSA)**, Dec 18, 1971, Pub. L. 92-203, 85 Stat. 688, 43 U.S.C. § 1601 et seq.: Alaska Natives received approximately 45 million acres (CRS) and approximately $962.5 million, through 12 regional corporations (plus a 13th, landless) and over 200 village corporations `[verified]` (CRS R46997). The commonly quoted figure is 44 million acres `[from-memory]`; sources differ; the settlement extinguished aboriginal claims and did not create reservations (except Metlakatla) `[from-memory]`.
- **Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act (ISDEAA)**, Pub. L. 93-638, Jan 4, 1975, 88 Stat. 2203, 25 U.S.C. § 5301 et seq. `[verified]` (Cornell LII): "638 contracts" allow tribes to administer programs the BIA and IHS formerly ran `[verified-secondary]`.
- Indian Child Welfare Act (1978), American Indian Religious Freedom Act (1978), Indian Land Consolidation Act (1983) `[from-memory]` for citations.
- **Indian Gaming Regulatory Act (IGRA)** (1988, Pub. L. 100-497) is often placed at the edge of this era and the next `[from-memory]`; it governs gaming on trust lands acquired after Oct 17, 1988 (the cutoff) `[verified-secondary]` (DOI 2005 testimony).

**Land effect.** Mixed: targeted restorations such as Blue Lake and ANCSA coexisted with the continued fractionation of allotments (ILCA's 1983 escheat provision was struck down in *Hodel v. Irving*, 481 U.S. 704 (1987), a 439-owner, $1,080-income tract example `[verified]` (Oklahoma Bar Journal, citing the case), and the amended version in *Babbitt v. Youpee*, 519 U.S. 234 (1997) `[verified]`).

## 9. Era 8: Self-governance (1988-2000)

- **1988 amendments to ISDEAA** created a self-governance demonstration project `[from-memory]`.
- **Tribal Self-Governance Act of 1994**: Title II of Pub. L. 103-413 (enacted Oct 25, 1994), 108 Stat. 4270, which enacted subchapter IV of 25 U.S.C. chapter 46 (§ 5361 et seq.) `[verified]` (Cornell LII). It made self-governance permanent for Interior programs `[from-memory]`.
- **Federally Recognized Indian Tribe List Act of 1994** requires an annual list of recognized tribes (Pub. L. 103-454) `[from-memory]`; the current list is published in the Federal Register (see D3).
- **American Indian Trust Fund Management Reform Act of 1994** established the Office of the Special Trustee `[verified]` (CRS).
- **Cobell litigation** filed June 10, 1996 `[verified-secondary]`.
- **Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act (NAGPRA)**, Nov 16, 1990, Pub. L. 101-601, 25 U.S.C. § 3001 et seq. `[verified]` for the Code; the Stat. cite 104 Stat. 3048 is `[from-memory]`. Provides for return of human remains and cultural items by federal agencies and museums.

This era is mainly institutional; the land consequence is mostly through gaming revenue funding land purchases, and through the beginning of reparative law.

## 12. Boarding schools as part of land-for-assimilation (brief)

Only claims from the Department of the Interior's own investigative reports and press materials are used here.

- **Scale.** Volume I (May 11, 2022): 408 schools, 431 sites, 1819-1969. Volume II (July 30, 2024): 417 institutions, 451 sites; at least 973 deaths; at least 74 burial sites at 65 school sites; $23.3 billion in appropriations, 1871-1969 `[verified]`.
- **Link to land, in the report's own words.** Volume II states that "Indian Tribes ceded to the United States approximately 1 billion acres of land" and quotes a 1969 Senate report: "Treaties, although almost always signed under duress, were the window dressing whereby we expropriated the Indian's land" `[verified]` (Volume II, pp. 44-45, as read through a fetch tool). It records that education provisions appeared in treaties: **127** treaties by one count (p. 17), **171** by another (pp. 45-46) `[disputed]`; NABS's page on the report also says 127 `[verified]`.
- **Land patents to schools.** Interior "issued numerous land patents to religious institutions and organizations for existing religious or school activities"; the 1909 law authorized unrestricted patents; the 1922 law capped them at 160 acres with reversion `[verified]` (Vol. II, pp. 49-50).
- **Land-base problem today.** Recommendation 4 notes that "Many Indian Tribes do not have the land base to rebury human remains"; Recommendation 5 proposes helping return former school sites `[verified]` (Vol. II, p. 27).
- **The framing "boarding schools served land dispossession"** is a reasonable synthesis (schools were funded in part under treaty education provisions tied to land cessions, and assimilation was paired with allotment in the Dawes era), but I did **not** find a single sentence in the DOI reports stating that causal claim. Present it as the site's synthesis and attribute the supporting facts above. `[unverified as a direct DOI claim]`
- **Sensitivity.** The reports discuss unmarked graves; the site should not publish burial-site locations and should invite survivor and descendant guidance. Cite the 2000 Gover apology and the 2024 Biden apology as public statements.
