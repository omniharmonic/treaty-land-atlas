## 2. Recognized tribes

### 2.1 Federally recognized

- **575** tribal entities on the list published Jan 30, 2026. The notice "publishes the current list of 575 Tribal entities" recognized and eligible for BIA funding and services; it updates the Dec 11, 2024 list (89 FR 99899) and adds the Lumbee Tribe following the National Defense Authorization Act for FY2026, enacted December 18, 2025. Its eligibility for services is subject to conditions: verification of the tribal roll, a needs determination, and a delay until the third fiscal year after enactment. The notice also adds former names in parentheses for renamed tribes `[verified]` (GovInfo, 91 Fed. Reg., Jan 30, 2026).
- **574** on the Dec 11, 2024 list `[verified]` (GovInfo, 89 Fed. Reg., Dec 11, 2024); 574 as of January 2020 (CRS R46647 p. 3 n. 6) `[verified]`; DOI's Dec 2023 buy-back release also cites 574 `[verified]`.
- **Alaska**: 229 of the entries are in Alaska (so mostly Alaska Native villages), according to Wikipedia's "Federally recognized tribe" article, which also oddly displays 575 in its header while saying 574 in text `[verified-secondary]`; check against the Federal Register list itself.
- **Reservations**: the federal government "administers approximately 326 land areas as Indian reservations" (CRS R46647, p. 17, citing a BIA FAQ) `[verified]`. There are fewer reservations than recognized tribes because many Alaska entities have no reservation (ANCSA corporations hold fee land) and some tribes share a reservation.
- **Why the count is not a single number.** Sources vary by date. The count includes Alaska Native villages, and listed status is not the same as treaty status or as service eligibility (Lumbee). Several nations with treaties are not federally recognized, and some recognized tribes have no treaty. For definitions see D4.

### 2.2 Recognition pathways

- **Administrative acknowledgment** under 25 CFR Part 83 (BIA rules first issued in 1978; seven criteria; petitions have taken 12 to 14 years; Shinnecock petitioned in 1978 and was recognized in 2010) `[verified-secondary]` (Wikipedia). A revised Part 83 was issued in 2015 (80 Fed. Reg. 37861 `[from-memory]`) and further revisions were proposed in 2022 (a Federal Register notice dated April 27, 2022 appeared in search results but was not read) `[unverified]`.
- **Congressional recognition** (for example Lumbee, 2025) or **court order**. No counts of tribes by pathway were found in a source I read `[unverified]`.
- **List Act (1994)** requires publication of the list `[from-memory]`.
- **Termination and restoration** altered the list (see D2 §7). Wikipedia states 31 of 46 terminated rancherias restored and 6 still seeking restoration `[verified-secondary]`.

### 3.1 Dispossession as a share of ancestral area (peer-reviewed estimate)

Farrell, Ahtone and colleagues' 2021 study in *Science* (contiguous United States) reports `[verified-secondary]` (NSF Public Access copy and the Christian Science Monitor):

- Historical spatial coverage: **7,011,450 km²** (about 1.73 billion acres `[calc]`) compared with **426,598 km²** today: a **93.9%** reduction. Cumulative coextensive lands (which count overlapping tribal areas separately) show a **98.9%** reduction.
- Tribes that still hold land hold on average **2.6%** of their historical area (median 0.4%).
- **42.1%** of tribes with a documented historical presence have no federally or state-recognized land base today.
- Native peoples were forcibly moved an average of about **150 miles** (as reported by the CSM).
- Data sources include Kappler (1904), the University of Nebraska American Indian Treaties Portal, Royce cession schedules, ICC records, and Native Land Digital for cross-checking.

Note the difference between "93.9%" (area) and "98.8%" (the CSM's phrase about share of land tribes "no longer possess," which the paper's cumulative metric rounds to about 99%).

## 7. Fractionation, heirship, Cobell, and the Land Buy-Back Program

### 7.1 The problem

When an allottee died, ownership of the allotment passed to heirs as undivided shares. Over generations the number of co-owners grew. The classic example from the Supreme Court: in *Hodel v. Irving*, 481 U.S. 704 (1987), Tract 1305 (40 acres, $1,080 in annual income, valued at $8,000) had **439 owners**, one-third receiving less than $.05 a year and two-thirds less than $1; the largest owner received $82.85 a year and the smallest $.01 every 177 years `[verified]` (481 U.S. at 713, Justia). The Court struck down the Indian Land Consolidation Act's escheat provision as an unconstitutional taking; a revised version was also struck down in *Babbitt v. Youpee*, 519 U.S. 234 (1997) `[verified]` (same source). The Native Governance Center gives the definition: "after the death of the original allottee owner, title ownership is divided up among heirs," and "some individual parcels have hundreds of 'owners'" `[verified-secondary]`.

An OBJ illustration: with three children per generation, "there would be 243 heirs and potential co-owners" within six generations (more than 1,000 with four children) `[verified-secondary]`; under 25 CFR 162.012(a), a "highly fractionated tract" has 20 or more owners, and leasing requires 50% co-owner consent `[verified-secondary]`.

**Baseline (Dec 18, 2012):** "more than 92,000 tracts of land held in trust for American Indians contain 2.9 million fractional interests" `[verified]` (DOI press release launching the program). Owner counts were not stated in the pages I read `[unverified]`.

Why it matters for land: fractionated tracts are hard to lease, develop, or use, tend to sit idle or be managed by the BIA, and often cannot be sold or improved without many owners' consent. Fractionation continues because trust land passes to heirs; the American Indian Probate Reform Act of 2004 (Pub. L. 108-374 `[from-memory]`) tried to limit it `[verified-secondary]` for the date.

### 7.2 Cobell settlement

- *Cobell v. Babbitt* was filed June 10, 1996 by Elouise Cobell and other representatives as a class action against Interior and Treasury over IIM account mismanagement `[verified-secondary]`. A class of about 300,000 account holders was described by CRS at filing `[verified]`.
- Settlement announced Dec 8, 2009 at $3.4 billion `[verified-secondary]`; Congress funded it in the Claims Resolution Act of 2010; district court approval Aug 4, 2011; final Nov 24, 2012 after appeals `[verified]` (DOI, Nov 26, 2012).
- Two funds: **$1.5 billion** for accounting and trust mismanagement claims (nearly 500,000 class members), and **$1.9 billion** Trust Land Consolidation Fund for voluntary purchases of fractional interests; **up to $60 million** of the $1.9 billion could be set aside for scholarships `[verified]` (DOI).

### 7.3 Land Buy-Back Program for Tribal Nations (LBBP)

Purchases began Dec 2013; the ten-year window closed **Nov 24, 2022** `[verified]` (DOI program page). The Secretary established the program in 2012 `[verified]`.

**How the numbers differ.** These are **not contradictory measurements of the same thing** so much as snapshots at different dates and in different units: 2.3 million acres (through 2018), nearly 2.6 million (through 2019), about 2.9 million to "nearly 3 million" (final). The "acres" figures count the acreage equivalent of fractional interests purchased (an owner buying 10 percent of an 80-acre tract counts as 8 acres consolidated), not the land area that changed hands. Present the final number as "about 2.9 million acre-equivalents of fractional interests" `[verified-secondary]` and note that the land stays where it is; what changes is who owns the undivided shares (now the tribe, in trust).

**What the program did not fix.** The Final Report says purchases could not be implemented at 63 percent of the roughly 150 targeted locations `[verified-secondary]`, and that fractionation rose in some places (Cherokee Reservation +283%; Muscogee (Creek) Reservation +63%, possibly related to *McGirt*-era jurisdiction changes, which is my inference and is `[unverified]`). Interior's FY2027 statement says "Interior has prioritized addressing the probate backlog" `[verified]`.

## 8. Land-into-trust (fee-to-trust): process and recent changes

### 8.1 What it is

Land into trust (fee-to-trust) transfers a parcel from fee ownership to title held by the United States in trust for a tribe or an individual Indian. Statutory root: Section 5 of the Indian Reorganization Act, 25 U.S.C. § 5108 (48 Stat. 984) `[verified]`. Regulations are at **25 CFR Part 151** `[verified]`.

### 8.2 The 2023 rule (effective Jan 11, 2024)

Federal Register, 88 FR 86222 (Dec 12, 2023), Doc. 2023-27077, RIN 1076-AF71; proposed Dec 5, 2022 (87 FR 74334); comments to March 1, 2023 `[verified]`.

- **Timeline:** BIA must decide within **120 days** of a complete application package; completeness notice within 30 days (the prior rule had no deadline) `[verified]`.
- **Four categories:** on-reservation, contiguous, off-reservation, and a new "initial Indian acquisition" (§ 151.12) for tribes without trust land `[verified]`.
- **Policy shift:** the Secretary's policy "expressly supports trust acquisitions"; "protect" homelands rather than "establish" them; adds climate and cultural-practice purposes `[verified]`.
- ***Carcieri* analysis (§ 151.4):** three evidence categories (conclusive, presumptive, probative) for "under Federal jurisdiction" in 1934 `[verified]`.
- **Definitions:** "Indian reservation" revised for Oklahoma after *McGirt*; Alaska exception removed `[verified]`.
- **Baseline figures from the preamble:** average time to a final decision "approximately 985 days"; "941 cases pending approval"; BIA "has acquired over a million acres of land into trust for Tribes and individual Indians since... 1934" `[verified]` (as quoted via fetch tool; first 100,000 characters of the 135,000-character document read).
- Acquisitions mandated by Federal law are outside Part 151 (§ 151.1) `[verified]`.
- Regulations were "first published in 1980" `[verified]` (DOI 2005 testimony page). IGRA cutoff date for after-acquired trust land: Oct 17, 1988 `[verified]`.

### 8.3 Related legal constraints

- ***Carcieri v. Salazar*, 555 U.S. 379 (2009)**: the Secretary may take land into trust only for tribes "under Federal jurisdiction" in 1934 `[verified]`. Tribes recognized later (for example Lumbee in 2025) face this issue unless Congress specifies otherwise `[from-memory]`; check the Lumbee act's trust language `[unverified]`.
- **Alaska:** history of Interior positions: 2014 regulation removing the "Alaska exception" after a 2013 ruling; M-37043 (2017); withdrawn by M-37053 (2018-2021) and rescinded by M-37064; M-37069/M-37076 restored authority (2021-2023, with a trust acquisition for Central Council Tlingit and Haida); and **M-37087 (Feb 24, 2026)** revoked it again, with rulemaking planned `[verified-secondary]` (Brownstein Hyatt Farber Schreck, March 11, 2026). The 2023 rule had removed the Alaska exception from the regulations `[verified]`.
- Gaming-purpose acquisitions are subject to IGRA's § 20 exceptions and two-part determinations `[verified-secondary]` (DOI 2005 testimony).

### 8.4 Gaps

- No current (2025-2026) official tally of fee-to-trust approvals, pending cases or average processing time was found `[unverified]`.
- Whether Interior has initiated rulemaking to revise the 2023 rule beyond Alaska is not verified `[unverified]`.

1. **Current tribal-vs-individual split of the 56 million trust acres** was not found in any page read; need BIA Division of Real Estate Services or GAO. Also no number for fee land owned by tribes or Native individuals, restricted-fee land, or Alaska Native corporation land (45 million acre ANCSA conveyance is the only figure).

Recognition status is political and legal, and many unrecognized nations assert their own sovereignty; the site should avoid implying that unrecognized nations do not exist.
