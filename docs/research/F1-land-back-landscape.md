## 1. What "Land Back" means (and who defines it)

There is no single authoritative definition. The site should present several and name who wrote each one.

| Source | Definition or framing | Tag |
|---|---|---|
| NDN Collective, LANDBACK Manifesto | "It is the reclamation of everything stolen from the original Peoples" (opening line of the manifesto page). The campaign page says LANDBACK is about "returning Indigenous Lands to Indigenous hands" and names the closure of Mount Rushmore and return of public lands in the Black Hills as its cornerstone effort. | `[verified]` (landback.org/manifesto and /donate) |
| Yellowhead Institute (Indigenous-run, Toronto Metropolitan University) | "The project of land back is about reclaiming Indigenous jurisdiction: breathing life into rights and responsibilities." Its first Red Paper, titled *Land Back*, was released in October 2019 and concerns Canada. | `[verified]` |
| High Country News explainer (author's gloss) | "The land. Back." The movement is "less about a mass real estate transaction than it is about sovereignty, recognition of treaties," and ultimately the end of the U.S. real-estate concept of land. | `[verified]` (quoted as the author's reading, not a movement consensus) |
| Wikipedia, "Land back" | A decentralised campaign that emerged in the late 2010s among Indigenous peoples in Australia, Canada and the U.S.; seeks restored Indigenous sovereignty with political and economic control of ancestral lands; also notes tensions (market purchases inside the colonial property system, co-management as a fallback). | `[verified]` (secondary; use only for orientation) |

**Plain-language gloss for the site:** Land Back ranges from a tribe buying back one parcel, to a city deeding back an island, to a demand that the United States honor the treaty it signed and give up land it never lawfully acquired. The common thread is Indigenous authority over land, not only ownership of a deed.

### Origins, with the dates we could confirm

- **Hashtag lineage.** The HCN explainer says the term appeared as a hashtag in the early 2010s tied to land theft in South Africa and spread to North America in mid-2019 during a Tiny House Warriors pipeline blockade in Secwepemc territory. `[verified]` (single secondary source; treat the South Africa lineage as `[disputed]` until a second source is found).
- **Yellowhead Red Paper, October 2019.** `[verified]`.
- **Mount Rushmore, July 2020.** NDN Collective activists protested on a highway leading to Mount Rushmore before a presidential campaign event; the Land Back Manifesto came out of that action (Wikipedia). `[verified]` (secondary). HCN says NDN Collective launched landback.org with the manifesto on Indigenous Peoples Day, August 2020 `[verified]` (the HCN article is the only source read for that date; the manifesto page itself gives no launch date).
- **Earlier precedents the movement points to.** HCN names the return of Blue Lake to Taos Pueblo as "one of the first modern LandBack victories" and cites the 1969 Alcatraz occupation as a demand for return of federal land `[verified]`. Blue Lake details are in F3.
- **Precedent from the Black Hills claim.** *United States v. Sioux Nation of Indians*, 448 U.S. 371 (1980) (decided June 30, 1980) held that the Act of Feb. 28, 1877 effected a taking of the Black Hills requiring just compensation; the Sioux have declined the money because the land was never for sale (AP report, April 2024) `[verified]` (Cornell LII for the holding; the amount is discussed in F3).

The brief's rule applies: the movement's genealogy is contested and partly oral; the site should say "described as" rather than assert a single origin.

## 2. A working taxonomy of how land actually comes back

This is the structure the knowledge graph should use for "mechanism".

| Mechanism | What happens | Examples we verified (details in F3) | Who benefits / who holds title |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Congressional restoration** | Congress passes a statute returning federal land to a tribe or restoring a terminated tribe. | Blue Lake (1970); Menominee restoration (1973 law, 1975 land); National Bison Range to CSKT (Dec. 2020 law) | Tribe or United States in trust for tribe |
| **B. Federal administrative transfer** | Executive agencies transfer or place land in trust. | Bison Range trust transfer (2021); Oneida fee-to-trust (2023 BIA decision for about 270 acres) | United States in trust |
| **C. Government-to-tribe gift or sale (state, county, city)** | A non-federal government deeds land back or funds a purchase. | Eureka to Wiyot, Tuluwat Island, about 200 acres, 2019; California Tribal Nature-Based Solutions funding (Tule River, Washoe, Mechoopda, Esselen); Caltrans Blues Beach to Kai Poma (approved June 2026, pending deed) | Tribe or tribal nonprofit; often fee title |
| **D. Conservation-intermediary "buy and transfer"** | A conservation nonprofit buys land (often with state or philanthropic money) and conveys it to a tribe. | Western Rivers Conservancy, Yurok Blue Creek and Esselen Adler Ranch; The Conservation Fund, Mi'kmaq parcel; Tributary Land Returns (Maine); Pacific Forest Trust, Southern Sierra Miwuk | Tribe; the intermediary is an ally, not Indigenous-led |
| **E. Indigenous-led land trust or conservancy** | A Native-led nonprofit holds land for Indigenous stewardship. | Sogorea Te' Land Trust; Native Land Conservancy (MA); Amah Mutsun Land Trust; Esselen Tribe of Monterey County (tribal 501(c)(3)); Cheyenne River Youth Project's Bear Butte parcel | The nonprofit holds title; this differs from a tribal government holding title |
| **F. Tribal purchase on the open market** | The tribe buys back parcels. | Wiyot (2000, 1.5 acres); Stockbridge-Munsee, Monument Mountain (372 acres, 2025); Menominee buybacks; Oneida reacquisition | Tribe; often must still be placed into trust to restore sovereignty (*City of Sherrill*, 2005) |
| **G. Private gift or stewardship agreement** | A private landowner or foundation returns land or control. | Elliotsville Foundation to Penobscot Nation (735 acres, Oct. 2020; described as a stewardship agreement, title mechanism unclear); Nebraska writer's donation to Pawnee (unnamed, KPBS film) | Varies; check the instrument |
| **H. Fractional-interest consolidation** | Individual owners sell tiny shares of allotments; the program restores them to tribal trust. | Land Buy-Back Program for Tribal Nations (2012-2022): nearly 3 million acres | Tribal trust; this consolidates ownership but is not recovery of ceded land |
| **I. Reparative payment or rent (not land transfer)** | Non-Indigenous residents pay a voluntary tax or rent to Indigenous organizations. | Sogorea Te' Shuumi Land Tax; Real Rent Duwamish | The Indigenous organization |
| **J. Co-management or access** | The land stays with the government; the tribe gets a management role or access. | Not a return; HCN and Wikipedia note co-management as a fallback | Government retains title |

**Distinct legal contexts the site must label:**

- **Hawaii.** The history is the 1893 overthrow of the Hawaiian Kingdom (an independent state that the United States had recognized) rather than the U.S.-tribal treaty-and-reservation system; Native Hawaiians are not a federally recognized tribe, and the key land institutions are state programs. Treaty relations between the United States and the Kingdom are outside this file's evidence and are `[from-memory]` only. DHHL administers about 203,981 acres of Hawaiian Home Lands under the 1920 Act, with 47,086 lease applications from 29,451 beneficiaries `[verified]` (dhhl.hawaii.gov). OHA is a semi-autonomous *state* agency `[verified]`. Wikipedia: Public Law 103-150 (1993) is the Apology Resolution; the Akaka bill failed; a 2016 DOI process was cut short; a 2022 DOI consultation policy followed `[verified]` (secondary). Hawaii organizations in F2 are labeled "distinct legal context" and are not mixed with tribal-government rows.
- **Alaska.** The Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act (Pub. L. 92-203, signed Dec. 18, 1971) conveyed "up to 44 million acres" and about $962.5 million through 12 land-owning for-profit regional corporations (plus a 13th non-land-owning one) and more than 200 village corporations `[verified]` (Wikipedia). It is not a treaty and not a tribal-government land return; some Alaska Natives criticize it as "cultural genocide" or as an illegitimate settlement (same source). HCN (Feb. 2026) reports nearly 28,000 acres to NANA Regional Corporation and says over 700,000 acres have returned to Alaska Natives since 1971 `[verified]` (single secondary source; the 700,000 figure is unexplained, treat as `[unverified]`).
- **Non-federally-recognized nations.** The Duwamish Tribe states it is "not federally recognized and do not have a reservation" (Buffalo's Fire, Aug. 6, 2026; Real Rent Duwamish site) `[verified]`. The Esselen Tribe of Monterey County, the Amah Mutsun Tribal Band, and the Mutsun Costanoan band at Indian Canyon are widely reported as not federally recognized `[from-memory, not checked]`. Land return and donation routes work differently for these nations because land is often held by a tribal nonprofit rather than in trust.

## 4. Landscape: who is doing the work

The directory in F2 holds the details. Strategically, the landscape sorts into six layers:

1. **Pan-Indigenous campaigns and capacity builders.** NDN Collective (LANDBACK Campaign), Indian Land Tenure Foundation, First Nations Development Institute, Native American Rights Fund. They do not usually hold land themselves. ILTF has done land-specific work (Pe' Sla, 2012, with its affiliate Indian Land Capital Company providing $900,000 in fast financing).
2. **Indigenous-led land trusts and conservancies.** Sogorea Te' Land Trust, Native Land Conservancy (MA), Amah Mutsun Land Trust, Esselen Tribe of Monterey County (tribe as 501(c)(3)). They hold or steward land directly.
3. **Tribal governments.** The actual recipients of most large returns (Yurok, Tule River, Washoe, CSKT, Mi'kmaq, Menominee, Stockbridge-Munsee, Taos Pueblo and others). Tribal governments rarely run public fundraising; see F4 for how to route support respectfully.
4. **Allied conservation intermediaries.** Western Rivers Conservancy, The Conservation Fund, Trust for Public Land, The Nature Conservancy, Pacific Forest Trust, McKenzie River Trust, Save the Redwoods League, First Light (Maine). They broker deals and raise money, and several describe their work as supporting tribes, but they are not Indigenous-led. The Yurok Blue Creek project depended on this layer for 23 years.
5. **Place-based ally groups.** Right Relationship Boulder's Land Group (Arapaho and Cheyenne, Boulder Valley, acting at tribes' request), Shared Spaces Foundation (Duwamish).
6. **Government funders and programs.** California Natural Resources Agency (TNBS), the Wildlife Conservation Board, DOI Buy-Back, Hawaii DHHL.

### Patterns worth telling honestly on the site

- **Money mostly flows through public programs and philanthropy, not small donors.** Blue Creek cost a reported $56 million, with only $8 million from direct public grants; the rest was private foundations, philanthropy, carbon credit sales and federal New Markets Tax Credits `[verified]` (Native News Online). A $50 gift to a conservancy is not a "$50 land return."
- **California is the 2025-26 center of gravity.** Yurok (47,097 acres, final phase May 30, 2025), Tule River (17,030 acres, Oct. 29, 2025), Washoe (10,274 acres, Feb. 2026), Southern Sierra Miwuk (about 900 acres, Dec. 2025), Mechoopda (450 acres, 2026), Blues Beach (136 acres, approval June 26, 2026), all `[verified]` in F3 sources.
- **Maine's Wabanaki Tributary Land Returns** is the largest coordinated multi-parcel campaign: 11 parcels, 50,000+ acres, $50 million target, six complete, "about halfway" in July 2026 `[verified]` (Bangor Daily News, Maine Public).
- **Federal reversals and blockages are real.** Fort Reno (about 6,700 acres) remains USDA-held and a House-committee-advanced farm bill (H.R. 7567) would permanently bar disposal `[verified]` (NonDoc, Mar. 20, 2026). Headlines for 2026 also report a presidential veto stalling Miccosukee land expansion and a Utah trust-land transfer effort that undercut the Ute Indian Tribe's hopes `[unverified]` (headlines only; articles not read).
- **Transactions inside the property system are contested within the movement.** Some commentators note purchases accept the colonial real-estate framework even as the movement rejects it (Wikipedia, "Land back") `[verified]` (secondary). The site should present Land Back as both a legal-restoration project and a sovereignty project, not only a real-estate one.
- **Tribal-led versus nonprofit-led matters.** A tribe holding land in trust or fee has jurisdiction; an Indigenous-led nonprofit (Sogorea Te', Native Land Conservancy, Kai Poma) holds land under state property law and tax rules, and an ally nonprofit holds it until transfer. The graph should model "holder of title" separately from "organization that works on it."

## 5. Recommendations for the site's use of this material

1. Model returns with four fields: `mechanism` (A-J above), `title_holder_type` (tribal government, tribal nonprofit, ally nonprofit, federal trust), `recipient_nation`, and `status` (completed, pending deed, blocked, headline-only).
2. Show an explicit "legal context" badge for Hawaii and Alaska.
3. Display acreage with its source and range (for example Monument Mountain: 372 acres per Tribal Business News, 351 per Berkshire Edge) rather than silently choosing.
4. Use each nation's own name where known (WélmeltiɁ Preserve, Waší·šiw Land Trust, Tuluwat, Peeskawso Peak, Pe' Sla) and flag all placenames for tribal review before publication.
5. Treat 2025-26 items as time-sensitive; show an "as of" date.
6. Do not publish locations of sacred sites beyond what the tribe itself publicizes. Bear Butte, Blue Lake and Pe' Sla are widely publicized by their stewards, but the Cheyenne River parcel's exact location and any Oak Flat-type site should be handled with tribal consent (brief principle 5).
