# G4. Pedagogy and Narrative Arcs

**Research agent:** G. **Companions:** G1 (prior art), G2 (methodology), G3 (ethics and governance).

**Purpose.** A map of cessions and violations teaches something whether or not the designers intend it. If it only shows land turning red, it teaches that Native nations are a thing of the past. This file sets out pedagogical principles drawn from sources read this session, design patterns for the time-slider and guided tours, and a sketch of narrative arcs, each marked with which beats are verified and which still need research or tribal review.

**Tags:** `[verified]` = confirmed in a source read this session; `[from-memory]`; `[disputed]`; `[unverified]`. Items I propose are marked **Recommendation**.

---

## 1. Evidence base for the pedagogy

### 1.1 What goes wrong in mainstream teaching
- **Pre-1900 framing.** A study of K-12 U.S. history standards (Shear, Knowles, Soden, and Castro, "Manifesting Destiny: Re/presentations of Indigenous Peoples in K-12 U.S. History Standards," *Theory and Research in Social Education* 43(1): 68-101, 2015) found the standards "overwhelmingly present Indigenous Peoples in a pre-1900 context," placing Indigenous peoples mainly in the distant past and downplaying their presence in later U.S. history. `[verified]` (Penn State research portal abstract; the abstract I read gives no percentages, so I do not cite any.)
- **Past-tense-only writing** reinforces the "Vanishing Indian" myth; use present tense and contemporary examples, and add dates or context when past tense is needed. `[verified]` (NMAI/Arizona State Museum "Impact of Words")
- **Monolithic "Native Americans."** Avoid "all Native Americans"; use "most" or name the nations. `[verified]` (same)
- **Symbolic acknowledgment without action.** USDAC calls acknowledgment alone "a small gesture" that matters only with authentic relationships and informed action. `[verified]` (secondary page)
- **Settler "moves to innocence."** Tuck and Yang (2012, *Decolonization: Indigeneity, Education & Society* 1(1): 1-40) argue that settler colonialism is a structure, not a past event; that decolonization requires "the repatriation of land"; that treating critical awareness ("conscientization") as the end point lets people avoid giving up land, power, or privilege; and that solidarity should be "uneasy" rather than reconciled. `[verified]` (paper PDF). Implication: a site that makes users feel informed but moves them nowhere has failed its own purpose.

### 1.2 Positive frameworks to build on
- **NMAI Native Knowledge 360 Essential Understandings (ten)** `[verified]`:
  1. Cultures develop through shared language, place, and community, with distinct cultures from ancient times to today.
  2. Time, continuity, and change: Indigenous peoples shaped the hemisphere for thousands of years; understanding America's history requires Indian perspectives.
  3. People, places, and environments: long-standing study, management, and living in homelands, continuing today.
  4. Individual development and identity: disrupted by contact; many communities now revive traditions.
  5. Individuals, groups, and institutions: Native peoples have always organized their own institutions.
  6. Power, authority, and governance: complex governments before colonization; today tribes "remain sovereign and maintain government-to-government relationships with the United States."
  7. Production, distribution, and consumption: extensive trade networks; today's enterprises.
  8. Science, technology, and society: Native knowledge helps address contemporary challenges.
  9. Global connections.
  10. Civic ideals and practices: tribal, U.S., and state citizenship can overlap, and not all Native people accept U.S. citizenship.
  The NMAI says it created these with Native communities, education agencies, educators, and organizations, and offers a framework PDF and a classroom-applications guide. `[verified]`
- **Place-based, user-paced, primary-source learning with Elders' voices:** the Treaty Relations Commission of Manitoba's StoryMap is designed for user-driven navigation, primary sources and timeline, and place-based learning ("locate themselves on the land"), with Elders on video, progress reports to an Elders Council, and text reviewed by an education advisor. `[verified]` (Esri Canada case study)
- **Primary-source inquiry:** a National Archives DocsTeach lesson on the Cherokee petition against the Treaty of New Echota has students analyze the document without being told what it is, predict whether it succeeded, then read the background and discuss; the petition (sent by the National Cherokee Council; 3,352 signers) failed, and the Senate ratified the treaty by one vote in May 1836. `[verified]` (DocsTeach)
- **Inquiry Design Model** is the one framework NK360's home page names (for an Inka Empire lesson). `[verified]`
- **Community-authored story mapping** (Mapping Indigenous LA) shows Indigenous presence in a contemporary city, and states a goal of informing the public that Indigenous people are present "today, not only in its distant past." `[verified]` (see the project's status note in G1 s.7 for governance lessons)
- **Indigenous language and naming in cartography** (Decolonial Atlas; Native Land Digital). `[verified]`
- **Style guidance:** Younging's *Elements of Indigenous Style* (2nd ed., 2025) for editorial standards. `[verified]` (listing only)

## 2. Design principles for the Atlas (Recommendations drawn from the above)

1. **Present-first, past-second.** Every historical view carries a persistent "Today" element: the nation's current name (endonym), official website link, and a one-line statement of its current land base or programs, reviewed per G3. No historical state appears alone (G2 L6).
2. **Four braided story strands, not one.** Every nation or treaty story displays, side by side: **(1) Instruments and promises**, **(2) Loss and violation (with evidence)**, **(3) Resistance, law, and persistence**, **(4) Returns, restoration, and continuity**. If a story has only strand 2, it is not ready to publish.
3. **Centering nations, not settlers.** Narratives start from the nation's perspective and name (G3 s.6), and avoid making settler guilt the emotional center. Guilt is not a call to action; specific support pathways are (G3 s.8).
4. **Treaties as living law.** Stress that treaties remain operative, with verified examples: *McGirt* (2020) and the visibility of five Oklahoma nations' reservation boundaries on Google Maps (Sept. 2020); *Herrera v. Wyoming* (2019); the fishing-rights cases. `[verified]` (see G2 s.7)
5. **Distinguish what is certain from what is modeled** (G2 s.8). Teach uncertainty as part of the history: "the boundaries were written in language that surveyors could not always locate."
6. **Diversity of nations:** avoid a pan-Indian voice. Give each nation's story its own shape; resist template sameness.
7. **Agency language.** Use verbs of agency for nations (negotiated, refused, petitioned, sued, purchased, restored) and precise verbs for the U.S. (ratified, enacted, ordered), not passive "were removed."
8. **Don't make users pick a side on contested inter-nation matters** (G2 s.5).
9. **Let learners control pacing**; allow reflective pauses; provide content warnings (G3 s.10).
10. **Honest about gaps:** "This page has not yet been reviewed by [Nation]" is part of the lesson about who gets to tell the story.

## 3. The time-slider: designing against the "vanishing" effect

### 3.1 Multi-track timeline (Recommendation)
Replace a single "land lost" curve with four parallel tracks on the same time axis:

| Track | Content | Source notes |
|-------|---------|--------------|
| **Instruments** | Treaties, agreements, statutes, executive orders (ratified/unratified) | Kappler, Stat., USFS lineage (G1) |
| **Alterations and violations** | Cessions, diminishments, takings, allotment openings with evidence category (G2 s.4) | Cited records |
| **Law and resistance** | Court cases, petitions, protests, treaty councils, tribal legal actions | e.g. the 1836 Cherokee petition `[verified]`; *Worcester*, *Sioux Nation*, *Herrera* |
| **Returns and restoration** | Land returns, restoration acts, boundary confirmations, co-management | e.g. Blue Lake 1970; Pe' Sla; Yurok 2025; *McGirt* 2020 `[verified]` |

A second axis (a small line chart under the map) shows **cited acreage in each status** (held / altered / returned) with an explicit "range of estimates" band, since acreage figures vary by source (brief principle 7). The Land-Grab Universities dataset shows how to compute acreage and dollars per transfer from parcel-level lineage `[verified]`; for the Atlas, publish ranges and sources, not a single figure.

### 3.2 Interaction design (Recommendation)
- **Entry points:** (1) a nation (endonym-first list), (2) a place (county-level, no address), (3) a theme (treaty, river, court case), (4) a guided tour.
- **Play mode** is optional and pausable (WCAG 2.2.2 `[verified]`, G3 s.11); it ends on the "Today" frame, not on the frame of maximum loss. The last frame of any time-lapse is the present, with returns and nation names visible.
- **Stop-the-clock compare:** a before/after handle between "as signed" and "what happened" within a chosen cut-point year (G2 s.2(b)).
- **Click-through to documents:** each alteration links to the treaty text (Kappler/NARA/IDA), statute, or case; learners can read the words (the DocsTeach approach `[verified]`).
- **Uncertainty toggle** (G2 s.8.4).
- **Teacher mode:** vocabulary pre-teach, discussion prompts, printable primary-source packs. Align to standards after verifying the relevant state standards `[unverified]`.
- **Share cards:** exported images embed lineage, uncertainty legend, and a "not a legal boundary" line (G2 s.10).

### 3.3 Wording of key UI strings (Recommendation)
- "Instrument signed" not "land lost."
- "Altered by [Act of ___]" not "stolen" in labels; the word "dispossession" is appropriate in analysis where scholars use it (Saunt: dispossession made the U.S. a transcontinental power `[verified]`), with attribution.
- "Returned" and "restored" labels as first-class states.
- "Nation today: [endonym]" in every popup.

## 4. Guided tours (narrative arcs)

Each tour is a short sequence of **beats**, with: question, claim, evidence, map state, "today" connection, and call-to-learn/act. Format guidance: 6-10 beats, 8-15 minutes, ends in the present, includes at least one resistance beat and one continuity or return beat. For each tour below, beats marked `[verified]` were confirmed in sources read this session; others are research tasks.

**Authoring checklist for every tour**
1. Sources for every beat, with pin-cites.
2. Present-day connection and the nation's own links.
3. Tribal review status shown (G3 s.3.3).
4. Content warnings as needed (G3 s.10).
5. Accessibility pass (text equivalent of every map beat).
6. A "What we don't know" beat or note.
7. A call to action reviewed per G3 s.8.

### Tour A. Follow one treaty: Fort Laramie (1851 and 1868) and the Black Hills

**Question:** What happens to a promise over time?

| Beat | Content | Status |
|------|---------|--------|
| 1 | **1851 Treaty of Fort Laramie:** first major effort to define the territory of the Great Sioux Nation and neighboring tribes; it did not create a reservation; it set boundaries within which tribes could live and hunt; written in English with too few interpreters; government representative decision-making clashed with Plains consensus practice; both sides broke its terms | `[verified]` (ND Studies page) |
| 2 | **Overlap and recognition:** Court of Claims in *Crow Tribe v. United States* (1960) held the 1851 treaty recognized the Crow's title; a dissent argued several tribes roamed the same area | `[verified]` (opinion summary) |
| 3 | **1868 Treaty of Fort Laramie:** established the Great Sioux Reservation including the Black Hills; unceded lands in Wyoming, Nebraska, and Dakota Territory reserved for hunting; Bozeman Trail to be closed; required approval of three-fourths of adult males; Sitting Bull's Hunkpapas did not sign | `[verified]` (ND Studies page) |
| 4 | **1874 gold; refusal to sell; conflict** | `[verified]` (ND Studies: gold discovered in 1874, Sioux refused to sell) |
| 5 | **Act of Feb. 28, 1877** takes the Black Hills | `[verified]` (*Sioux Nation*) |
| 6 | **1980: *United States v. Sioux Nation of Indians*, 448 U.S. 371 (June 30, 1980):** taking requiring compensation, $17.1 million principal plus 5% interest from 1877 | `[verified]` |
| 7 | **Refusal of payment:** nine tribes of the Great Sioux Nation turned down the payment (reported at $1.3 billion in a 2011 PBS NewsHour piece) because the land was never for sale; leaders sought unused federal land and co-management, excluding Mount Rushmore, Ellsworth AFB, and private property | `[verified]` (PBS) |
| 8 | **Pe' Sla:** tribes raised $9 million to purchase Pe' Sla, part of the Great Sioux Nation's creation story; the Indian Land Tenure Foundation was among the leading groups; the year is not stated in the article read | `[verified]` for the purchase; year `[from-memory]` 2012 |
| 9 | **Today:** link to Great Sioux Nation tribal governments (to be identified with the nations) and to Indigenous-led organizations (organization researchers) | to research |

**Why this tour:** it connects treaty text, overlap, unilateral action, a Supreme Court taking, a refusal that is itself an act of sovereignty, and a land return. **Risks:** the Black Hills are sacred (G3 s.4); coordinate with the Oceti Sakowin nations before publishing; avoid precise locations.

### Tour B. Follow one river: the Klamath (pilot proposal)

**Question:** What does it look like when a river, a nation, and a treaty-era promise come together in the present?

| Beat | Content | Status |

| 1 | The Yurok Tribe's relationship to the Klamath (nation-authored text needed) | to research with the Yurok Tribe |
| 2 | 19th-century federal treaty negotiations in California and their fate; early reservations | `[unverified]`; needs primary-source research (I recall that treaties negotiated in California in 1851-52 were not ratified; `[from-memory]`) |
| 3 | Industrial logging and dams | to research |
| 4 | Klamath dam removals (referenced as "just months" before a 2025 land transfer) | `[verified]` (Grist, reference only); details to research |
| 5 | **Blue Creek / Klamath land return:** the Yurok Tribe's 47,097-acre transfer, completed in 2025; Western Rivers Conservancy bought land in pieces from Green Diamond over about two decades; total cost $56 million from private funding, tax credits, carbon credit sales, loans, federal programs, and settlement funds, partly funded by California; designated a salmon sanctuary and community forest; trust placement planned | `[verified]` (Grist, June 5, 2025) |
| 6 | **Today:** the Yurok Tribe's own channels | to research |

**Alternative rivers** `[from-memory]`, all needing research: the Columbia and Celilo Falls with the 1855 treaties; the Missouri River and Pick-Sloan dam inundation; the Colorado River and *Winters* water rights. Pick one with a nation's partnership before building.

**Why this tour:** it shows a *success* arc led by a nation, with a documented funding model that includes public, private, and conservation partners. **Risk:** the sources I read present the partnership and ecological claims largely from the Tribe and the conservancy; the Atlas should attribute those claims accordingly `[verified]` (Grist caveat).

### Tour C. Follow one court case: from *Johnson v. M'Intosh* to *McGirt*

**Question:** How did the law decide whose rights count, and how did it change?

| Beat | Content | Status |
|------|---------|--------|
| 1 | *Johnson v. M'Intosh*, 21 U.S. (8 Wheat.) 543 (1823): discovery gave title to the discovering government; Native nations were "the rightful occupants of the soil, with a legal as well as just claim to retain possession," but only a right of occupancy | `[verified]` |
| 2 | *Worcester v. Georgia*, 31 U.S. 515 (1832) and the Cherokee context | `[from-memory]`; verify |
| 3 | *Tee-Hit-Ton Indians v. United States*, 348 U.S. 272 (1955) | `[verified]` |
| 4 | *Solem v. Bartlett*, 465 U.S. 463 (1984): only Congress can diminish a reservation | `[verified]` |
| 5 | *City of Sherrill v. Oneida Indian Nation*, 544 U.S. 197 (2005): equitable bars; trust acquisition as the path | `[verified]` |
| 6 | ***McGirt v. Oklahoma*** (July 9, 2020): Creek reservation remains Indian country for Major Crimes Act purposes; Google Maps adds boundaries for five nations (Sept. 2020); Cherokee Principal Chief Hoskin comments | `[verified]` |
| 7 | *Herrera v. Wyoming* (May 20, 2019) as a treaty-right victory | `[verified]` |
| 8 | **Today:** what these rulings changed and did not change (legal reviewer) | needs legal review |

**Why this tour:** it teaches the legal categories (G2 s.1) through cases. **Risk:** do not present doctrine as destiny; include both setbacks and wins.

### Tour D. Follow one nation across 250 years: the Cherokee Nation (pilot proposal)

**Question:** What does continuity look like across removal, law, and rebuilding?

| Beat | Content | Status |
|------|---------|--------|
| 1 | Cherokee homeland and early treaties (1785 Hopewell onward) | `[from-memory]`; verify with Kappler |
| 2 | *Worcester v. Georgia* (1832) | `[from-memory]` |
| 3 | **Treaty of New Echota** (Dec. 29, 1835): Ross's Sept. 28, 1836 protest says the treaty was signed by Major Ridge and members "not authorized to sign on their behalf" and was not "the act of our Nation" | `[verified]` (NPS) |
| 4 | **The 1836 petition** (National Cherokee Council; 3,352 signers) and the Senate's one-vote ratification in May 1836 | `[verified]` (DocsTeach) |
| 5 | Removal; the Trail of Tears (content warning) | `[from-memory]` for details; the NPS Trail of Tears resources exist (seen in search results, not read) |
| 6 | Rebuilding in Indian Territory; allotment and statehood | to research |
| 7 | **2020:** Cherokee Nation reservation boundaries appear on Google Maps after *McGirt*; Chief Hoskin: the ruling prompted many questions and the map makes boundaries easier to see | `[verified]` (KOKH, Sept. 22-23, 2020). The article's stated area ("about 7,000 miles") has an ambiguous unit; verify before repeating. |
| 8 | **Today:** the Cherokee Nation's own sources (language, government, citizenship); consider Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians and United Keetoowah Band as separate nations with their own stories | to research with each nation |

**Why this tour:** the Cherokee story has an unusually rich documentary record (petitions, treaty text, protest letters). **Risk:** Cherokee stories are over-represented in popular teaching; use as a pilot but do not let it stand in for "the" Native experience (G4 s.2, point 6). Also, the Atlas should not rank "good" and "bad" factions of the Cherokee; present documented positions and let the nation's historians frame them.

### Tour F. Follow one right: a treaty clause that still works

**Question:** What does "in common with" mean today?
| Beat | Content | Status |
|------|---------|--------|
| 1 | Mid-1800s Washington treaties reserved the right to fish "in common with the citizens of the Territory" | `[verified]` (DOJ page) |
| 2 | *United States v. Washington* (the 1974 Boldt decision) | `[from-memory]`; the page read did not cover it |
| 3 | *Washington v. Washington State Commercial Passenger Fishing Vessel Ass'n*, 443 U.S. 658 (1979): "fair share," up to half, not more than a moderate living | `[verified]` (DOJ) |
| 4 | 1989 shellfish proceeding and $33 million settlement for tribes to acquire shellfish beds | `[verified]` (DOJ) |
| 5 | *Herrera v. Wyoming* (2019): hunting right on "unoccupied lands" survives statehood | `[verified]` |
| 6 | Tribal co-management and salmon restoration; the culverts case | to research |

**Why this tour:** treaty rights as living law without needing polygons; broad appeal; shows how tribes secured rights in court.

### Tour G. Follow one dollar and one acre: the Morrill Act

Based on Land-Grab Universities (HCN): about 10.7 million acres from nearly 250 tribes, bands, and communities; more than 160 cessions; roughly 80,000 parcels; sale proceeds funded university endowments `[verified]`. Beats: pick one land-grant university and its parcels; show the treaty or other transfer method; show the payment for Indigenous title vs. the sale proceeds; show today's institutional responses (the article reports universities were contacted and fewer than half responded `[verified]`). Needs: reuse permission/license check (G1 s.8); tribal review for each nation named; the research voice should note HCN's own caveat that parcel data may contain transcription errors `[verified]`.

### Tour H. Follow one word: "cession"

A short media-literacy tour: what the word hides and reveals; compare Royce's Schedule entries with the treaty text, the tribal account, and the evidence categories (G2 s.4); show Saunt's view that treaties were often negotiated under duress or facilitated with bribes `[verified]` and the NLD practice of keeping contradictory accounts `[verified]`.

### Tour I. Start where you are

An entry tour for the casual visitor: choose a county; see which nations are associated with the area in cited sources, which instruments apply, and what is there today (reservations, language programs, nation websites). No address; no "stolen land" verdict; ends with ways to learn and support local nations (G3 s.8-9).

### Other possible tours (not sketched)
Follow one law (e.g., allotment) `[from-memory]`; follow one family or delegation (only with descendants' consent); follow one language; follow one pipeline or dam; follow one boarding school (content warnings, G3 s.10, and nation partnership).

## 6. Evaluation (Recommendation)

- **Misconception probes** (before/after, anonymous): e.g., "Do Native nations still hold treaty rights today?"; "Which statement best describes a 'cession'?"; track change, not scores.
- **Educator pilots** with a small number of classrooms and informal educators, using NK360 materials as a comparison `[verified]` that NK360 offers educator professional development.
- **Tribal reviewer feedback** as the primary quality signal; record what changed because of it.
- **Avoid invasive analytics:** no tracking of individual users on nation pages; use aggregate, privacy-preserving measures, which the architecture agent should specify.
- **Success measures beyond clicks:** number of nation pages reviewed; number of verified recipient links followed (counted privately by recipients, not by the site); educator adoption.

---

## 7. Content and UX pitfalls to avoid (checklist)

1. A final frame that is empty or red, with no nation names. Fix: end in the present.
2. "Lost" or "vanished" language for nations or for land that is held in trust or legally intact.
3. Single-voice templates that make nations look alike.
4. Using Indigenous people as props (stock photos, headdress clip art).
5. Language that blames individuals, or that uses "Indian" uncritically as a noun.
6. Calls to action that end at "donate" with no context (G3 s.8).
7. Maps as proof: viewers will treat polygons as legal facts unless told otherwise (G2).
8. Tours that spend all beats on loss. Rule: at least one resistance beat and one continuity or return beat.
9. Unlabeled contested positions (inter-tribal overlap; internal political divisions such as New Echota).
10. Overpromising "Land Back" outcomes; keep claims tied to documented returns and legal reality (G2 s.7).

## Confidence & Gaps

**Overall: medium.** The pedagogical principles rest on a small number of verified sources (NK360 Essential Understandings, the ASM/NMAI terminology guidance, Shear et al. abstract, Tuck and Yang, the TRCM and DocsTeach designs). **Not verified**
