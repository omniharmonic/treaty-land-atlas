# PSL TREC 2024: Introduction and Executive Summary (PDF pp. 1-26)

Benjamin Life (@omniharmonic). Research-assistant draft. Source: People of the Sacred Land, Truth, Restoration and Education Commission (TREC), "The Legal and Political History of Colorado Tribes" (2024). Findings CSV: `00-intro-exec.csv` (PSL-INT-001 to 029).

Note on evidence: this part of the report is a summary. Almost every claim here is ASSERTED with no citation on the page. The evidence should be in the tribal chapters (book pp. 27-466) and the Fraud Report (p. 467). Treat this file as a map of claims to verify, not as proof.

## 1. Ten-line summary

1. TREC uses three legal tests for which nations it prioritizes: aboriginal title, congressional (reserved) title under the 1834 Trade and Intercourse Act, and treaty title (p. 8).
2. Ten federally recognized nations meet all three: Southern Ute, Ute Mountain Ute, Ute Indian Tribe of Utah, Kiowa, Comanche, Apache of Oklahoma, Eastern Shoshone, Northern Cheyenne, Northern Arapaho, Cheyenne and Arapaho of Oklahoma (p. 8). The CCIA recognizes 48 nations with ancestral lands in Colorado (p. 8); the 48 are not listed here.
3. ICC: land was central to every claim, yet "not a single acre was returned" (p. 9). Awards carried no compounding interest (p. 21).
4. Fort Wise Treaty of 1861: the signatures it required were never obtained, so TREC calls the transfers illegal (p. 20). It asks for land in Article 6 to be restored to the Northern Cheyenne or Northern Arapaho (p. 23).
5. Denver and other Front Range cities: TREC says they lack valid title (p. 20). The 1864 Act of Congress meant to fix Denver titles is "tainted" by "suspected illegal actions" in the amendment (p. 21).
6. Settlement before cession broke the 1834 Act, which barred land deals without a treaty. TREC says compensation was "rarely if ever paid" (p. 20).
7. Railroad Act of 1862 grants included land "not legally ceded" (p. 21). CSU's Morrill Act lands came from "illegal or coerced land dispossession" and CSU still holds about 19,000 acres (pp. 14, 21, 23).
8. The State Land Board ($4.1B endowment) has never publicly accounted for land that came from Native cessions. TREC calls this "Theft in Perpetuity" (p. 21).
9. Violence and duress: Governor Evans's 1864 proclamations told citizens to kill Indians and take their property (p. 20). Sand Creek compensation went largely unpaid (p. 20). An 1865 census found more than 100 Native children sold into slavery in Costilla and Conejos counties, and TREC ties this to Indian Agent Lafayette Head (pp. 20, 23).
10. Retained rights the cession map hides: hunting and fishing rights never revoked for at least ten nations (p. 22), and water and subsurface mineral rights TREC says were never ceded (p. 21). Both are contested legal theories.

## 2. What the atlas likely gets wrong or flattens

- **"Ceded" as a single state.** TREC separates aboriginal, congressional and treaty title (p. 8). A Royce polygon colored "ceded" erases the congressional-title layer, the land the 1834 Act protected as Indian Country until title was extinguished. It also hides the period when settlers occupied land illegally before any cession.
- **Fort Wise (1861).** Royce shows it as a completed cession. TREC says the signatures the treaty required were never obtained (p. 20). It should be shown as contested or incomplete, not ceded. The Royce area number is not stated in the source. I think it is Royce 477, but this needs checking.
- **Denver metro.** The atlas probably shows it as ordinary ceded land. TREC claims a title defect, and says the curative 1864 Act may be fraudulent (pp. 20-21).
- **ICC as closure.** If the atlas treats ICC dockets as settlements, it repeats the federal framing. TREC's framing is money instead of land, at no interest, under an unfair process (pp. 9, 21, 25).
- **Dispossession as past tense.** State trust lands, CSU Morrill parcels and railroad grants still earn revenue from land TREC calls illegally taken (pp. 14, 21-22). The atlas could show these present-day holdings.
- **Retained rights.** Hunting and fishing reservations (p. 22), and possibly water and minerals (p. 21), survive cession. A "ceded" fill implies nothing was kept.
- **Who counts.** Showing only treaty signatories leaves out most of the 48 CCIA-recognized nations (p. 8). It also suggests the removed nations are historical, while TREC stresses that tens of thousands of their citizens live in Colorado now (p. 22).
- **Royce itself.** TREC also relies on Royce maps (pp. 8-9). The fix is to label them honestly, for example "recorded federal cessions", not to replace them.

## Recommendations (pp. 23-26, close paraphrase)

**Restoration (p. 23)**
- Congress should rescind the Indian Appropriations Act rider that denies Tribal sovereignty, and restore the right of Native Nations to negotiate treaties.
- Congress should legislate so that nations removed immorally or illegally can return to and reoccupy any part of their traditional homelands in Colorado.
- Congress and the US railroads should return all unused railroad land to its original owners unless there is proof of compensation.
- Congress and Colorado should resolve the illegal land transfer under Fort Wise 1861 Article 6 by restoring land unlawfully taken from the Northern Cheyenne or Northern Arapaho.
- CSU should return the remaining 19,000 acres of its original land grant to the nations it was illegally taken from.
- Colorado and its Department of Natural Resources should restore treaty hunting and fishing rights within the state.
- Colorado should recognize Indian citizenship under the 1924 Indian Citizenship Act, whatever the person's location.

**Reparations (pp. 23-25)**
- Congress should compensate nations that held congressional title, for invasion in violation of the 1834 Trade and Intercourse Act.
- Congress should provide restitution to Sand Creek descendants.
- Congress should hold an inquiry and compensate descendants of children abducted and enslaved by Indian Agent Lafayette Head.
- Congress should compensate nations that depended on the buffalo.
- Congress should compensate for lives lost in the invasion of homelands, and for homes and property destroyed or stolen by the US military.
- Congress should require every Morrill Act 1862 land-grant institution to give descendants of affected nations free tuition and room and board.
- Congress should compensate for minerals on ceded lands that treaties did not specifically release.
- Colorado should compensate descendants and nations for the harm from Governor John Evans's two proclamations.
- Colorado should compensate the Northern Cheyenne and Northern Arapaho for Denver's and surrounding cities' occupation without legal title.
- Colorado should require an appropriate fee on all future real estate deals, described as "not a tax", to address the lasting effects of displacement and genocide. See also p. 12.
- Colorado and CSU should set up an independent Commission to manage the Morrill grant assets for Native students, especially those from the tribes whose land went to CSU.
- CSU should provide room, board and tuition at all levels to all American Indian students, especially Ute and other displaced nations.
- CSU should create a veterinary program admitting at least 10 American Indian students in each yearly cohort.
- The Colorado School Fund and Land Board should identify land illegally taken from nations, and budget special funds for Native students in public schools (p. 25).

**Reconciliation (pp. 25-26)**
- Congress and the Department of the Interior should calculate the water rights taken without permission in treaties ceding Colorado land.
- Congress should correct the construction, discriminatory processes and implementation of the ICC.
- Congress and the USFS should work with nations to protect sacred sites. About 200 are unprotected (p. 21).
- Colorado should conduct a full public audit of the Public School Fund and the State Land Board.
- Colorado should require accurate history, including genocide, forced removal and dispossession, at all levels of public schooling and public higher education.
- CDE should require the same history in teacher training and for licensure of out-of-state teachers.
- Colorado should create, with the Native community, an orientation document for state legislators.
- Colorado and Denver should build an American Indian Cultural History Center with offices for every nation that ceded land in Colorado.
- The state and federal governments should set up a restorative justice center with the Native American Rights Fund.
- The state boarding school inquiry should include more American Indian scholars.
- Colorado should hold individuals and entities fully accountable for abuse at boarding and industrial schools.
