## 1. Summary (10 lines)

1. **Little Arkansas 1865 consent was incomplete.** Some Comanche bands were absent (p.31). Article 6 made absent bands bound once they "participated in the beneficial provisions", meaning once they accepted goods. The author says "Many of the two Nations' leaders did not sign" (p.54).
2. **Little Arkansas 1865, Art. 2, promised a reservation of more than 39 million acres "for the absolute and undisturbed use and occupation" of the two nations.** It was cut back to a fraction two years later at Medicine Lodge (pp.51-52).
3. **The 1865 relinquishment clause is what took the Kiowa and Comanche claim in southeastern Colorado:** "more especially their claims and rights in and to the country north of the Cimarone River and west of the eastern boundary of New Mexico" (p.52). The author calls the whole article "suspect".
4. **Medicine Lodge 1867 duress.** The narrative says the tribes "were forcibly coerced to agree by threats of starvation and military intimidation" (p.41). This is asserted, not documented in this chunk.
5. **Medicine Lodge was predetermined.** Quoting ICC Docket 257 (Aug 9, 1971), the book shows that N.G. Taylor's July 1867 report recommended a removal territory south of Kansas before the council met. It also shows that Congress had repealed treaty-making authority in March 1867 (15 Stat. 7, sec. 6) (pp.54-56).
6. **Recorded objections at Medicine Lodge.** Ten Bear said the land offered was "too small", and Satanta said "I don't want to settle there" (pp.56-57). The author says the leaders rejected the Article 2 boundaries, yet those boundaries were inserted. A State Department note says parts of Art. 2, including "with the consent of the United States", were written in pencil (pp.58-59).
7. **Signatory gaps run through the whole series.** The Kiowa walked out of Camp Holmes 1835 without signing. Principal chief Dohasan never signed the 1837 treaty. A band chief (Setangya) signed Fort Atkinson 1853 over Dohasan's objection. Lone Wolf (Gui-pago) alone among Kiowa leaders refused Medicine Lodge. Only one Comanche band signed in 1846.
8. **Promises went undelivered.** Under Fort Atkinson Art. 7, the US promised protection and compensation; the author found no such compensation in the appropriations acts. After Medicine Lodge, rations were refused because "Congress had yet to approve the treaty", and "none of the promised rations were delivered by 1871" (pp.41-42).
9. **The reservation was filled by hostage-taking and violence.** The US seized Lone Wolf and Satanta under a white flag and threatened to execute them unless the Kiowa came to Fort Cobb (p.42). Washita 1868 killed 103 Cheyenne, and 53 women and children were taken. Carson (1864) and Mackenzie (1874) destroyed winter villages and food.
10. **Recognized territory south of the Arkansas.** Fort Atkinson 1853 calls the nations "inhabiting the said territory south of the Arkansas River". Little Arkansas Art. 3 lets them "range at pleasure" over country "they claim as originally theirs, which lies south of the Arkansas River". Both are US acknowledgments that include southeastern Colorado.

## 2. What the atlas likely gets wrong or flattens

- **Royce 478, 510, 511 (atlas crosswalk) look like neutral reservation and cession events.** In fact the 1865 reserve was a promise of "absolute and undisturbed use" with a hidden escape clause ("or such portion of the same as may hereafter from time to time be designated by the President"). The US then broke it within two years. Most of it lay in Texas, where the US did not control public land. That Texas point is my observation, not the book's; verify it.
- **The SE Colorado Kiowa/Comanche cession of 1865 is probably colored as a plain cession.** It should carry three flags: absent bands, consent deemed from accepting annuities, and occupation of the land before the cession (the Pike's Peak rush of 1858, with "settlements... built illegally", p.40).
- **Medicine Lodge (TLA-T-364/365) is framed as negotiated.** The book offers three counterpoints: the predetermined removal plan (ICC Docket 257), the recorded refusals, the pencil insertions in Art. 2, and the duress claim. Medicine Lodge should be at least "signed under contested consent".
- **Fort Atkinson 1853 (TLA-T-262) is described as a passage and forts treaty only.** It also implicitly recognized territory, held a coercive annuity-withholding clause (Art. 8), and made a protection and compensation promise (Art. 7) that went unperformed.
- **Intertribal and pre-US geography is missing.** That includes the 1840 Kiowa-Cheyenne peace that fixed the Arkansas River as a border, and Comancheria as claimed in 1836. Removal reserves in western Indian Territory overlapped Comanche hunting grounds, and the Comanche objected (p.30).

## 3. Land promised or reserved (mappable descriptions)

**A. Little Arkansas, Oct 18, 1865, Art. 2 (pp.51-52).** The treaty text runs as follows. Start at the NE corner of New Mexico. Go south to the SE corner of New Mexico. Go northeastward to a point on the main Red River opposite the mouth of the North Fork, then down the Red River to the 98th meridian. Go due north on the 98th meridian to the Cimarron River. Go up the Cimarron to where it crosses the southern boundary of Kansas. Follow that boundary west to the SW corner of Kansas, then go west to the place of beginning. The land was "set apart for the absolute and undisturbed use and occupation" of the parties and admitted friendly tribes. No white persons were allowed except government employees. The book says the area was "over 39 million acres". The grant was qualified by "or such portion of the same as may hereafter from time to time be designated by the President".

**B. Little Arkansas 1865, Art. 3 (p.52).** This was an interim right "to reside upon and range at pleasure throughout the unsettled portions of that part of the country they claim as originally theirs, which lies south of the Arkansas River". It lasted until removal and was subject to the ten-mile rule for roads and posts.

**C. Medicine Lodge, Oct 21, 1867, Art. 2 (p.59).** Start where the Washita River crosses the 98th meridian. Go up the Washita, along the middle of its main channel, to a point 30 miles by river west of Fort Cobb. Go due west to the North Fork of Red River, if that line strikes it east of the 100th meridian; if not, go west to the 100th meridian and then south to the North Fork. Go down the North Fork, along the middle of its main channel, to the main Red River. Go down the Red River to the 98th meridian, then north on the meridian to the start. The US "now solemnly agrees that no persons except those herein authorized... shall ever be permitted to pass over, settle upon, or reside in" it. Art. 3 promised added arable land if there was less than 160 tillable acres per person.

**D. Fort Atkinson, July 27, 1853 (p.46).** No boundaries are given. The nations are described as "inhabiting the said territory south of the Arkansas River". The treaty recognizes occupancy but grants no reservation.

**E. Hunting right, 1867 (pp.31, 41, 57).** This was a "last-minute concession" to hunt on former lands, in Kansas (p.31) or in Kansas and Texas (p.41). Henderson put it as "hunt up the river". The treaty article (Art. 11) is not in this chunk.

## 5. Open questions and claims needing verification

- **Fort Atkinson location conflict.** The book places it in present-day Kansas on p.31 and "near present-day Davis, Oklahoma" on p.39. The 1853 treaty site was Fort Atkinson on the Arkansas near Dodge City, Kansas, so p.39 looks wrong.
- **Medicine Lodge distance.** The narrative says "about 60 miles" south of Fort Larned (pp.31, 41). The treaty text says "seventy miles" (p.58).
- **Little Arkansas internal contradictions.** p.41 says the tribes "agreed to settle in western Kansas" and that Kansas lobbied the treaty away. That does not fit the Art. 2 boundary, and p.31 says they agreed to Indian Territory. p.41 says "all the leading chiefs eventually signed", but p.54 says many leaders did not. The Kansas lobbying claim may belong to the Cheyenne-Arapaho 1865 treaty.
- **Pencil insertions in Medicine Lodge Art. 2.** Who wrote them, and when (before or after signing)? Check the NARA original. Do not call this forgery without archival evidence.
- **ICC Docket 257.** The book cites it as a motion for summary judgment on recognized title, with "favorable findings" for the tribes, a Kuykendall dissent (pp.123-131), and an appeal. The outcome is not in this chunk. It is unclear whether the quoted Congressional Globe material comes from the majority or the dissent.
- **Annuities.** The book does not say whether the Fort Atkinson $18,000 a year (Art. 6, 10 years plus a 5-year option) or the Little Arkansas $10/$15 per-capita payments for 40 years (Art. 5, base 4,000 people) were actually paid. Check the appropriations acts and annual reports.
- **The book's claim that "Congress made it illegal for any Kiowa to leave the reservation" after 1879 (p.44)** is unsourced. It was likely an administrative order.
- **The book's statement that "None of the promised rations were delivered by 1871" (p.42)** may overstate; check agency records.
- **Events the book describes but does not name.** My identifications are the 1840 Council House Fight, the 1846 Council Springs treaty, the Nov 1864 First Battle of Adobe Walls, and the 1874 Palo Duro Canyon fight. All need confirming. The book writes "Ronald Mackenzie" for Ranald S. Mackenzie.
- **The book's Art. 6 comment that "by these presents" connotes a bribe misreads a standard legal formula.** Do not cite it as evidence of bribery. The evidence of inducement is p.38: the US "offered gifts and trade goods to persuade them to sign" in 1837.
- **Royce numbers here (478, 510, 511) come from the atlas crosswalk, not the book.** The Royce number for the 1865 relinquishment of land north of the Cimarron, which covers Colorado, should be confirmed.
- **The Fitzpatrick signer-selection claim (p.50) is an explicit assumption by analogy to Fort Laramie 1851.** Its confidence is low.
