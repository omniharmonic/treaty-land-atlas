# PSL TREC 2024, Eastern Shoshone chapter (PDF pp. 93-128): findings

Benjamin Life (@omniharmonic). Research-assistant draft. Source: People of the Sacred Land, "The Legal and Political History of Colorado Tribes" (2024). Findings CSV: `03-shoshone-a.csv` (PSL-SHO-A-001 to 027). The endnotes are not in this chunk, so citations are given as endnote numbers.

## 1. Summary (10 lines)

1. The Eastern Shoshone came uninvited to the 1851 Fort Laramie (Horse Creek) council and were ruled "not parties to the treaty" (p. 98). The 1851 boundaries and right-of-way articles never bound them.
2. The 1863 Fort Bridger treaty (Art. 4) *recognized* Shoshone country, with a southern line at the Yampa River and an eastern line through North Park, CO. It was a peace and right-of-way treaty, not a cession (pp. 103, 109).
3. The Supreme Court's account (Shoshone Indians v. US) quotes the 1862 commission instructions: the treaty was "not expected... [to] extinguish Indian title" (p. 103).
4. Surveys (Stansbury 1849-50, Pacific Railroad Surveys 1853-55), the 1861 telegraph and the stage lines crossed Shoshone land with no treaty. 1863 Art. 3 then approved the telegraph and stage lines after the fact (pp. 99-108).
5. At the Bear River Massacre (Jan 29, 1863), troops killed 224 (Connor's count) to 493 (Jasperson's count) Northwestern Shoshone. Five months later came the 1863 treaties. A Shoshone author (Darren Parry) calls the signing forced (pp. 105-107).
6. Cache Valley and Bear River valley were settled by Mormons before any treaty. The report says the land was taken "through a massacre" (p. 106).
7. The 1851 treaty assigned the Wind River country to the Crow, and the 1863 treaty assigned it to the Shoshone. The overlap was settled by the 1866 Crowheart Butte duel (p. 113).
8. The 1868 Fort Bridger treaty promised the Wind River reservation for "absolute and undisturbed use". The report says it also removed Colorado from Shoshone land (pp. 116, 124).
9. The 1872 Brunot cession admits that miners were already inside the reservation. ICC Docket 63 (1954) found the $27,500 paid for land worth $560,513.60 "unconscionable" (pp. 126-127).
10. The report asserts that Colorado land "still belongs to the Shoshone" (p. 99). This claim is not cited in the chunk.

## 2. What the atlas likely gets wrong or flattens

- **1863 Fort Bridger treated as a cession.** Its text recognizes territory and grants rights of way only. Title stayed with the Shoshone until 1868. If Royce 520 is colored "ceded" for 1863, that is wrong.
- **The Colorado portion is missing or too small.** The treaty text (Yampa River south line, North Park) and later maps (Stewart 1957, Robertson 2023 overlay of Royce 520) show more Colorado land than Doty's 1859 map, which went to the Senate.
- **1851 Fort Laramie is shown as binding on all tribes in the region.** The Shoshone were excluded, and part of their land (Wind River) was assigned to the Crow.
- **Use before consent is invisible.** The trails (1847-63), surveys (1849-55), telegraph (1861) and the Cache Valley and Bear River settlement all came before any Shoshone treaty.
- **Massacre and treaty are not linked.** Bear River (Northwestern Band) should be linked to the 1863 Fort Bridger and Box Elder treaties.
- **The 1868 reservation was "absolute and undisturbed" on paper only.** Brunot (1872) admits intrusion by miners. The Hot Springs cession (1896) was amended by Congress.

## 3. Land promised or reserved, with mappable boundaries

**1863 Fort Bridger, Art. 4 (recognized Shoshone country), p. 109:**
> On the north, by the mountains on the north side of the valley of Shoshonee or Snake River; on the east, by the Wind River mountains, Peenahpah river, the north fork of Platte or Koo-chin-agah, and the north Park or Buffalo House; and on the south, by Yampah river and the Uintah mountains. The western boundary is left undefined ... the bands now present claim that their own country is bounded on the west by Salt Lake.

Map note: "Peenahpah river" needs to be identified (possibly the Sweetwater or a Platte tributary). North Park, CO and the land north of the Yampa River in CO fall inside this boundary.

**Lander 1859 (pre-treaty Eastern Shoshone range), p. 97:** north to Wind River waters (lat. 43°30' N); east from South Pass to the North Platte headwaters; west to Bear River near the mouth of Smith's Fork; south to Brown's Hole on the Green River (CO).

**Hughes, via Faris (pre-1800 use), p. 96:** all land north of the Yampa River, North Park, and the mountains to the Front Range north of present Rocky Mountain National Park.

**1868 Fort Bridger, Art. II (Shoshone reservation), p. 116:** from the mouth of Owl Creek due south to the crest of the Sweetwater/Popo Agie divide; along that divide and the Wind River Mountains summit to the longitude of the North Fork of Wind River; due north to its mouth; up its channel 20 miles; then a straight line to the headwaters of Owl Creek; then down the middle channel of Owl Creek to the start. Promised for "absolute and undisturbed use and occupation." Bannock reservation: "reasonable portions of the 'Port neuf' and 'Kansas Prairie' countries" (later folded into Fort Hall, 1869-72).

**1872 Brunot cession (taken from the 1868 reservation), p. 126:** everything south of this line: from a point on the eastern boundary due east of the mouth of the Little Popo Agie, west to that mouth, up the Popo Agie to the North Fork, up the North Fork to the mouth of the canyon, then west to the western boundary.

**1896 Big Horn Hot Springs cession, p. 128:** from the NE corner of the reservation (Owl Creek at the Big Horn River), south 10 miles along the eastern boundary, due west 10 miles, due north to the middle of Owl Creek, then down Owl Creek to the start. Water rights are included.

## 4. Quotes worth preserving

- "These Indians (Shoshone) were not considered by the Superintendent [of Indian Affairs] as embraced by his instructions, and were consequently not parties to the treaty negotiated with the other tribes." (p. 98, quoting an official report, n. 20)
- "it was not expected that the proposed treaty would extinguish Indian title to the lands, but only secure freedom from molestation for the routes of travel" (p. 103, quoting Shoshone Indians v. US)
- "The telegraph and overland stage lines having been established and operated through a part of the Shoshonee country, it is expressly agreed that the same may be continued" (p. 108, 1863 treaty Art. 3)
- "Doty, Lincoln, Young and Connor were all instrumental and I feel conspired together to force the 1863 signing of the treaty at Fort Bridger with all of the Shoshone bands." (p. 107, Darren Parry, 2019)
- "Brigham Young took control of Shoshone land, not with a treaty but through a massacre" (p. 106)
- "previous to and since the date of said treaty, mines have been discovered, and citizens of the United States have made improvements within the limits of said reservation" (p. 126, Brunot agreement preamble)
- "the consideration of $27,500 ... for the Brunot lands worth $560,513.60 constituted an unconscionable consideration" (p. 127, ICC Docket 63)
- "Furthermore, land in present-day Colorado still belongs to the Shoshone." (p. 99)

## 5. Open questions and claims to verify

- **1863 Art. 7 (Mexican-title limit).** The source labels it an amendment. Check when the Senate added it and whether the Eastern Shoshone agreed to it. If they did not, it is a Senate amendment made without consent.
- **Colorado title.** What do the ICC dockets (Docket 63 and the other Shoshone dockets) say about the Colorado land in the 1863 country? Was it extinguished by the 1868 general relinquishment clause, by an ICC payment, or never? The report defers this to Dr. Joseph Robertson's analysis.
- **Royce 520.** Compare the atlas polygon to the Art. 4 text, the Stewart 1957 map (Marriott Library id 358831) and the Doty 1859 map (ark:/87278/s63z134h).
- **Errors in the source to correct, not copy:**
  - It says the Pacific Railway Act chose the "32nd parallel" (p. 103). The 1862 act chartered the central route.
  - It writes "Treaty of 1968" (p. 102). This should be 1868.
  - It dates Shoshone Indians v. US to "decided December 1945". The case is 324 U.S. 335, which I believe was decided March 1945.
  - It dates the Winnemucca memoir to "1863". Life Among the Piutes was published in 1883, and the episode concerns Western Shoshone in Nevada.
  - It claims Doty "wanted the Shoshone moved to Minnesota in 1841". This is unsupported.
- **Bear River is a Northwestern Band event,** although it appears in the Eastern Shoshone chapter. Keep the band attributions separate on the map.
- **Single-source or uncited claims:**
  - 40 million acres taken in 1868, and the dollar valuation.
  - A Wind River agent removed for bribery in 1910.
  - 1863 annuities not paid after 1868.
  - Wyoming violating the 1868 hunting rights.
  - The killing of "Chief Pagunapin Smithfield".
- **Article XI majority rule (1868).** Check the signature counts on the 1872 and 1896 agreements.
