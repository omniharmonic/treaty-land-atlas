# Southern Cheyenne and Southern Arapaho after 1864: findings from PSL TREC 2024, pp. 203-236

Benjamin Life (@omniharmonic). Research-assistant draft.

Source: People of the Sacred Land, Truth, Restoration & Education Report, "The Legal and Political History of Colorado Tribes" (2024), chapter "The Southern Cheyenne and Southern Arapaho Post-1864" (pp. 205-232) and "Cheyenne and Arapaho Tribes of Oklahoma TREC Recommendations" (pp. 233-236). The chapter relies heavily on Berthrong (1963, 1976), Powell (1981) and Hagan (2003). The CSV is `06-so-cheyenne-arapaho.csv` (37 rows, PSL-SCA-001 to 037).

Statute and Kappler cites marked "verify" in the CSV were added by the reader. The source does not give them.

## 1. Summary of the most important findings

1. **Little Arkansas 1865 (pp. 210-212).** About 8 of more than 280 Southern lodges were behind the signing chiefs (Powell). The chiefs said too few people were present to make a treaty. The commissioners told them it bound only their bands, and the source calls that a lie. Three days after signing, a "confederated tribes" clause was added without tribal agreement.
2. **Sand Creek reparations (p. 212).** The 1865 treaty acknowledged Sand Creek in writing and promised land acreages to survivors and to "mixed bloods" on the Fort Wise reservation. The source says "the promised reparations for Sand Creek were never paid to the individuals named in the treaty." It does not give article numbers or acreages.
3. **Senate amendment of Little Arkansas (p. 212).** Under pressure from Kansans, the Senate barred any reservation land in Kansas. Only Black Kettle and Little Robe signed the amended version (Nov 1866). The Dog Soldiers and many Southern Cheyennes never recognized it. The 1866 adhesions were obtained by Wynkoop "bribing them with supplies and threatening them with military attacks."
4. **Medicine Lodge 1867 (pp. 213-215).** It followed Hancock's 1867 village burnings. The act creating the Peace Commission provided for 4,000 volunteers to force the tribes onto reservations if peace failed. Key bands (Roman Nose, Medicine Arrows) were absent, the Arapahos were destitute, and gifts likely drove the signing. The tribes did not understand the reservation's location. The treaty reservation was never occupied.
5. **Washita 1868 (p. 217).** Hazen refused Black Kettle's peace request at Fort Cobb and told him not to come there. One week later Custer killed Black Kettle and 18-40 women and children. After that: starvation surrenders, Custer's hostage-taking of three chiefs, and Summit Springs (Colorado, 1869). Per Berthrong, Summit Springs "ended Cheyenne occupation" of the Platte-Arkansas country. That was a military result, not a treaty one.
6. **The 1869 reservation (pp. 218-219, 223).** The reservation the tribes actually lived on came from an executive order, not a treaty (about 4.29 million acres). About 760,000 acres of it were reassigned to the Wichita in 1872.
7. **Jerome Commission 1889-91 (pp. 225-227).** Coercion included threats to impose the Dawes Act, warnings that annuities would lapse, and beef rations cut in half. Hagan judges payment by the attorneys "highly likely" to have bought signatures. The tribes' attorneys took a 10 percent contract and received $67,000. Interior Secretary Noble told Jerome to "close with a majority" if three-fourths could not be had. The agent's count of 464 required signatures is not credible per Berthrong, who estimates 680 were needed. Seven Cheyenne chiefs protested and were ignored. The price was 50 cents per acre against the $1.25 the statute authorized.
8. **Outcome of allotment (pp. 227-228).** The tribes kept 529,692 of 4,294,415 acres (12 percent). About 3.5 million acres were opened in the land run of April 19, 1892.
9. **ICC (pp. 229-230).** The ICC found 51,210,000 acres owned under Fort Laramie 1851. It valued them at $23.5M as of Oct 14, 1865 (about 45 cents per acre, with an undocumented method) and offset $1,434,131.38 of treaty consideration. In 1965 the government offered to settle all Southern claims for $15M, and 338 of the 1,542 members notified voted to accept. The tribe argues the settlement fell below even the inflation-adjusted value of its half share of the 1865 valuation.
10. **The tribe's own recommendations (pp. 233-236).** They allege the Senate's removal of Fort Wise Article 11 (1861) involved "erasures, possible date changes, and outright misrepresentation of signatures." They say this affects Denver and about 20 towns founded before 1861. They call the 1851 treaty "unratified" and seek to reopen ICC Dockets 329/348 (329-A, 329-B; 16 Ind. Cl. Comm. 612).

## 2. What the atlas most likely gets wrong or flattens

- **Colorado plains shown as "ceded" by treaty.** The source says land north of the South Platte had never been ceded when homesteading began (p. 208). Effective dispossession came from military defeat (Sand Creek, Summit Springs). The ICC later treated Oct 14, 1865 (the Little Arkansas date) as the valuation and taking date.
- **Little Arkansas 1865 shown as an ordinary ratified cession.** It was signed by a small minority on a band-only understanding, altered after signing, amended by the Senate with only partial assent, and never implemented (pp. 210-212).
- **Medicine Lodge 1867 shown as a cession plus a reservation.** The treaty reservation was never occupied, overlapped the Cherokee Outlet (promised to two nations), and was given up by an Arapaho-only 1872 agreement with no Cheyennes present (pp. 221, 225).
- **The 1869 executive-order reservation conflated with treaty land.** It is a separate polygon with no treaty basis.
- **The 1891 Jerome agreement shown as a consensual cession.** It likely failed Medicine Lodge's three-fourths consent requirement and was obtained under threats, ration cuts and probable bribery.
- **ICC payment presented as compensation.** It covered land only (no loss of life or property), used 1865 valuations, deducted offsets, was settled by compromise, and no appeal was available to the tribe (p. 236).

## 3. Land promised or reserved (mappability)

- **Fort Laramie 1851 Cheyenne-Arapaho territory.** 51,210,000 acres in CO, WY, KS and NE per the ICC (p. 229). Boundaries are not described in this chunk. Use the treaty text and the ICC Docket 329 findings.
- **Little Arkansas 1865 reservation, south of the Arkansas.** Boundaries are not given in the source; consult the treaty text. It was never established. The treaty also reserved hunting in unoccupied parts of the Arkansas-Platte country (p. 210).
- **Little Arkansas survivor and mixed-blood land grants.** Promised as "specified acreages," with the mixed-blood grants on the Fort Wise reservation (p. 210). Never delivered (p. 212). No locations are given.
- **Medicine Lodge 1867 reservation.** In western Indian Territory just south of the Kansas border, in the Cherokee Outlet area, on the Salt Fork of the Arkansas (pp. 214, 218, 225). The treaty also reserved hunting "on any lands south of the Arkansas so long as the buffalo may range thereon" and barred white settlement on the 1865 reservation for three years (p. 215). Exact boundaries are in treaty Art. 2, which the source does not quote.
- **1869 executive-order reservation.** On the North Fork of the Canadian; 4,294,412 to 4,294,415 acres per Berthrong, or "about 4.3 million" (pp. 219, 224, 227). The Wichita carve-out of about 760,000 acres came in 1872.
- **After 1892.** 529,692 acres of allotments remained (3,329 allotments of 80 acres crop plus 80 acres grazing), plus 231,828 acres of school land and 32,343 acres of military land (pp. 227-228).

## 5. Open questions and claims needing verification

- **Little Arkansas Articles 5 and 6.** The source never names the articles. My understanding, to be checked against Kappler II 887-891 and 14 Stat. 703, is that Art. 5 granted 640 acres to named persons of mixed descent and Art. 6 granted 320 acres to each named Sand Creek survivor who lost a husband or parents, and 160 acres to each child. Check whether any patents issued, and whether ICC Docket 329 addressed these grants.
- **Fort Wise 1861 Article 11.** The source does not say what Article 11 provided or what evidence supports the forgery allegation. This claim is the tribe's and is contested. Compare with the NARA original and Senate executive journal.
- **Typos or errors in the source:**
  - The attorney contract is dated "May 1869 in Oklahoma City" (p. 226). It is almost certainly 1889.
  - Boulder is said to have been platted by squatters "in 1851" (p. 220). It was actually around 1858-59.
  - "Daniel Berthrong" (p. 225) should be Donald.
  - "Fort Hayes" should be Fort Hays.
  - A "Little Robe" is listed among the Sand Creek dead, yet a Little Robe signs in 1865.
- **Acreage inconsistency.** The ICC hearing figure of 4,608,878 acres taken in 1891 (p. 230) conflicts with the 4,294,415-acre reservation total (p. 227).
- **The 1872 Arapaho relinquishment** of the Medicine Lodge reservation (p. 221). Its ratification status is unclear, and the source later says the Outlet lands were never ceded before 1890 (p. 225).
- **Single-source claims.** Manypenny's suggestion that annuities were diverted (p. 217) has only one source, and Dodge's "kill every male over 12" order (p. 209) has no endnote.
- **Category of the 1851 treaty.** The tribe calls it "unratified" (p. 236), while the ICC treated it as the source of title. Reconcile this with how the atlas classifies 1851 Fort Laramie.
- **Royce areas.** No Royce numbers are given in this chunk. Map the Fort Wise, Little Arkansas, Medicine Lodge and 1891 cessions to Royce areas from the atlas data.
