## 1. Summary of the most important findings

1. **Land north of the South Platte was never ceded by the Northern Cheyenne and Arapaho in 1861** (pp. 262-263, 271). Fort Wise Article 6 needed the absent bands to join. Only one signature was ever obtained, and the source says he was a drunk non-leader. The Commissioner of Indian Affairs' own 1862 report calls the cession "imperfect and indefinite". In 1863 US Attorney S.E. Browne gave the opinion that the land north and west of the South Platte, including Denver and the gold districts, had not been ceded.
2. **The Council of 44 formally refused Fort Wise** (p. 260). Commissioner Greenwood answered: "We will make this Treaty over the Heads of the Cheyenne" (Coel p. 120). Black Kettle and White Antelope attended without Council permission (p. 261).
3. **The Fort Wise amendment assent of October 29, 1861 may have been coerced, and possibly forged** (pp. 266-268). Annuities had been held back while about 3,000 hungry people waited at the fort, and agent Boone reportedly promised 36 uniforms and a trip to Washington. On the document, the signers' "+" marks look like one person's hand, and dates have been erased and rewritten. The author says this is speculative.
4. **The Senate changed Fort Laramie 1851 without consent, and the treaty was never proclaimed** (pp. 249-252). Most nations never assented to the amendment. The Indian Office's own endorsement reads "they never got together for the purpose". Kappler printed it as accepted in 1904, then admitted the error in 1929. Royce p. 786 says it was "never ratified", and the ICC still used it as the baseline for the claim.
5. **Only Southern chiefs signed Fort Laramie 1851**: four of ten bands, with no Northern chief (p. 252). The US also told the tribes to name single "head chiefs", which overrode the Council of 44 (Art. 6).
6. **Denver's land title came from the Act of May 28, 1864** (the "Congressional Grant", 960 acres), not from a cession. The town companies had been squatters since 1858, as Smiley (1901) says, and the tribes were never paid (p. 269).
7. **Governor Evans' proclamations** of June 27 and August 11, 1864 authorized citizens to "kill and destroy" "hostile Indians" (p. 272). Evans also disobeyed Indian Office orders to hold a council and to keep the peace (pp. 271-272). The source ties these directly to Sand Creek, November 29, 1864.
8. **Fort Laramie 1868 (Northern Cheyenne and Arapaho) was signed by only four Council of 44 chiefs**, and the Council rejected it (p. 290). The reservations promised in Article 2 were never provided, and the Northern Cheyenne reservation was later made by executive order (p. 286).
9. **Oral promise for 1868.** Little Wolf was reportedly told that the land from the North Platte to the Yellowstone, and from the Big Horns to the Missouri, would belong to the Cheyenne and Arapaho (Powell, via p. 290).
10. **Massacres and mass violence in this span:** the 1856 attack on a Cheyenne camp (10 killed); Sumner 1857; the 1860 rape of women in Left Hand's camp at Denver; Sand Creek 1864; strychnine poisonings (Ware; Tall Bull family oral history); Connor's 1865 "kill every male over 12" order and the attack on Black Bear's camp; Pawnee scouts killing 24 Cheyenne; the burning of the Dull Knife village on November 25, 1876; and the 1877 removal to Darlington, where nearly 50 died.

## 2. What the atlas most likely gets wrong or flattens

- **Royce 477 (Fort Wise 1861, verify the number) is likely drawn as one clean cession.** The source and the federal record both support splitting it. Land north and west of the South Platte, including Denver, should be marked as not ceded by the Northern bands in 1861. Its title was only "perfected", if ever, by Fort Laramie 1868, and the ICC itself calls that ambiguous.
- **Fort Laramie 1851 is likely shown as a ratified treaty and as the "original" Cheyenne-Arapaho tract.** It ceded no land, the Senate amended it without consent, it was never proclaimed, and its Cheyenne signers were Southern only. The territory it recognized should be drawn as recognized or promised homeland, together with the reserved rights to hunt, fish and pass.
- **Signatory legitimacy.** Federal texts say signers were "duly authorized". Under Cheyenne law, only Council of 44 consent bound the nation. This matters for 1851, 1861 and 1868. The 1825 treaty is the one counterexample: the source says the Council approved it.
- **Cheyenne and Arapaho treated as one "confederated" nation.** The source says neither 1851 nor 1861 had their agreement to that.
- **Northern and Southern Cheyenne split by agencies.** Before 1861 the bands acted as one nation, and the Southern chiefs refused to make a separate peace.
- **Settlement timing.** Up to 100,000 gold seekers occupied the Front Range from 1858 to 1861, before any cession. That was illegal trespass under the 1834 Act and Fort Laramie 1851 Art. 3.
- **1877 Black Hills agreement.** The atlas should list the Northern Cheyenne and Arapaho as parties and flag that the Article 8 majority rule of their 1868 treaty was not met.

## 4. Quotes worth preserving

- Mitchell, 1851: "We do not come to you as traders ... We do not want your land, horses, robes, nor anything you have" (p. 246).
- Luke Lea, Commissioner of Indian Affairs, 1852, on amending treaties without consent: "anything but a mockery of the highest and most solemn form of contract" (pp. 240, 250).
- Indian Office endorsement on the 1851 amendment: "This [amendment] was to be submitted to the Indians, but they never got together for the purpose." (p. 250)
- Greenwood, 1860-61: "We will make this Treaty over the Heads of the Cheyenne." (p. 260, via Coel)
- Coel on Fort Wise Art. 6: "This crucial article put the entire treaty in abeyance; it could not take effect until all the Arapaho and Cheyenne chiefs, northern and southern, had signed." (p. 262)
- CIA Report 1862: "this treaty of the Upper Arkansas [1861] is imperfect and indefinite as to the extent of cession" (p. 263)
- Smiley, History of Denver: "All of them were merely squatters on land to which the Indians had the first claim" (p. 269)
- Evans, second proclamation, Aug. 11, 1864: "to kill and destroy as enemies of the country wherever they may be found, all such hostile Indians" (p. 272)
- Joint Committee on the Conduct of the War, 1865: Chivington "surprised and murdered, in cold blood, the unsuspecting men, women, and children on Sand Creek" (p. 281)
- Connor, 1865: "attack and kill every male Indian over 12 years of age" (p. 283)
- On 1868: "Most Northern People rejected the 1868 Treaty as a binding agreement because the Council of 44 did not approve it." (p. 290)

## 5. Open questions and claims needing verification

- **Errors in the 1851 dates.** The source says the Senate "ratified" on September 17, 1851, which was the signing date. It also says the treaty went to Fillmore on February 17, 1852, and puts the amendment on May 24 (1852). Check these against Prucha pp. 440-442.
- **1851 Cheyenne signers.** The source lists four "may have signed" names (p. 252) but also says six original Cheyenne signatures existed (p. 250).
- **Who denied signing Fort Wise.** Gary Roberts notes that White Antelope's denial comes to us second-hand, through Elbridge Gerry (p. 265). Roberts also found a document saying the chiefs approved the amendment "in full council" on October 29, 1861. That cuts against the forgery theory.
- **Fort Wise signer list.** It puts Left Hand (Namos) among the Cheyenne. Left Hand is usually Arapaho, so check the treaty text. The source also has an unresolved page-check note on Lavender, Bent's Fort (p. 371 vs. p. 346).
- **Wording of the second Evans proclamation.** The sentence about keeping "the property of the victims" may be a paraphrase printed inside the quote.
- **Agreement of 1877 chronology.** The source dates it September 1877 and argues the Cheyenne were already removed. The agreement was actually signed in Sept-Oct 1876 and ratified February 28, 1877. The removal argument needs to be redone, but the Article 8 majority argument stands on its own.
- **"February 2, 1862"** (p. 282) should be 1865.
- **Claims without a citation:** the 1834 Act being "rescinded in 1873"; the 100,000 gold seekers; the 53 million acres; Culver's $5,000 award; Boone's Huerfano ranch.
- **Royce numbers.** None are given by the source. Royce 477 for Fort Wise is my inference. Royce p. 786 has the footnote saying the 1851 treaty was "never ratified".
- **ICC Docket 329** (decided December 1961, pp. 35-36) should be read directly to see how it treated the land north of the South Platte.
- **Unrau, "Prelude to War," Colorado Magazine XLI(4) 1964, pp. 299-313** is the key secondary source on the unceded South Platte land. Get it.
