# PSL TREC 2024, Cheyenne chapter part B (PDF pp. 297-356)

Benjamin Life (@omniharmonic). Research-assistant draft.

Source: People of the Sacred Land, "The Legal and Political History of Colorado Tribes" (TREC report, 2024), PDF pages 297-356. Findings CSV: `08-cheyenne-b.csv` (PSL-CHY-B-001 to 053).

Scope note: this chunk does not cover Sand Creek, Little Arkansas, Medicine Lodge or the Washita in narrative form (those must be in part A). It holds: the end of the 1876-77 agreement text plus the author's endnotes on the 1868 and 1876-77 instruments (pp. 297-303); Part III, the 1878-79 Northern Cheyenne exodus and Fort Robinson (pp. 305-320); Part IV, Northern Cheyenne legal history 1879-2020 including the ICC case (pp. 321-351); and the Northern Cheyenne TREC recommendations to Colorado (pp. 353-356).

## 1. Summary of the most important findings

1. Fort Robinson, Jan 3-22, 1879: to force a second removal south, Capt. Wessells cut off food and fuel (Jan 4) and water (Jan 8) in subzero weather and put Wild Hog in irons. The breakout and 13-day pursuit left an Army-counted 64 Cheyenne dead; the report calls it the Fort Robinson Massacre (pp. 308-312, 337). Sourced to the Army's own 1879 Board of Officers proceedings (RG 98).
2. Army officers testified that civilians killed wounded Cheyenne and mutilated women's bodies; wounded infants died (pp. 309-310, 317).
3. The 1877 removal of the Northern Cheyenne to Indian Territory killed perhaps one-third of them through disease (p. 305; Grinnell gives 41 deaths in the first winter).
4. ICC Dockets 329/348: the 51,210,000-acre 1851 territory was valued at $23.5 million as of Oct 14, 1865 (46 cents/acre), with no interest. That date comes from the Little Arkansas treaty, which the Northern Cheyenne never signed (pp. 333-334, 338).
5. Northern Cheyenne compromise, 13 Ind. Cl. Comm. 1 (Nov 27, 1963): $5,360,886.19 minus $1,000,000 left a net $4,360,886.19. That payment also released claims for wrongful attacks, wrongful removal, buffalo destruction and accounting (pp. 339-340). Less than $4 million reached the tribe for more than 12.5 million acres, and the BIA held back most per-capita shares (pp. 335-336).
6. Fort Wise 1861: the Senate struck Article 11 and the treaty was never returned for tribal assent. An Aug 12, 1861 State Department letter itself flagged the missing consent, and Cheyenne chiefs denied signing. Congress then transferred Denver land in 1864 (pp. 338-339).
7. Bear Bow's sworn 1918 statement says the 1876 Black Hills agreement was understood as gold-mining for half the gold, not a sale. He also says only about one-sixth of the Cheyenne (friendly bands and scouts) were present, without authority from the rest (pp. 328-329).
8. The author argues the 1868 Northern Cheyenne-Arapaho treaty's relinquishment is void because the U.S. never located them on either promised reservation. He also argues the 1877 relinquishment of hunting rights lacked the adult-majority consent the treaty required (pp. 300-302).
9. The Northern Cheyenne Reservation was made by executive order (Nov 26, 1884; 371,200 acres; extended Mar 19, 1900 to 444,157 acres). The author calls this legally questionable because the Cheyenne had never ceded the northern lands (pp. 321-322).
10. Recommendation 2 asserts that the Colorado Front Range up to the Continental Divide was "legally titled" to the Northern Cheyenne and never ceded by a treaty they signed. Recommendation 10 asks Colorado to support reopening the ICC settlement (pp. 353-356).

## 2. What the atlas most likely gets wrong or flattens

- **The 1851 Cheyenne-Arapaho polygon as "ceded".** The ICC treated payment as extinguishment, at an 1865 valuation date tied to a treaty (Little Arkansas) that excluded the Northern Cheyenne. The map should label this as extinguishment by payment, not cession by the Northern Cheyenne.
- **Fort Laramie 1851 as plainly ratified.** The author says it was never ratified (citing Kappler II 594-596, the Royce footnote at 786-787, and Prucha 1994, 440-447). This is contested, since the Senate amended the annuity term and the treaty is usually treated as in force. Label it "ratification status contested / Senate-amended".
- **Fort Wise 1861 as a clean ratified cession.** The Senate struck Article 11 without tribal consent (the State Department letter documents this), and signatures were disputed.
- **The 1876-77 Black Hills agreement as binding on the Cheyenne.** Cheyenne signatures were added at Spotted Tail Agency on Sept 28, 1877, a year after the commissioners signed. Only a minority were present, the signing came after removal had begun, and the source alleges a bribery clause (houses for chiefs).
- **The Northern Cheyenne Reservation as a federal "grant".** The report frames it as the result of the 1878-79 exodus and as a remnant of land never validly ceded.
- **Massacres limited to Sand Creek and the Washita.** Sappa Creek (1875) and Fort Robinson (1879) should be added.
- **No Colorado gold-rush trespass phase.** Up to 100,000 people occupied the land illegally from 1858 to 1861, in violation of the 1834 Intercourse Act, Sec. 11. The report puts minerals taken from 1859 to 1865 at $15,307,000, before any cession.

## 3. Land promised or reserved, with mappable detail

- **1851 Fort Laramie Cheyenne-Arapaho territory.** The chunk gives only "south of the North Platte River into Nebraska, Kansas, and Colorado" (p. 328) and 51,210,000 acres (p. 333). Article 5 kept "rights or claims they may have to other lands" and hunting and passage rights (quoted p. 328). Use Kappler II 594 for the boundary.
- **1868 Northern Cheyenne-Arapaho treaty.** It offered a choice of the Medicine Lodge reservation or the Sioux reservation, and the U.S. never fulfilled it (endnote 24). Hunting rights under Art. 2 are cited at Kappler 1012.
- **Executive Order of Nov 26, 1884 (Tongue River).** 371,200 acres (680 sq mi), south of the Yellowstone on the Tongue River and Rosebud Creek. Shiraw (1890) places settlements starting about 80 mi up the Tongue and 65 mi up the Rosebud from their mouths, running 20 mi upstream, with about 5 mi along Lame Deer and Muddy creeks (p. 326).
- **Executive Order of Mar 19, 1900.** Extended the reservation to the west bank of the Tongue River, for a total of 444,157 acres. Cheyenne east of the river were moved west (pp. 321-322).
- **Allotment.** Completed by 1932: 1,457 allottees at 160 acres each. Non-Indians bought and leased land inside the reservation (p. 330).
- **Coal leases, 1966-71.** 214,000 acres, half the reservation, were leased, and Congress cancelled the leases in 1980 (pp. 341-343).
- **Colorado claims in the recommendations.** The Front Range to the Continental Divide is claimed as "legally titled" to the Northern Cheyenne (Rec. 2, endnote 2). CSU still holds 19,000 acres of Northern Cheyenne land, and the state School Fund received 4 million acres (endnote 11).

## 4. Quotes worth preserving

- Dull Knife: "We are back on our own ground, and have stopped fighting. We have found the place we started to come to." (p. 306, via Grinnell 417)
- Dull Knife to Wessells: "Tell him if he tries to send us back, we will butcher each other with our own knives." (p. 308, via Bronson 1908)
- "Wessells immediately began attempting to 'change their minds' by cutting off their food and firewood, escalating on January 8 to cutting off their water." (p. 308)
- Lt. Cummings: "As there were no soldiers there, I was convinced that these civilians were killing wounded Indians." (p. 317)
- Bear Bow (1918): "they did not sell the Black Hills... They did not give the friendly band at the agency authority to make the treaty for them." (pp. 328-329)
- Seward to Dole, Aug 12, 1861: "it does not from the face of the paper that the amendment referred to has been agreed to by the Indian Tribes concerned, through their proper representatives" (p. 339)
- Sec. Krug, 1946, on the purpose of the ICC: "the claims which the Indians assert are in nearly all cases grossly exaggerated in size and in many cases wholly without merit." (p. 332)
- Ted Rising Sun: "I would rather be poor in my own country, with my people, with our way of life than be rich in a torn-up land where I am outnumbered 10 to one by strangers." (pp. 343-344)

## 5. Open questions and claims needing verification

- **"Fort Sill".** The source repeatedly says the Northern Cheyenne were held at Fort Sill. Historically they were held at the Darlington / Fort Reno agency. This is probably a source error.
- **Executive order date.** The p. 322 quote says 16 November 1884, while the narrative says 26 November 1884. The usual date is Nov 26.
- **1851 ratification claim.** Check this against Kappler's note and Royce 786-787. It is contested, not settled.
- **Hunting-rights consent clause.** Endnote 54 cites "Article 8" of the 1868 treaty for adult-majority consent. Confirm which treaty and article (the Sioux 3/4 clause is Art. 12).
- **Uncited items.** The 1864 Denver land act (no Stat. cite given), the 1926 Black Hills claim, the "Flute case" (endnote 69), and the 1980 coal-lease act (possibly Pub. L. 96-401) all need identification.
- **Mixed ICC figures.** The source blends Southern and Northern figures: the $23.5M valuation, the $15M Oklahoma compromise and the $4.36M Northern award. The Cheyenne-Arapaho of Oklahoma held a 50.61% interest (endnote 60). Reconcile these against 16 ICC 162/185 and 13 Ind. Cl. Comm. 1.
- **Fort Robinson casualties.** Figures vary: the Army counted 64 dead, Cummings counted 39 men and 22 women and children, and the first-night burial detail found about 50 against Wessells' 30. Cheyenne accounts run higher.
- **Signature dates.** The 1877 Cheyenne signature date (Sept 28, 1877) falls after removal south had begun. Confirm it against the original.
- **Stephen Douglas quote.** The 1858 Senate quote has no page citation.
- **Mineral figure.** The $15,307,000 mineral figure has no specific source.
