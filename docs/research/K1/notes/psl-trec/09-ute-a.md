## 1. Summary of the most important findings

1. **One band signed for many (1863).** PSL says only Ouray and nine other Tabeguache leaders signed the Conejos Treaty (13 Stat. 673). One Capote and three Mouache chiefs attended, the Weeminuche walked out, the Grand River, Yampa and Uintah sent no one, and Kaniache (Mouache) refused to come. Even so, the treaty ceded all land east of the Divide plus Middle Park and recited "Tabeguache" country reaching the 41st parallel (pp. 363, 406-408).
2. **Altered text claim (1863).** PSL says the San Luis Valley cession was written in without Ute agreement, that Agent Head confirmed the treaty "had been altered without the Utes' consent", and that the Conejos Agency stayed in the "ceded" valley for five more years (p. 408). This is asserted with no endnote visible here.
3. **Troops at the council.** About 500 Colorado soldiers under Col. John Chivington were present at Conejos in October 1863 (p. 408).
4. **Packed delegation.** Agent Head reportedly picked Washington delegates who had no authority among the Ute (p. 407). The source hedges this with "supposedly".
5. **The likely Abiquiu signer had been jailed.** Months before the 1849 Abiquiu treaty, Kaniache, probably the "Quixiachigiate" who signed as principal chief, was jailed without evidence, told he would be executed, and escaped. At the same time dragoons were attacking Ute camps (p. 400). The Southern Ute timeline instead names the signer as a Capote (p. 360).
6. **Starvation and captives before the 1855 treaties.** In 1855 the Fauntleroy campaign killed 40 at a camp near Poncha Pass and captured 50+ women and children, "probably" enslaved. The August 1855 Meriwether treaties were then signed by starving Mouache and Capote, and never ratified (pp. 404-405).
7. **The 1868 promise was broken almost at once.** Article 2 barred all unauthorized persons from the reservation forever. Miners entered the San Juans "at an alarming pace". The army planned to remove squatters, but the territorial governor objected and nothing was done. The Brunot cession of a 60 x 90 mile block followed "under pressure from miners" (pp. 369-370, 390, 411).
8. **Imposed head chief and personal salary.** Officials insisted on making Ouray principal chief of all Utes to ease cessions, though no single chief held that authority (pp. 407, 410). The Brunot Agreement paid Ouray $1,000 a year "so long as he shall remain Head Chief" (p. 361).
9. **Survey manipulation and an uncompensated taking.** Under his instructions, surveyor Miller ran the 1868 north line 15-18 miles too far south (p. 379). The 1875 executive-order addition was later "restored to the public domain" (1882) without compensation. *Confederated Bands* (1947) called the Utes "tenants at will" there, over a three-justice dissent (pp. 378-386).
10. **The 1880 removal was punishment.** The Supreme Court itself says Congress removed the Utes from Colorado as punishment for the Meeker events, including the Uncompahgre, who were not involved (pp. 370, 383). Schurz admitted the government could not "control the action of our Western people" (p. 391).

## 2. What the atlas most likely gets wrong or flattens

- **The 1863 Royce cession (Colorado) as a "Tabeguache cession".** Coloring it as ceded hides three things: (a) other bands that used the land did not sign, notably the Grand River and Yampa in Middle Park and North Park, and the Mouache and Capote in the San Luis Valley and Front Range; (b) the San Luis Valley inclusion is alleged to be an unauthorized alteration; (c) armed troops were present. Suggested flags: SIGNATORY_ILLEGITIMATE, EXCLUDED_PARTY, contested alteration.
- **Land occupied before cession.** Settlers were preparing to occupy Los Conejos lands the Utes had been forced off in 1851 (p. 401), and miners were in South Park in 1859 (p. 405). Both happened before the 1863 cession. The San Juans were trespassed after 1868 and before Brunot. A "ceded on date X" model hides that the taking came first.
- **Abiquiu 1849 is not a cession.** It is a jurisdiction and peace treaty, and the US governor later treated it as confirming Ute country (p. 407). If the atlas shades pre-1863 Ute land as Mexican-cession public domain, that repeats the federal premise reported by Jorjani (no aboriginal title under Mexican law, p. 372). It should not be the map default.
- **Unratified instruments.** The 1855 Capote and Mouache treaties and the 1865 Spanish Fork (Utah) treaty were never ratified. The Army enforced the 1865 treaty anyway (p. 369).
- **1868 boundary.** PSL's own narrative (p. 410) says "south of the 40th parallel". The treaty, as recited in the Court of Claims findings, puts the north line 15 miles north of the 40th parallel. Use the treaty line.
- **Band identity in the 1868 signer lists.** PSL's transcription puts Ou-ray under "Muaches" and Ka-ni-ache under "Tabaguaches", the reverse of their usual bands. Do not import band tags from this list without checking 15 Stat. 619.
- **Uncompahgre Reservation (Utah).** It is legally intact as Indian country (Ute III, 773 F.2d 1087) but almost entirely non-tribal in ownership, and "extinguished" in money terms by the ICC Docket 349 settlement of $300,000 (1965). It should not be drawn as simply "former reservation".

## 3. Land promised or reserved, with boundaries as given

**1863 recital of Tabeguache country** (13 Stat. 673, p. 363, verbatim summary): beginning on the 37th parallel at the eastern base of the Sierra Madre; north along the base of the Rocky Mountains to the 41st parallel; west on the 41st parallel to the summit of the Snowy Range northwest of North Park; south along the Snowy Range to the Rabbit Ear Mountains; along the Rabbit Ear range, west of Middle Park, to the Grand River; down the Grand to its confluence with the Gunnison; up the Gunnison to the mouth of the Uncompahgre; up the Uncompahgre to its source in the Snowy Range opposite the source of the Rio Grande; in a right line south to the summit of the Sierra La Plata range dividing the San Juan and Rio Grande waters; southeast along that summit to the 37th parallel; east on the 37th parallel to the beginning.

**1863 reserved Tabeguache land** (pp. 363-364): beginning at the mouth of the Uncompahgre; down the Gunnison to its confluence with the "Bunkara" river; up the Bunkara to the Roaring Fork; up the Roaring Fork to its source; along the Arkansas/Gunnison divide to the divide between the San Luis Valley and the Arkansas; along that summit to the source of Sandy Creek (San Luis Valley); down Sandy Creek to where it sinks at low water; in a right line to where the centre of the Rio Grande channel crosses 106 W; up the Rio Grande to 107 W; south along 107 W to the Rio Grande/San Juan divide; west along that summit to a point due south of the Uncompahgre source; to that source and down the Uncompahgre to its mouth. The treaty allowed non-Indian mining anywhere in this reserve (p. 408).
Note: "Bunkara" appears to be a period name for a Gunnison tributary; identification needs verification.

**1868 reservation** (15 Stat. 619, Art. 2): Article 2's full metes and bounds are not quoted in this chunk. What the chunk does give: the north line is "a line fifteen miles north of, and parallel to the 40th parallel" (Court of Claims finding, p. 378); the east line is the 107th meridian (p. 410, and the 1875 EO starts at the 1868 NE corner on 107 W); the area is roughly the western third of Colorado. Two agencies were planned, one at Los Pinos and one at White River. Further cessions required the consent of three-fourths of adult Ute men (p. 411).

**1875 executive-order addition** (EO Nov. 22, 1875, 1 Kappler 834, quoted pp. 395-396): "commencing at the northeast corner of the present Ute Indian Reservation, as defined in the treaty of March 2, 1868 ... thence running north on the 107th degree of longitude to the first standard parallel north; thence west on said first standard parallel to the boundary line between Colorado and Utah; thence south with said boundary to the northwest corner of the Ute Indian Reservation; thence east with the north boundary of the said reservation to the place of beginning." It was restored to the public domain by the 1882 EO. Murphy's dissent says the White River Valley lands lay about nine miles south of the first standard parallel north.

## 4. Quotes worth preserving

- "Only Ouray and nine other Tabeguache leaders ultimately signed the treaty." (p. 408)
- "Even Agent Head corroborated the fact that the treaty had been altered without the Utes' consent." (p. 408)
- "Any citizen of the United States may mine, without interference or molestation, in any part of the country hereby reserved to said Indians" (1863 treaty, quoted p. 408)
- "You want our land because there is plenty of gold there, but all that you are willing to give us is copper. Do you think that we are fools?" (unnamed Ute delegate, 1863, p. 407)
- "When Kaniache arrived in Abiquiú alone, he was taken captive and accused of having slain Williams and Kern without any evidence whatsoever." (p. 400)
- "No single chief held absolute power or made decisions for the entire tribe." (p. 407)
- "disgracefully worthless, rotten, and disgusting, and might reasonably have been made the grounds of revocation of the treaty." (Gov. Cummings, 1866, p. 410)
- "the territorial governor objected and government protection of Ute treaty lands never materialized." (Rust 2017, p. 370)
- "That order made the Indians no more than tenants at the will of the Government" (330 U.S. 169, p. 382)
- "Congress decided to remove the Indians from the Colorado reservation as part of the punishment meted out for this tragedy." (330 U.S. 169, p. 383)
- "Uncompahgre Indians have no title to any of the lands within the [Uncompahgre] reservation [and] nothing more than the privilege of temporary occupancy." (H. Rpt. 660, 1894, via Jorjani, p. 372)
- "the government will not always be able in all things to control the action of our Western people" (Schurz, 1880, p. 391)

## 5. Open questions and claims needing verification

- **1863 alteration claim:** find Lafayette Head's correspondence (the source is not visible here). Also check whether "altered" really refers to the Senate amendments (p. 372), and whether the Tabeguache assented to those amendments (Kappler II).
- **1863 signer bands:** "Colorado" and "Sa-wa-wat-se-wich" appear among the 1863 signers and again in 1868 as Yampa. Was the 1863 signer group entirely Tabeguache?
- **1868 signer list:** the Ouray/Kaniache band headings look swapped, and it is unclear which date goes with which group. Verify against 15 Stat. 619.
- **1868 Article 2:** get the exact boundary text from Kappler II 990.
- **Brunot Agreement:** the cite (18 Stat. 36), the three-fourths compliance, the trust amount (PSL's "$50,000 ... sufficient to produce $25,000" is garbled), and the Ouray's-son story all need the next chunk or Kappler.
- **Smallpox after the 1854 council:** speculative in the source itself. Do not map as fact.
- **Abiquiu principal signer:** Capote (Southern Ute site) or Mouache Kaniache (Stewart)?
- **Spanish and Mexican land grants over Ute land:** this chunk only gives the 1851 Los Conejos settlement note. Cross-check with the Conejos (1833/1842) and Sangre de Cristo (1843) grants from other sources.
- **"Questionable tactics" in 1868 (p. 410):** these are unattributed. Find the underlying sources.
