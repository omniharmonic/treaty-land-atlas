# PSL TREC 2024, Fraud Report ("Fraud in Indian Country"), PDF pp. 467-519

Benjamin Life (@omniharmonic). Research-assistant draft. Findings PSL-FRD-001 to 047 are in `11-fraud.csv`.

The chapter has no named author. It is a narrative history of Colorado from 1834 to 1868 with a section on the ICC. It cites mostly secondary works: Roberts' 1984 dissertation, Kelsey 1968, Hyde's George Bent letters, Grinnell, Jones on Medicine Lodge, Trenholm and Gard. Its primary sources are Evans's Indian Affairs correspondence (History Colorado), the Evans proclamations, S. Rep. 142 (1865) and S. Rep. 156 (1867), the RIT 364 negotiation file, the Boulder City Town Company records, and the ICC 1965 findings in Dockets 329-A/B. Many endnotes are "Ibid."-chained to Roberts 1984. Several strong claims have no endnote.

Scope note: the brief asked about state school-land grants. This chapter does not discuss them. The only land grants it covers are the Pacific Railway Act and the 1864 Denver townsite act.

## 1. Ten-line summary

1. Legal theory: the 1834 Trade and Intercourse Act (4 Stat. 729) made Colorado "Indian Country". The fur trade, the 1858-59 towns and the town-company surveys were therefore statutory trespass, and the federal government failed its duty to remove intruders (FRD-001).
2. Before any cession, Kansas Gov. James W. Denver organized Arapahoe County on 1851 Cheyenne-Arapaho treaty land. Denver, Boulder and Colorado City town companies sold lots with invalid titles: Boulder had 4,044 lots at $1,000 each, and Denver lots were claimed with "four-log houses" (FRD-004 to 008).
3. Congress made the defective Denver titles good with the Act of May 28, 1864, at $1.25/acre, before the area was ceded by the Little Arkansas treaty (1865). TREC says Gov. Evans bought land this way (FRD-006).
4. Fort Wise (1861): the Senate struck Art. XI, which would have sold land to Denver at $1.25/acre. The amendment was "allegedly" re-signed Oct 29, 1861, with uniform bold-cross X marks. Northern bands never signed, Article VI assent never happened, and the Council of 44 never approved (FRD-012 to 015).
5. Most map-relevant: in 1862-63, US Attorney S.E. Browne and Commissioner Dole concluded that Fort Wise ceded nothing north of the South Platte. That area includes Fort Collins, Greeley, Julesburg, Boulder and part of Denver. TREC says it is "still unceded" (FRD-016).
6. Agent Samuel Colley, Dole's cousin, passed annuities to his son Dexter and "Lying John" Smith to resell. Dexter reportedly made $25,000-35,000. Agent Loree withheld ammunition, and Upper Platte chiefs got annuities only after signing an adherence paper (FRD-017, 018, 024).
7. Gov. Evans had a conflict of interest: railroad ambitions (Pacific Railway Act, 6,400 acres per mile), refusal to accept Dole's boundary ruling, and an Aug 1864 proclamation letting citizens kill "hostile" Indians and keep their property (FRD-022, 031).
8. Sand Creek happened on the Fort Wise reserve (Royce 477), against people who were prisoners of war under the Camp Weld arrangement. Eleven months later the Little Arkansas treaty took the reserve, with about one sixth of the Cheyenne present (FRD-032, 034).
9. Medicine Lodge (1867): oral promises from Sen. Henderson's private talk with Buffalo Chief, including hunting rights between the Arkansas and South Platte, were left out of the text. The reserve included land in the Cherokee Outlet. Sand Creek captive children were still unreturned (FRD-039 to 041).
10. ICC Dockets 329-A/B (1965): 51,210,000 acres were valued at $23.5M as of Oct 14, 1865 (about $0.46/acre). There was no interest, and all further claims were barred. The government expert valued grazing land at $0.02/acre, against $1.25/acre federal sale prices (FRD-047).

## 2. What the atlas likely gets wrong or flattens

- **Royce 426 shown as one 1861 cession.** By TREC's account, Fort Wise was signed only by some Southern bands. Northern Cheyenne and Arapaho never signed. In 1863 the Commissioner himself read the cession as stopping at the South Platte. The atlas should at least split 426 at the South Platte and add a note. Before calling the northern part "never ceded", check the 1868 Northern Cheyenne-Arapaho Fort Laramie treaty (15 Stat. 655) and the 1876-77 agreement (Royce 597). TREC does not discuss whether either reached this land.
- **Royce 477 shown as "reserve".** The reserve was never surveyed properly (Wright's survey had to be redone), never furnished, occupied by troops, never lived on, and was the site of Sand Creek.
- **Front Range towns treated as post-cession.** Denver, Boulder and Colorado City predate any cession. Denver's title came from an 1864 act of Congress, not from tribal consent.
- **Little Arkansas 1865.** The atlas (TLA-T-341) lists it as ratified, 14 Stat. 703. TREC says it was "never ratified" and that the Cheyenne refused the amendments. This is probably a TREC error about ratification. The real point is likely that the Senate amendments were not accepted and the reservation was never set up. Model it as "ratified with amendments; amendments reportedly refused; promised land never delivered".
- **1851 Fort Laramie.** The Senate cut annuities from 50 years to 10. TREC says the signatures on the amendment do not match the original signers. It gives no cite for this.
- **The atlas has no "consent quality" dimension.** TREC proposes four tests: who chose the signers; oral terms left out of the text; pre-written, unappropriated money terms; and treaties as contracts of adhesion.

## 3. Land promised or reserved (mappable detail)

- **Fort Wise reserve, 1861 (Royce 477):** 40 acres per person in severalty, held compactly, with leftover land held in common, water rights held in common, and agency and school land. The chapter gives no metes-and-bounds; use the Royce 477 polygon.
- **North of the South Platte:** Dole's 1863 determination. The South Platte River in Colorado is the line. North of it is "not ceded by Fort Wise".
- **Cache la Poudre Arapaho reserve request, early 1864:** Friday's band asked for it in exchange for peace. It would have displaced 16 squatter families. No boundaries are given; map it as a point or label at Camp Collins.
- **Little Arkansas 1865, Art. III:** the right to live between the Arkansas and Platte rivers, except where whites occupied the land. Art. II gave a reservation in Indian Territory and Kansas on land the US did not hold title to. Allotments were 640 acres for mixed-blood relatives and 160 acres for Sand Creek survivors (Art. VI), never delivered.
- **Medicine Lodge 1867, oral terms:** hunting between the Arkansas and South Platte if the hunters stayed 10 miles from settlements; hunting in the Big Bend of the Arkansas and on the Smoky Hill range. These were never written into the treaty. Little Raven understood the reserve to include the land from Bent's Fort to the Rockies.
- **1859 Lew Cassil hunting line (KS/NE):** a line across the Solomon River near Pipe Creek in Cloud County, then to the Republican River, then to the mouth of the Big Sandy in Nebraska. Whites were told not to hunt west of it. This is outside Colorado.

## 5. Open questions and claims needing verification

- **Little Arkansas ratification status.** TREC says "never ratified"; the standard record says proclaimed in 1867 with amendments. Check NARA RIT 341 and the Senate Executive Journal.
- **Fort Wise amendment signatures (Oct 29, 1861).** TREC implies forgery from the style of the marks. This needs inspection of the original in the NARA RIT file.
- **The "federal courts" ruling on the South Platte.** No case is named. The chain is Roberts 1984, 149-153. Find Browne's letters and Dole's reply in the OIA Letters Received (Colorado Superintendency).
- **"Still unceded" north of the South Platte.** This needs reconciling with the 1868 Fort Laramie treaty (Northern Cheyenne and Arapaho), the 1876 agreement, and the ICC dockets. Northern Cheyenne and Arapaho claims went through separate ICC dockets, and this chapter does not cover them.
- **1851 amendment signatures.** TREC says they differ from the original signers; no source given.
- **ICC actual award.** TREC gives the $23.5M valuation but not the compromise payment (believed $15M after offsets; verify in the Docket 329-A/B final judgment).
- **Denver details.** The "Montana Land Company" as Denver's original name (likely a conflation of Montana City, St. Charles and Denver City town companies). The cite for the May 28, 1864 Denver act (Statutes at Large page not given), and the text gives May 28 while a map caption says "Act of March, 1864". Evans's land purchase has no specific cite.
- **Pacific Railway Act.** The act grants 200 feet on each side of the track, not a 200-foot right-of-way. It is also unclear whether Curtis was its primary author.
- **Single-source claims.** The Brill (1938) claim that the Senate struck a back-annuity article from Medicine Lodge is uncited, and TREC says so. The 30 children taken at Sand Creek comes from a single personal communication. The $30M cost of Sand Creek is unsourced.
- **Small errors in the chapter.** Dole is called "Interior Secretary" (he was Commissioner); Friday, an Arapaho, is listed among Cheyenne signers; the 1868 thirteen-signature claim is marked "[1868?]" by TREC itself. There are also editorial remnants ("[suggest: ...]") in the text, which suggests the chapter was not fully edited.
