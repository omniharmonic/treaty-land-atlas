# Verification pass: Ute and Shoshone claims (PSL TREC findings 03, 04, 09, 10)

Benjamin Life (@omniharmonic). Research-assistant draft, adversarial fact-check. 2026-10-20.

## How this check was done, and its limits

- Direct downloads were blocked in this session. govinfo, OSU Kappler (treaties.okstate.edu), loc.gov, Justia, CourtListener, archive.org and the tribal websites all returned proxy 403 or DNS failure. The only working tool was a web search that returns summaries of pages, sometimes with short quoted passages. So no Statutes at Large page was read directly in this pass.
- Where this note says "primary text", the wording comes from one of three places: (a) treaty wording quoted in the atlas's own overlays (TLA-T-328, TLA-T-367, TLA-A-1874), which were taken from the OSU Kappler pages; (b) Royce 1899 quotes in `crosswalks/royce-entry-roles.csv`; or (c) passages from opinions or treaty transcriptions that the search tool quoted. Anything resting only on the PSL book, or on one secondary source, is labeled that way.
- Verdict scale: CONFIRMED, PARTLY, NOT SUPPORTED, CONTRADICTED, UNRESOLVED.
- Before publishing, someone should open the govinfo PDFs for 13 Stat. 673, 15 Stat. 619, 18 Stat. 36, 21 Stat. 199, 18 Stat. 291 and 23 Stat. 22.

## Cite collision to fix in data

13 Stat. 673 is the 1863 Tabeguache (Conejos) treaty. 15 Stat. 673 is the 1868 Fort Bridger treaty with the Eastern Shoshone and Bannock. Same page number, different volumes. Check every row that cites "Stat. 673" for the right volume.

## 1. Conejos (Tabeguache) treaty, Oct. 7, 1863, 13 Stat. 673

| Sub-claim | Verdict | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Treaty made with the Tabeguache band only | CONFIRMED | Treaty text, as summarized from Kappler II p. 856 and govinfo: made with the "chiefs and warriors of the Tabeguache band". Atlas TLA-T-328 has the same reading. |
| Only Ouray and nine other Tabeguache signed | PARTLY | Transcriptions list signers including U-ray (Arrow), Un-cow-ra-gut (Red Color), Sha-wa-she-yet (Blue Flower), Colorado, No-va-ve-tu-quar-et, Sa-wa-wat-se-wich (Blue River). Two more names (A-ca-mu-che-ne, Mu-chu-chop) appear in some versions only. The count of ten comes from PSL p. 408 and was not checked against the scan. |
| Other bands absent (Weeminuche walked out, Kaniache's Mouache refused, northern bands sent no one) | PARTLY | The Colorado Encyclopedia ("Conejos Treaty") says only the Tabeguache attended "in any significant number" and a few members of other bands came. The specific walkout and refusal details are PSL p. 408 only (from Nicolay's report, per PSL). |
| Cession reached lands of other bands (east of the Divide, Middle Park, San Luis Valley) | CONFIRMED as a matter of text; the San Luis Valley alteration is UNRESOLVED | Art. 2: the band will "cede, convey, and relinquish all of their claims, right, title, and interest in and to any and all lands" outside the reserve. That clause covers all land outside the reserve, including the valley and Middle Park. The preamble describes Tabeguache country running north to the 41st parallel. The claim that Agent Head admitted the San Luis Valley cession was written in without Ute consent rests on PSL p. 408 alone, with no document cited. |
| Military presence | PARTLY | Search transcriptions list "J. M. Chivington" and Lt. Col. S. F. Tappan among the witnesses. The figure of 500 Colorado soldiers is PSL p. 408 only. |
| Senate amendments | CONFIRMED | Ratified with amendments Mar. 25, 1864, assented Oct. 8, 1864, proclaimed Dec. 14, 1864. Under the amendment, the US admits no greater title than existed under Mexico (atlas TLA-T-328). |

Suggested wording: "Signed at Conejos by Tabeguache leaders only. The Weeminuche, Capote, most Mouache and the northern bands did not sign. The treaty still had the Tabeguache 'relinquish' all lands outside their reserve, including country other bands also used: Middle Park, the Front Range, the San Luis Valley. The other bands' claims were not given up by any treaty until Article 3 of the 1868 treaty. Colonel Chivington signed as a witness. PSL reports that 500 soldiers were present."

Data note: do not color Mouache, Capote or Weeminuche country as "ceded 1863". Their relinquishment instrument is 1868.

## 2. Abiquiu treaty, Dec. 30, 1849, 9 Stat. 984

| Sub-claim | Verdict | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Treaty cedes no land; Utes "unconditionally submit" to US jurisdiction | CONFIRMED | Treaty Art. 1, quoted in atlas TLA-T-256 and PSL p. 400. |
| A signer was jailed and threatened beforehand | PARTLY / CONTESTED | Southern Ute chronology: Quiziachigiate, a Capote, signed as principal chief, with 28 subordinate chiefs. PSL pp. 400 and 360, citing Omer Stewart, says "Quixiachigiate" was probably Kaniache (Mouache). A local history piece agrees that Kaniache was arrested in 1849 over the Williams and Kern killings, threatened with death, and escaped. If Stewart's identification is right, the principal signer had been jailed months before signing. If it is wrong, the jailing story concerns a Ute leader who may not have signed. The visit at Capt. Chapman's request and the three-day detention are PSL only. |

Suggested wording: "Months before the treaty, US authorities at Abiquiu jailed the Mouache leader Kaniache without evidence over two killings, and he escaped. Anthropologist Omer Stewart identified the treaty's principal signer 'Quixiachigiate' as Kaniache. The Southern Ute Tribe's chronology names him Quiziachigiate, a Capote. Contested."

## 3. Ute treaty, Mar. 2, 1868, 15 Stat. 619

| Sub-claim | Verdict | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Art. 2 boundary | CONFIRMED (with a correction to PSL p. 410) | Art. 2 runs from where the 107th meridian crosses Colorado's southern line, north along the meridian to a point fifteen miles north of the 40th parallel, west to the territorial line, then south and east. Confederated Bands, 330 U.S. 169, recites the northern line as "fifteen miles north of, and parallel to the 40th parallel". PSL's shorthand "south of the 40th parallel" is wrong. |
| Exclusion clause | CONFIRMED | Art. 2: "no persons, except those herein authorized so to do ... shall ever be permitted to pass over, settle upon, or reside in the Territory described in this article" (atlas TLA-T-367, PSL p. 390). The three-fourths adult-male consent rule is Art. 16 (atlas), not Art. 2. |
| Bands party to it | CONFIRMED | Tabeguache, Muache, Capote, Weeminuche, Yampa, Grand River and Uintah. Art. 1 extended the 1863 terms to all of them. Senate-amendment assents were taken band by band, Aug. 15 to Sept. 25, 1868 (PSL pp. 365-368, quoting the assent pages). |
| Miners' trespass before Brunot | CONFIRMED | The Southern Ute and Ute Mountain Ute amicus brief in Herrera v. Wyoming (No. 17-532, 2018) refers to "the unlawful influx of miners into a portion of Ute treaty lands upon discovery of hard rock minerals in the San Juan Mountains." The 1874 ratifying act recites that the 1872 negotiation failed "owing to the refusal of said Indians to relinquish their right to any portion of said reservation." Royce row 566 (1872-04-23) records the authorizing act. |

Suggested wording: "The 1868 treaty promised that no unauthorized person would 'ever be permitted to pass over, settle upon, or reside in' the reservation. Miners entered the San Juans anyway from about 1870. The US did not remove them, then sought a cession. The Utes refused it in 1872."

## 4. Brunot Agreement, Sept. 13, 1873, ratified by Act of Apr. 29, 1874, 18 Stat. 36 (Royce 566)

| Sub-claim | Verdict | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Ouray's captive son used as leverage | CONFIRMED in substance; captor details CONTESTED | The Colorado Encyclopedia ("Ouray", "Brunot Agreement") says Brunot learned that Ouray's only son had been captured and traded to the Arapaho, and promised to return him. Accounts differ on who took the boy: Lakota then traded to Arapaho, or an Arapaho band, or a Sioux attack. The young man later produced was not accepted as the son (PSL pp. 414-418, Colorado Encyclopedia). |
| Ouray's $1,000 salary | CONFIRMED | The agreement text as printed in the ratifying act: "Ouray, Head Chief of the Ute nation shall receive a salary of one thousand dollars per annum for the term of ten years or so long as he shall remain Head Chief" (PSL p. 361). PSL calls this Art. 6; check the article number in 18 Stat. 36. Brunot's report says "No presents were given ... and no promises were made other than those mentioned in this report" (H.R. Ex. Doc. No. 1, 43d Cong., 1st Sess., per PSL p. 417). |
| Utes understood they sold mines only and kept hunting | PARTLY | Hunting is in the text, Art. 2: "permit the Ute Indians to hunt upon said lands so long as the game lasts and the Indians are at peace with the white people." The "mines and mountains only" understanding rests on Ouray's Sept. 13 letter as quoted by PSL p. 416 ("We are perfectly willing to sell our mountainland ..."). A March 1881 delegation also told Secretary Kirkwood they had "only sold the mountains and mineral lands" (University of Utah Ute collection). That is Ute testimony, not treaty text. |
| Uncompahgre Park reserved, then taken | CONFIRMED in outline | The agreement carves the park out of the cession and keeps it as reservation (Brunot's report: "The Uncompagre Park ... reserved to the Indians", PSL p. 416). Royce 566 remark (p. 886): Executive Order of Aug. 17, 1876 "declares a tract in Uncompahgre valley to be a portion of Ute reservation, in accordance with agreement ratified Apr. 29, 1874." The UCSB Presidency Project copy of that order describes a four-mile square. |
| Taken by Act of May 14, 1884 | PARTLY | An Access Genealogy list of Colorado reservation authorities cites "May 14, 1884 (XXIII, 22)" among the Ute statutes. That supports the link, but the text of 23 Stat. 22 was not seen. PSL p. 456 is the only source that says what the act did. |
| Royce 566 | CONFIRMED | Royce: "Agreement ... for cession of part of reserve established by treaty of Mar. 2, 1868" (p. 864), ratified Apr. 29, 1874 (p. 874). About 3.7 million acres (Herrera amicus, Colorado Encyclopedia). |

Suggested wording: "In 1873 Brunot got the San Juan cession (Royce 566) after promising Ouray to recover his captive son, a promise he did not keep. The agreement also paid Ouray $1,000 a year for ten years, which Brunot did not list as an inducement. Ute leaders said they were selling mountain and mining land, not valleys. The text kept their hunting right and reserved Uncompahgre Park. A survey put the park inside the cession. An 1876 executive order restored it, and Congress returned it to the public domain in 1884 (verify 23 Stat. 22)."

## 5. Ute agreement of Mar. 6, 1880, Act of June 15, 1880, 21 Stat. 199 (Royce 616/617)

| Sub-claim | Verdict | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Three-fourths consent requirement | CONFIRMED | The act made the agreement effective on ratification by three-fourths of adult male Utes (Telluride Magazine; PSL p. 437). This tracks 1868 Art. 16. Royce p. 902 (1880-09-11): "Sufficient signatures obtained to complete ratification." |
| Mears paid $2 per signature | CONFIRMED as reported by multiple secondary sources | Telluride Magazine says Mears "personally offered $2 to any Ute male willing to sign." The Denver Public Library blog says "Mears resorted to bribery." PSL p. 437 cites Conetah. No primary record was seen. |
| Mears later reimbursed (by Sec. Kirkwood) | NOT SUPPORTED beyond one source | Only PSL p. 437, citing Fred Conetah's History of the Northern Ute People. No other source found. Label it single-source. |
| Ouray's signature | PARTLY | Ouray headed the Washington delegation that signed on Mar. 6, 1880. He died Aug. 24, 1880 at the Southern Ute agency, before ratification was complete. That he never signed the ratification is from Conetah via PSL. |
| 1881 forced removal of the Uncompahgre to Utah | CONFIRMED | Coloradonewsline (2026) and the San Miguel County apology resolution (2014) describe a military removal. Removal dates are given as Aug. 28 or Sept. 7, 1881. Col. Ranald Mackenzie's 24-hour ultimatum is PSL p. 439. The Uncompahgre took no part at Milk Creek (PSL p. 435). |
| Royce 616/617 | PARTLY (assignment uncertain) | Royce p. 849: "Nos. 566, 616, and 617 compose this reservation." The atlas's own note says which of 616 and 617 is the ceded tract and which is the Southern Ute strip "could be reversed." Fix this before tagging either number. |

Suggested wording: "Congress required the consent of three-fourths of adult Ute men. After Ouray's death, commissioner Otto Mears paid Ute men $2 each for signatures. Fred Conetah (Northern Ute) writes that Mears was later reimbursed (single source). In 1881 the Army removed the Uncompahgre, who had no part in the Milk Creek fight, to Utah."

## 6. Ute claims: ICC Dockets 327, 328, 349; 330 U.S. 169; 402 U.S. 159; Hunter Act

| Sub-claim | Verdict | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Docket 327 (Royce 566): $8,500,000 value, $507,662.84 offset, Ute experts at $40.8M | UNRESOLVED | No source outside PSL p. 455. 25 U.S.C. sec. 676 and following confirm a final judgment in Docket 327 and its split: 60% Uintah and Ouray Tribe with the Ute Distribution Corp., 20% Ute Mountain, 20% Southern Ute. |
| Docket 328 (Southern Ute, Hunter Act lands) | CONFIRMED on substance; docket number not independently seen | United States v. Southern Ute Tribe, 423 F.2d 346 (Ct. Cl. 1970). The Commission found a taking of 230,547.44 acres given out as free homesteads 1899-1938. The Court of Claims affirmed liability but not the acreage figure. |
| Docket 349 (Uncompahgre), $300,000 | CONFIRMED (single primary-type source) | Interior Solicitor memo M-37051 (2018): stipulated settlement, $300,000, findings Feb. 18, 1965, bars all claims "asserted or could have asserted." Funds appropriated Act of Apr. 30, 1965, 79 Stat. 81. Cite discrepancy: PSL row gives 12 ICC 707; M-37051 as summarized gives 14 ICC 707. Check the volume. |
| 330 U.S. 169 (1947) | CONFIRMED | Black, J.: the 1875 order "made the Indians no more than tenants at the will of the Government on that part of the land outside the true treaty reservation," so no compensation was owed when the 1882 order restored it. |
| 402 U.S. 159 (1971) res judicata | CONFIRMED | The Southern Ute claims "are barred by res judicata" because of the 1950 Court of Claims consent judgment (117 Ct. Cl. 433, No. 46640) covering lands "ceded ... by the Act of June 15, 1880." The Southern Ute chronology gives the 1950 judgment as $31,761,206. |
| Hunter Act, Feb. 20, 1895, 28 Stat. 677 | CONFIRMED in substance | 402 U.S. 159 and 423 F.2d 346 describe the 1895 Act as confirming the 1880 Act and directing Southern Ute allotment. The Court held that sec. 5 contained no Southern Ute cession. |

Suggested wording: "The ICC paid money and extinguished claims. It did not return land. For the Hunter Act homestead lands, the Supreme Court in 1971 barred even the money claim, holding it settled by a 1950 consent judgment. For the Uncompahgre, the 1880 promise of a Colorado reservation ended in a $300,000 settlement in 1965."

## 7. Fort Laramie 1851; Fort Bridger 1863 and 1868; Royce 520

| Sub-claim | Verdict | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Shoshone excluded from Fort Laramie 1851, "not parties" | CONFIRMED in substance; exact quote UNRESOLVED | The treaty's preamble names eight nations: Sioux, Cheyenne, Arapaho, Crow, Assiniboine, Gros Ventre, Mandan and Arikara. Shoshone are not among them. WyoHistory ("Horse Creek Treaty") says the Shoshone came in force but "had not been invited" and were not asked to sign, because their affairs ran through the Salt Lake office. The PSL quote ("not considered by the Superintendent ... not parties to the treaty", p. 98) was not found online. It is probably from the 1851 superintendent's report. One search summary claimed Shoshone leaders signed. That is wrong, and the treaty text contradicts it. |
| Fort Bridger 1863 (18 Stat. 685) Art. 4 is a recognition of boundaries, not a cession | CONFIRMED | Art. 4 records "the Shoshonee country, as defined and described by them": "on the east, by the Wind River mountains, Peenahpah river, the north fork of Platte or Koo-chin-agah, and the north Park or Buffalo House; and on the south, by Yampah river and the Uintah mountains", with the west "left undefined" (Access Genealogy and dheller.org transcriptions of 18 Stat.). The treaty has no cession clause. Royce has no cession entry dated 1863 for this country, and area 520 is dated 1868 only. Ratified Mar. 7, 1864, proclaimed June 7, 1869, which is why it was printed in vol. 18. |
| Boundaries reaching Colorado (Yampa, North Park) | CONFIRMED | North Park and the Yampa are in Colorado. How far the Colorado portion extends is disputed among the Doty, Stewart and Robertson maps (PSL pp. 111-112, 143-144). |
| Fort Bridger 1868 (15 Stat. 673) cession | CONFIRMED | Art. 2 relinquished all other territory for the Wind River reservation. United States v. Shoshone Tribe, 304 U.S. 111 (1938), as quoted by PSL, gives 44,672,000 acres recognized in 1863 and 3,054,182 acres reserved in 1868 (the figures were not seen directly). |
| Royce 520 | CONFIRMED | Royce p. 850, July 3, 1868: "Relinquish all right to other territory". Atlas status: ceded, maps WY, CO, UT, ID. DigiTreaties cession 520 matches. |

Suggested wording: "The Shoshone came to the 1851 council but were not invited and are not a party to it. In 1863 the US recorded Shoshone country as the Shoshone described it, reaching into Colorado to the Yampa and North Park. That treaty took no land. The cession came in 1868 (Royce 520)."

## 8. Bear River Massacre

| Sub-claim | Verdict | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Date Jan. 29, 1863; Northwestern Shoshone; Col. P. E. Connor, California Volunteers | CONFIRMED | BYU RSC "Bear River Massacre: New Historical Evidence"; KUER 2019. |
| Death toll | CONFIRMED as a range | Connor's report: 224 or more. NPS documents give 250, 240-300 and 384. Settler and NPS-cited estimates run to about 400-500. Hans Jasperson counted 493. The tribe's estimates are cited as 350 or 400. |
| Relation to Box Elder treaty | CONFIRMED | Box Elder treaty, July 30, 1863 (13 Stat. 663), with the Northwestern bands six months later. It describes the bands as reduced "by the war" to destitution (atlas TLA-T-325). In Shoshone Indians v. United States, 324 U.S. 335 (1945), the Court held the treaty did not recognize Indian title. The causal link to the Fort Bridger 1863 signing is the opinion of Darren Parry (Northwestern Band) via PSL p. 105. Label it an Indigenous account. |

Suggested wording: "On Jan. 29, 1863, US troops under Col. Patrick Connor killed an estimated 250 to 500 Northwestern Shoshone men, women and children at Bear River. Six months later the survivors signed the Box Elder treaty."

## 9. Lander/Brunot cession, Sept. 26, 1872, Act of Dec. 15, 1874, 18 Stat. 291

| Sub-claim | Verdict | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Miners and settlers already in the ceded part | CONFIRMED in substance | Jackson Hole History Center copy of the 1874 ratification, and Iverson: miners rushed to the South Pass area from the 1860s, and the government did little to keep settlers out. The cession took the gold-bearing south of the reservation. |
| Size | CONFIRMED | Shoshone Tribe v. United States, 299 U.S. 476 (1937): the reservation was "reduced to 2,343,000 acres by the cession of 700,642 acres in 1874 for a money consideration." |
| ICC Docket 63 (Aug. 20, 1954): $27,500 against a value of $560,513.60, "unconscionable" | PARTLY | Not found outside PSL p. 126-127 and endnote 91. It is consistent with other sources: 700,642 acres x $0.80 = $560,513.60 exactly. $27,500 matches $25,000 in stock cattle plus, as I recall the act, $500 a year for five years to Washakie (check 18 Stat. 291). |

Suggested wording: "After miners had already moved in, the Shoshone ceded about 700,642 acres in the south of the 1868 reservation (agreement 1872, ratified 1874). In 1954 the Indian Claims Commission found the $27,500 paid 'unconscionable' against a value of $560,513.60."

## 10. Arapaho at Wind River and the Shoshone cases

| Sub-claim | Verdict | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Arapaho placed in 1878 without Shoshone consent | CONFIRMED | 299 U.S. 476: on March 18 (304 U.S. 111 says March 19), 1878, a band of Arapahos "under military escort" was settled on the reservation, over Shoshone protest. The 1868 treaty guaranteed "absolute and undisturbed use and occupation." |
| 299 U.S. 476 (1937) | CONFIRMED | Cardozo, J. The settlement of the Arapaho was a taking of an undivided half interest. Value is fixed as of 1878, not the 1927 act or 1891, with an added amount as part of just compensation. |
| 304 U.S. 111 (1938) | CONFIRMED | Butler, J. Timber and minerals were part of the land for valuation. The Court of Claims award for the half interest was $1,581,889.50 (Ct. Cl. No. H-219, per PSL). |
| 1939 Act | CONFIRMED | Act of July 27, 1939, Pub. L. 76-238, 53 Stat. 1128: tribal roll, per capita payments, and land purchase, loan fund and enterprises from the judgment fund. The $1,000,000 land-purchase sum appears in 25 U.S.C. sec. 576 and following (snippet only). The $4.2 million net and $2,450 per member figures are PSL p. 136 only. |
| Wyoming v. EPA, 875 F.3d 505 (10th Cir. 2017) | CONFIRMED | Tymkovich, C.J., with Kelly and Lucero: the 1905 Act (33 Stat. 1016) diminished the reservation. Cert. denied, 138 S. Ct. 2677 (June 25, 2018). Reported as a split decision. That Judge Lucero dissented is PSL p. 134; I believe it is correct but did not see it confirmed. |

Suggested wording: "In 1878 the Army brought the Northern Arapaho onto the Shoshone reservation without Shoshone consent. In 1937-38 the Supreme Court held this a taking of half the reservation and awarded money, including the value of timber and minerals. No land was returned. In 2017 the Tenth Circuit held that a 1905 act cut Riverton and the land north of the Wind River out of the reservation."
